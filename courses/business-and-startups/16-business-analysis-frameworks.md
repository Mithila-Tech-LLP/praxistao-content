## 1. Why Frameworks Exist (Structured Thinking, Not Crutches)

Think about the word "diagnosis." When you go to a doctor and say "I feel terrible," a good doctor does not just randomly guess what is wrong. They follow a structured process: they ask about symptoms, check your temperature, listen to your heart, run tests. They use frameworks — a systematic way of thinking through a complex problem so they do not miss anything obvious.

Business frameworks work exactly the same way. A business problem is almost always complex, with many moving parts, many people, many variables. A framework is a checklist for your brain. It makes sure you think about the right things, in the right order, without missing something important.

Here is the key distinction that most people miss: a framework is a thinking tool, not an answer machine. You cannot plug a company into SWOT and have it tell you what to do. You have to do the thinking. The framework just makes sure your thinking is complete and structured.

The consultants at McKinsey, BCG, and Bain use these frameworks every day — not because they are magic, but because they prevent you from being seduced by one brilliant-sounding idea while ignoring five important problems. They force you to look at the full picture.

There is also a danger with frameworks that you need to know upfront: over-reliance. Some people get so attached to frameworks that they cannot make a decision without one. They spend three weeks doing a perfect SWOT analysis and miss the market window entirely. The goal is structured thinking that leads to fast, confident decisions — not analysis paralysis dressed up in a nice table.

With that caveat clearly stated, let us look at the most useful frameworks in business.

## 2. SWOT Analysis — The One Everyone Uses Wrong

SWOT stands for Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats. It is probably the most famous business framework in the world. It is also the one that gets done badly most often.

### What It Is

SWOT organizes your thinking into four categories:

| | Internal (within the business) | External (outside the business) |
|---|---|---|
| **Positive** | Strengths | Opportunities |
| **Negative** | Weaknesses | Threats |

- **Strengths** are what your company does well — things you control.
- **Weaknesses** are where your company falls short — things you control but have not fixed.
- **Opportunities** are favorable things happening in the world outside — things you do not control but can take advantage of.
- **Threats** are unfavorable things happening in the world outside — things you do not control but need to defend against.

### How Most People Do It Wrong

Most people treat SWOT as a brainstorming exercise. They write down everything they can think of, put it in four boxes, look at it, say "good analysis!" and then do nothing with it.

That is not analysis. That is a list.

The real power of SWOT comes from the step most people skip: **matching Strengths to Opportunities and using that to decide what to do next.**

A proper SWOT leads to strategy. Specifically, you ask four questions after filling out the grid:

1. **SO (Strength + Opportunity):** How can we use our strengths to capture this opportunity?
2. **WO (Weakness + Opportunity):** How can we fix this weakness so we can take advantage of this opportunity?
3. **ST (Strength + Threat):** How can we use our strengths to defend against this threat?
4. **WT (Weakness + Threat):** What happens if this weakness gets hit by this threat? How do we prevent that?

These four questions turn SWOT from a list into an action plan.

### Real Example: Ola Electric SWOT (2024)

Let us do a real SWOT for Ola Electric, India's largest electric scooter maker.

**Strengths:**
- Largest EV manufacturing plant in the world (Futurefactory in Tamil Nadu) — massive production capacity
- Strong brand recognition in India, especially among younger buyers
- Vertical integration: they make their own batteries, scooters, and are building their own cells
- Strong founder (Bhavish Aggarwal) with high media presence and investor trust
- Software-first approach: scooters get over-the-air updates like a phone

**Weaknesses:**
- Very poor after-sales service reputation — service centers are inadequate, wait times are long
- Quality control issues — early models had software glitches, brake problems
- Heavily dependent on government subsidies (FAME scheme) — revenue model is fragile without subsidies
- Limited experience in hardware manufacturing at scale
- Customer complaints have gone viral multiple times, damaging trust

**Opportunities:**
- India is the world's largest two-wheeler market — 20+ million units sold per year
- Government is strongly pushing EV adoption with subsidies and reduced GST
- Rising petrol prices make EVs more attractive every year
- Battery costs globally are falling — making EVs cheaper to build
- Export opportunity: can sell to Southeast Asia, Latin America

**Threats:**
- Bajaj (Chetak), TVS (iQube), and Hero (Vida) are all releasing competing EVs with much better service networks
- Tata and Mahindra entering the electric two-wheeler space with deep resources
- Chinese manufacturers like Yadea and Ola's own supplier Longjia might enter India directly
- If government removes subsidies, prices jump significantly and demand could fall
- Any major safety incident (fire, brake failure) could trigger regulatory action

**Now the matching:**

- **SO move:** Use their Futurefactory capacity + falling battery costs (opportunity) to aggressively cut prices and grab market share before competitors catch up.
- **WO move:** Fix service centers urgently before Bajaj and TVS use that weakness to poach customers.
- **ST move:** Use their software advantage to create features competitors cannot easily copy — like connected vehicle features, health monitoring — to defend against the hardware-focused competition.
- **WT move:** Their weak service + strong competition is a dangerous combination. If they do not fix service, TVS and Bajaj will have a field day in 2025-26.

That is a real SWOT. Not a list — a strategic conversation.

## 3. PESTLE Analysis — Reading the Environment Before You Enter It

If SWOT is about analyzing your business, PESTLE is about analyzing the world your business lives in. It is especially useful when you are thinking about entering a new market, launching a new product, or trying to understand why an industry is changing.

PESTLE stands for:

| Letter | Factor | What It Means |
|---|---|---|
| **P** | Political | Government policies, regulations, political stability |
| **E** | Economic | GDP growth, inflation, interest rates, consumer spending power |
| **S** | Social | Demographics, cultural values, lifestyle changes, education levels |
| **T** | Technological | New technologies, automation, internet penetration, innovation |
| **L** | Legal | Laws, compliance requirements, labor laws, IP protection |
| **E** | Environmental | Climate change, sustainability regulations, weather risks |

### How to Use PESTLE for Entering a New Market

Imagine you run a startup like Zepto — you have conquered quick commerce in India's metros and you are now asking: should we expand to Southeast Asia? Specifically, should we launch in Indonesia?

Let us walk through a PESTLE for Zepto entering Indonesia:

**Political:** Indonesia has a relatively stable democracy but is known for protectionist policies. Foreign companies often need local partners or face restrictions on foreign ownership (maximum 49% foreign stake in retail businesses). There are also regulations around food import and handling. Risk is moderate-high.

**Economic:** Indonesia has a GDP of around $1.3 trillion (2024), with a growing middle class of 52 million people. But income inequality is high — the top cities like Jakarta have spending power close to Indian metros, but rural areas are much poorer. Inflation is around 3-4%. Consumer spending on food and groceries is growing at 8% per year. Opportunity is significant.

**Social:** Indonesians are heavy smartphone users — over 190 million internet users in a population of 275 million. Food culture is extremely important: eating fresh food, preferably local varieties, is central to daily life. Trust in Indian-brand companies might be low initially. Gen Z in Jakarta shops online heavily. The social dynamics favor quick commerce in urban centers.

**Technological:** 4G coverage is strong in Java (the main island) but patchy elsewhere. Digital payments are growing fast — GoPay, OVO, and Dana are the dominant wallets. There is an existing quick commerce player called GoMart (from Gojek). Last-mile infrastructure is less developed than India.

**Legal:** Labor laws in Indonesia require certain protections for gig workers — this affects the delivery partner model. Tax laws for foreign companies are complex. Food safety regulations exist and must be followed carefully. Compliance costs will be significant.

**Environmental:** Indonesia is in a tropical climate — food storage and transport need cooling infrastructure. Traffic congestion in Jakarta is severe and adds delivery complexity. There are also growing consumer movements around plastic packaging waste.

**Verdict from PESTLE:** Indonesia is a big opportunity but has real structural barriers — ownership restrictions, infrastructure gaps, and strong local competition (Gojek has first-mover advantage). A Zepto entry would require a local joint venture partner, heavy infrastructure investment, and starting with just Jakarta and Surabaya before expanding.

That is what PESTLE gives you: a structured view of the environment so you do not enter a market blind.

## 4. BCG Matrix — Managing a Portfolio of Products or Businesses

The BCG Matrix was created by the Boston Consulting Group in the 1970s. It is designed for companies that have multiple products or business units and need to decide where to invest money and where to cut.

The matrix plots products on two axes:

- **Market Growth Rate** (vertical axis): Is this market growing fast or slowly?
- **Relative Market Share** (horizontal axis): Does this product dominate its market, or is it a small player?

This gives you four quadrants:

```
                    HIGH market share    LOW market share
HIGH growth rate    ⭐ STARS             ❓ QUESTION MARKS
LOW growth rate     🐄 CASH COWS        🐕 DOGS
```

**Stars** — High growth, high share. These are your winners. They need investment to maintain their lead, but they are generating good returns. Your goal is to turn Stars into Cash Cows as the market matures.

**Cash Cows** — Low growth, high share. The market has matured but you dominate it. These products generate lots of cash without needing much investment. The cash from Cash Cows funds your Stars and Question Marks.

**Question Marks** — High growth, low share. The market is exciting but you are not winning yet. These need a decision: invest heavily to turn them into Stars, or exit before they drain resources.

**Dogs** — Low growth, low share. You are a small player in a stagnant market. Most of the time, the right move is to cut these loose.

### Tata Group's Portfolio Through the BCG Lens

Tata Group is one of India's largest conglomerates, with over 30 companies. Let us look at some of their businesses through BCG:

| Business | Market Growth | Tata's Position | BCG Category |
|---|---|---|---|
| TCS (IT services) | Moderate (8-10% per year) | #1 in India, top 3 globally | Cash Cow |
| Tata Motors EVs (Nexon EV, etc.) | Very high (40%+ per year in India) | Market leader in India | Star |
| Jaguar Land Rover | Low/stagnant (luxury cars slow post-COVID) | Strong brand but struggling | Question Mark (trending toward Dog) |
| Tata Steel | Low (steel is a mature commodity) | Major player but not dominant | Dog/Cash Cow (depends on region) |
| Tata Neu (super app) | High (fintech/e-commerce growing) | Very small market share currently | Question Mark |
| Indian Hotels (Taj) | Recovering, moderate growth | Premium leader in India | Cash Cow |

**What BCG tells Tata's leadership:**

- TCS is the engine that funds everything else. Do not mess with it.
- Tata Motors EVs deserves heavy investment — this is the Star that could become the next Cash Cow.
- JLR needs a hard decision: can it reinvent for the EV era, or should Tata reduce exposure?
- Tata Neu is bleeding cash as a Question Mark. The board needs to decide by 2025-26 whether to go all-in or fold it.

This is exactly how Tata's leadership thinks about capital allocation across their empire.

## 5. Ansoff Matrix — Four Ways to Grow

The Ansoff Matrix answers a deceptively simple question: *Where does our growth come from?*

Created by Igor Ansoff in 1957, it maps growth strategies on two dimensions: whether you are selling **existing or new products**, and whether you are selling to **existing or new markets**.

```
                    Existing Products    New Products
Existing Markets    Market Penetration   Product Development
New Markets         Market Development   Diversification
```

**Market Penetration** — Sell more of the same product to the same customers. Lowest risk. Tactics: discounts, better marketing, loyalty programs, beating competitors on price. Example: Swiggy offering Swiggy One subscription to get existing users to order more often.

**Product Development** — Create new products for your existing customers. Medium risk. Tactics: R&D, product line extensions, features, bundles. Example: Apple selling AirPods and Apple Watch to existing iPhone users. They already had the customer — they just gave them more to buy.

**Market Development** — Take your existing product into a new market. Medium risk. Tactics: geographic expansion, new customer segments, new channels. Example: Netflix expanding from the US to India in 2016. Same product (streaming), new market.

**Diversification** — New product for a new market. Highest risk. Two types:
- *Related diversification:* building on existing capabilities (Amazon building AWS — new product for new customers, but uses existing tech infrastructure)
- *Unrelated diversification:* completely new territory (Reliance going from oil to telecom to retail)

### Real Example: Flipkart's Growth Story Through Ansoff

Flipkart started selling books online to Indian internet users (2007). Let us trace their growth:

1. **Market Penetration (2007-2010):** Sell more books to more Indian internet users. Aggressive discounts, free shipping, cash on delivery.

2. **Product Development (2010-2012):** Launch electronics, apparel, and lifestyle products on the same platform to existing customers. This was the shift from "bookstore" to "everything store."

3. **Market Development (2013-2015):** Go from metro cities to Tier 2 and Tier 3 cities. Same products, same platform, new geography. They built logistics into smaller towns.

4. **Diversification (2016-2019):** Launch Myntra (fashion), PhonePe (payments), and Flipkart Health+ — entirely new product categories. Some related (payments uses existing customer trust), some less related.

By mapping their history on Ansoff, you can see exactly how Flipkart thought about growth at each stage — and where the risk level increased as they moved away from their original core.

## 6. OKRs — Objectives and Key Results

OKRs are how the world's best companies — Google, Intel, Spotify, LinkedIn, Swiggy — set and track goals. The system was invented at Intel by Andy Grove in the 1970s, then brought to Google by venture capitalist John Doerr in 1999 when Google was just 40 people. Google has used OKRs ever since.

The premise is simple: most companies set goals at the beginning of the year and forget about them by February. OKRs are designed to prevent that.

### The Structure

An OKR has two parts:

```
OBJECTIVE: What do we want to achieve? (qualitative, inspiring)
KEY RESULTS: How will we know if we achieved it? (quantitative, measurable)
```

An Objective is a clear, inspiring goal written in plain language. Not "improve our product" — that is vague and unmeasurable. A good Objective sounds like: "Become the most loved food delivery app in India."

Key Results are 3-5 specific, measurable outcomes that prove you hit the Objective. They are not tasks or to-do items — they are outcomes. The difference is critical.

- **Task:** "Send 10 new customer satisfaction surveys" — this is an action, not an outcome
- **Key Result:** "Increase NPS score from 42 to 60 by December" — this is an outcome

### What Good OKRs Look Like

Here is a real-world example of OKRs for a product team at a company like Zepto:

---

**Objective:** Make checkout so fast and delightful that customers never think about switching to a competitor.

**Key Results:**
1. Reduce average checkout time from 4.2 minutes to under 2.5 minutes
2. Increase checkout completion rate from 71% to 85%
3. Reduce payment failure rate from 6.2% to under 2%
4. Achieve NPS of 72+ for the checkout experience (currently 54)

---

Notice: the Objective is inspiring and memorable. The Key Results have current numbers and target numbers. Anyone on the team can look at these and know exactly whether they are winning or losing.

### OKRs at Google

When Larry Page and Sergey Brin set OKRs at Google, they set them publicly — everyone at Google can see what the CEO's OKRs are. This creates alignment. When the CEO's OKR is "Make Google Search available in 50 new languages," every team working on translation knows it matters at the highest level.

Google also follows a rule: **OKRs should be set at roughly 70% achievable.** If you are hitting 100% of your OKRs every quarter, you are not being ambitious enough. OKRs that stretch you — that you hit 70-80% of the time — drive faster growth than comfortable goals you always hit at 100%.

### Common Mistakes with OKRs

| Mistake | What It Looks Like | Why It Fails |
|---|---|---|
| KRs are tasks, not outcomes | "Launch 3 new features" | Does not tell you if those features worked |
| Too many OKRs | 15 objectives, 50 key results | No focus — everything is a priority, nothing is |
| OKRs are just the same as last quarter | Copy-pasting without thinking | No growth ambition |
| No check-ins | Set it in January, review in December | By then it is too late to course-correct |
| Sandbagging | Setting easy targets to look good | Destroys the stretch culture |

The best OKR cadence is: set annually, review quarterly, check in weekly. That weekly check-in is what separates companies that actually use OKRs from companies that just have them written on a wall somewhere.

## 7. Value Chain Analysis — Where Does a Business Actually Create Value?

Michael Porter (the same guy who created Five Forces) also gave us Value Chain Analysis. It asks: across all the activities a business does, where is the value actually being created? And where is money being wasted?

The idea is that a business is not one monolithic thing — it is a chain of linked activities. Porter splits these into:

**Primary Activities** (directly create the product or service):
1. Inbound logistics — receiving and storing raw materials
2. Operations — turning inputs into products
3. Outbound logistics — delivering products to customers
4. Marketing and sales — making customers want to buy
5. Service — after-sale support and maintenance

**Support Activities** (make the primary activities possible):
1. Firm infrastructure — finance, legal, management
2. Human resource management — hiring, training, culture
3. Technology development — R&D, software, automation
4. Procurement — buying materials and services

### What This Looks Like for Netflix

Let us trace Netflix's value chain:

**Inbound logistics:** Content licensing deals (paying studios) + original content production (Netflix Studios). This is where a huge chunk of Netflix's $17 billion annual content budget goes.

**Operations:** The streaming platform itself — servers, CDN (content delivery network), encoding technology. Netflix has one of the most sophisticated video compression systems in the world — they spend less on bandwidth than competitors because their algorithms are better.

**Outbound logistics:** The app on your phone, TV, laptop. The UX, the recommendation algorithm, the download feature. This is all delivery.

**Marketing and sales:** Trailers, social media, PR for big shows like Squid Game. Netflix also uses personalized marketing — showing you the specific thumbnail for a show that is most likely to make *you* click.

**Service:** Customer support, billing, the "are you still watching?" feature that manages bandwidth.

**Where Netflix creates unique value:** The recommendation algorithm (operations) and the content library (inbound logistics). These two things are why people pay ₹199/month and do not cancel. If Netflix just had the same shows as everyone else with an average recommendation system, they would be just another streaming service.

Value chain analysis helps you find your **competitive advantage** — the specific step in your chain where you are dramatically better than competitors. Everything else can be average. That one step needs to be world-class.

## 8. Jobs To Be Done — Understanding What Customers Actually Want

Harvard Business School professor Clayton Christensen had an insight that sounds obvious once you hear it, but changes how you think about products forever: **customers do not buy products. They hire products to do a job.**

When someone buys a milkshake from McDonald's at 8 AM on the way to work, the job they are hiring it to do is: "Keep me company and keep my hunger at bay during a boring commute." They are not hiring it because they are thirsty or because they want a treat. They are hiring it as a commute companion.

This insight — called Jobs To Be Done (JTBD) — completely changes how you design products and marketing.

### Three Types of Jobs

1. **Functional jobs:** The practical task. "I need to get from home to the airport."
2. **Social jobs:** How the customer wants to be perceived. "I want to look like someone who is environmentally conscious."
3. **Emotional jobs:** How the customer wants to feel. "I want to feel safe and in control."

Most businesses only think about the functional job. The best businesses solve all three.

### Example: Why People Use Airbnb vs. Hotels

**Functional job:** A place to sleep in a new city. (Both hotels and Airbnb solve this.)

**Social job:** "I want to look like a savvy, adventurous traveler, not a tourist at a corporate hotel."

**Emotional job:** "I want to feel like I am living in this city, not visiting it. I want a story to tell."

Hotels solve the functional job. Airbnb understood that the social and emotional jobs were being left unmet — and built their entire brand around that insight. "Belong Anywhere" is not a marketing slogan. It is their answer to the emotional job.

### Example: Why Zomato Succeeded Against Restaurant Websites

Before Zomato, restaurants had their own websites with menus and contact numbers. The functional job seemed solved. But JTBD reveals what was actually happening:

People ordering food had multiple jobs:
- "I want to browse options without commitment" — restaurant websites force you to decide on a restaurant before you see the menu
- "I want to trust that the restaurant is good" — reviews solve this
- "I want to avoid the awkward phone call" — online ordering solves this
- "I want someone to handle the logistics" — delivery aggregation solves this

Zomato hired itself for all these jobs simultaneously. That is why it won.

### How to Find the Jobs Your Customers Are Hiring For

The best method is an interview where you ask customers not what they want, but to walk you through the last time they used your product: when did the thought first occur to them, what were they doing, what alternatives did they consider, what almost stopped them from buying?

This "switch interview" approach, developed by Bob Moesta, reveals the actual job being done — which is almost never what you assumed.

## 9. When to Use Which Framework (and When to Ignore All of Them)

Here is a cheat sheet for matching frameworks to situations:

| Situation | Best Framework | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Evaluating your own company's position | SWOT | Covers both internal and external factors |
| Deciding whether to enter a new country/market | PESTLE | Scans the macro environment thoroughly |
| Deciding which products to invest in vs. cut | BCG Matrix | Forces portfolio-level thinking |
| Deciding how to grow | Ansoff Matrix | Maps growth options by risk level |
| Setting company or team goals | OKRs | Creates alignment and accountability |
| Finding where to build competitive advantage | Value Chain Analysis | Identifies highest-leverage activities |
| Understanding why customers buy | Jobs To Be Done | Reveals true motivation, not stated preference |
| Understanding competitive dynamics | Porter's Five Forces | Maps structural forces in an industry |

### When to Ignore All of Them

Frameworks are pre-made maps. But as the strategist Roger Martin says, "Strategy is about making choices, not filling in frameworks."

A few situations where frameworks can mislead:

**When speed matters more than completeness.** In a startup, doing a thorough PESTLE analysis for three weeks while your competitor launches might be the worst strategic decision you make. Sometimes "good enough analysis, fast decision" beats "perfect analysis, slow decision."

**When the situation is genuinely unprecedented.** During COVID-19, every BCG matrix and PESTLE analysis became obsolete overnight. Some situations require fresh thinking, not old maps.

**When the framework drives the answer you wanted anyway.** This is called confirmation bias with extra steps. If you already know you want to launch in Southeast Asia and you do a PESTLE that conveniently looks positive, your framework did not help you think — it helped you rationalize.

**When the real problem is interpersonal, not analytical.** Sometimes a team is underperforming not because they lack a framework, but because the manager is bad or there is a broken relationship. OKRs will not fix that.

The best business thinkers use frameworks as starting points and then trust their judgment when the framework gives an answer that feels wrong. The framework is a tool. Your judgment is the craftsperson.

## Summary

In this chapter, you learned eight major business analysis frameworks:

| Framework | Core Question | Best Used For |
|---|---|---|
| **SWOT** | What are our internal strengths/weaknesses and external opportunities/threats? | Company or product strategy reviews |
| **PESTLE** | What macro forces will affect us in this environment? | Market entry, regulatory planning |
| **BCG Matrix** | Which products/businesses deserve investment and which should be cut? | Portfolio management |
| **Ansoff Matrix** | What is our growth strategy and how risky is it? | Growth planning |
| **OKRs** | What are we trying to achieve and how do we measure success? | Goal-setting and alignment |
| **Value Chain** | Where in our operations do we create (or destroy) value? | Competitive advantage identification |
| **Jobs To Be Done** | What job are customers actually hiring our product to do? | Product design and marketing |
| **Porter's Five Forces** | How attractive and competitive is our industry? | Industry analysis (covered in Ch. 11) |

The meta-lesson: frameworks are for structured thinking, not for replacing thinking. Use them as a conversation starter, not a final answer. The best strategists know all the frameworks and know when to set them aside.

## Exercises and Discussion Questions

1. **SWOT in practice:** Pick any Indian startup that has been in the news recently — Meesho, CRED, Nykaa, or Groww. Do a proper SWOT for them. After filling out the four quadrants, write at least two SO moves and one WT risk. Do not stop at the list — write the strategic implications.

2. **PESTLE for a real expansion:** Imagine Blinkit (quick commerce) is considering expansion to a city in the UAE like Dubai. Do a PESTLE analysis. What is the single biggest opportunity? What is the single biggest threat?

3. **BCG on yourself:** Think of a company you know well (where a family member works, or a brand you follow). List 5 of their products or business units. Where would you put each one on the BCG matrix? What would you tell the CEO to do about their Dogs?

4. **OKR writing exercise:** You are the head of growth at a D2C brand that sells protein supplements online. Write one OKR for the next quarter. Make sure the Objective is inspiring and the Key Results have real numbers. Then trade it with a classmate or friend and critique each other's: are the KRs outcomes or tasks? Are they measurable?

5. **Jobs To Be Done interview:** Ask three people in your life about the last purchase they made that cost more than ₹1,000. Use the switch interview approach: when did the thought first occur to you? What were you doing? What alternatives did you consider? What almost stopped you from buying? Write up what jobs they were really trying to get done.

6. **Ansoff application:** Trace the growth story of any company you admire from founding to today. Map each major growth move onto the Ansoff Matrix. Which quadrant did they spend most time in early on? When did they attempt diversification, and did it work?

7. **Framework failure:** Think of a business decision that you know went wrong — either historically (like Nokia missing the smartphone shift) or something more recent. Which framework, if applied correctly, might have prevented that mistake? Which framework might have still missed the problem? This exercise teaches you both the power and limits of structured analysis.
