# Chapter 56: Netflix — Reinventing Itself Three Times

*Netflix is one of the only companies in history to successfully disrupt itself three times — DVDs to streaming, streaming to original content, originals to a global entertainment empire. Each transition required abandoning what made them successful.*

---

## Act 1: The DVD-by-Mail Business (1997-2007)

**The founding story:** In 1997, Reed Hastings returned a Blockbuster video rental and had to pay a $40 late fee for Apollo 13. He was embarrassed. The story of Netflix starting because of a Blockbuster late fee is partially myth (Hastings has acknowledged the story is somewhat embellished), but it captures the real insight: the video rental business was built on punishing customers.

Marc Randolph (co-founder) and Hastings tested whether people would rent DVDs by mail. They mailed a DVD to themselves. It arrived undamaged. The model worked.

**Netflix vs Blockbuster:**

Netflix had a structural advantage: no stores. Blockbuster had enormous retail overhead — rent, staff, utilities for 9,000 locations. Netflix had warehouses. Blockbuster's cost per transaction was dramatically higher.

**The subscription model pivot (1999):** Netflix's original model was pay-per-rental. Then they pivoted to the subscription model: flat monthly fee, unlimited rentals, no late fees.

This was genius for three reasons:
1. Predictable revenue for Netflix
2. No late fees (the customer's biggest frustration with Blockbuster)
3. Changed the psychology — you keep a movie as long as you want

**Blockbuster's response (too late):** In 2004, Blockbuster launched Blockbuster Online with no late fees. It was working. Blockbuster was gaining subscribers. But the Blockbuster board fired CEO John Antioco (who was running the online push) in 2005 because it was hurting the retail stores' revenue. The new CEO reversed the no-late-fee policy.

Netflix CEO Reed Hastings called this "the best thing that ever happened to us."

By 2010, Blockbuster had filed for bankruptcy. Netflix had 20 million subscribers.

---

## Act 2: The Streaming Pivot (2007-2013)

In 2007, Netflix launched "Watch Now" — a streaming feature alongside the DVD business. You could watch some content on your computer, instantly.

At the time: streaming was a small add-on. The real business was still DVDs.

The bet: streaming was the future. DVDs were the past. Netflix needed to be streaming before streaming became obvious.

**The technology challenge:** Streaming video required:
- Fast internet (broadband was still limited in 2007)
- Licensing deals with studios for digital rights
- Software that worked across devices

Netflix solved all three over several years, striking deals with studios, building apps for every device, and watching as broadband spread.

**The infamous Qwikster disaster (2011):** Netflix tried to split the DVD and streaming businesses into two separate companies/websites. DVD customers would use "Qwikster." Streaming customers would use "Netflix."

The customer reaction was catastrophic. Netflix lost 800,000 subscribers in one quarter — the largest subscriber loss in the company's history at that point. The stock fell 77%.

Hastings reversed the decision within three weeks. One of the fastest public course-corrections in business history.

The lesson: you can have a right long-term direction and execute it badly. The strategy was correct (streaming was the future); the execution (force customers to manage two accounts) was terrible.

## Act 3: Original Content (2013-2020)

House of Cards (2013) was the turning point. Netflix produced its first original series with $100 million for two seasons, starring Kevin Spacey, directed by David Fincher.

The data-driven bet: Netflix knew from user behavior that:
- Users loved David Fincher movies
- Users loved Kevin Spacey movies
- Users loved the original BBC House of Cards

The intersection was obvious: commission this show. The show was offered to HBO first; they passed. Netflix ordered it sight unseen, for two seasons.

House of Cards was a massive success. It signaled that Netflix could produce prestige TV — quality equivalent to HBO.

**Why original content changed everything:**

**Content as moat:** If a show is only on Netflix, you have to subscribe to Netflix to watch it. Licensed content can be taken away (studios can pull their content). Original content is owned by Netflix permanently.

**Global production:** Netflix could produce shows for global audiences — not just US shows dubbed into other languages, but shows made natively for India, Korea, Mexico.

**Breaking the weekly release model:** Netflix released entire seasons at once. "Binge-watching" became a cultural phenomenon. This was different from every network's weekly episode model.

**Indian content:** Sacred Games (2018) was Netflix's first Indian original — a Hindi-language crime thriller. It was a global hit, watched in 191 countries. Proved that Indian content could reach global audiences.

## The Password Sharing Crackdown (2023)

For years, Netflix had turned a blind eye to password sharing. It was estimated that 100+ million households shared passwords.

In 2023, Netflix enforced password sharing restrictions. You could only watch on devices in your household. Extra members outside the household would need to pay an extra fee.

The initial reaction: widespread criticism, predictions of massive subscriber losses.

The actual result: Netflix added 13.1 million subscribers in Q4 2023 (after the crackdown). The crackdown worked better than anyone expected. People who were using shared passwords were willing to pay for their own subscription.

---

## The Ad-Supported Tier (2022)

Netflix launched a cheaper, ad-supported subscription tier for $6.99/month (vs $15.49 for ad-free).

Why this mattered:
- Opens Netflix to price-sensitive customers who wouldn't pay $15
- Advertising revenue on top of subscription revenue
- Netflix's specific user data (what you watch, for how long) is extremely valuable for targeted advertising

The ad tier signed up 40 million subscribers in its first 18 months.

## Netflix's Business Model

**Revenue:**
- Subscriptions: primary revenue driver (~$33B in 2023)
- Advertising: growing but still small

**The economics:**
- Average revenue per membership: $16/month
- Content spend: $17B/year (one of the world's largest content buyers)
- The question: is $17B/year in content the right number, or too much?

**Pricing tiers (India, 2024):**
- Mobile Plan: ₹149/month (mobile only, HD)
- Basic: ₹199/month
- Standard: ₹499/month
- Premium: ₹649/month

India is priced dramatically lower than US ($15-$23). The Indian market requires price sensitivity.

## The Streaming Wars

Netflix created the streaming category but now faces aggressive competition:

| Platform | Owner | Strategy |
|----------|-------|----------|
| Netflix | Independent | Original content, global |
| Disney+ | Disney | Franchises (Marvel, Star Wars, Pixar) |
| HBO Max | Warner Bros Discovery | Prestige TV |
| Amazon Prime Video | Amazon | Bundled with Prime membership |
| Apple TV+ | Apple | Small but high-quality originals |
| JioCinema | Reliance | India-first, free (cricket) |
| Hotstar | Tata/Disney | India, cricket, originals |

**In India:** JioCinema disrupted the market by streaming IPL for free. Disney+ Hotstar (which had paid for IPL rights) lost millions of subscribers. The Indian streaming wars are driven by cricket, not Hollywood.

## Key Lessons from Netflix

**1. Destroy your own business before a competitor does.** Netflix was the world's best DVD-by-mail company in 2007. Instead of protecting that business, they aggressively built streaming — which would cannibalize it. This took courage.

**2. Data is a competitive advantage.** Netflix's recommendation algorithm and content investment decisions are driven by knowing what 200+ million people watch, for how long, and what they watch next.

**3. Culture at Netflix is unusual and intentional.** The Netflix Culture Deck (written by Hastings) is one of the most influential documents in startup culture: no vacation policy, no expense policy, hire only "fully formed adults," generous severance for people who don't fit. It's extreme, but consistent.

**4. Content costs are a treadmill.** Netflix must spend $17B/year to maintain competitiveness. If they reduce content spend, subscribers leave. Content is an ongoing obligation, not a one-time investment.

**5. Global from day one.** Netflix's original content strategy was global — Korean content (Squid Game) watched in 90 countries. This created a flywheel: global subscriptions fund global content, which attracts more global subscribers.

---

## Summary

- Netflix started as DVD-by-mail, won by eliminating late fees and using a subscription model
- Blockbuster's board fired the CEO who was fighting back, sealing their fate
- Streaming pivot (2007) was the right long-term direction; Qwikster was wrong execution
- House of Cards (2013) began the original content era — content as moat
- Password sharing crackdown (2023) worked better than expected, adding 13M+ subscribers in one quarter
