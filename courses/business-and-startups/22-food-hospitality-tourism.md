## 1. The Experience Economy — Why People Pay for Experiences, Not Just Things

In 1999, two consultants named B. Joseph Pine II and James Gilmore wrote a book that would quietly change how businesses think. The book was called *The Experience Economy*. Its core argument was simple but profound:

**The economy was shifting from selling things to selling feelings.**

Here is the progression they described. Think about coffee:

| Stage | What You're Selling | Example | What You Charge |
|---|---|---|---|
| Commodity | Raw coffee beans | A farmer selling beans | ₹50 per kg |
| Good | Packaged coffee | Nescafe jar at a supermarket | ₹300 per 200g |
| Service | A cup made for you | Coffee at a railway stall | ₹20 per cup |
| Experience | A whole vibe | Starbucks | ₹350 per cup |

The same coffee beans. Completely different prices. What changed?

At the commodity stage, you're just selling a raw material. At the goods stage, you've processed and packaged it. At the service stage, someone makes it for you. But at the experience stage, Starbucks is not really selling you coffee — they're selling you a comfortable chair, your name written on a cup, the idea of treating yourself, the feeling of sitting in a "third place" that is neither home nor office. The coffee is almost incidental.

This is the Experience Economy: **the idea that people will pay a premium not for what a thing is, but for how it makes them feel.**

This matters enormously for businesses in food, hospitality, and tourism because these are industries built almost entirely on engineering feelings. A hotel room is just a bed and a bathroom — technically. But a five-star hotel room makes you feel important, pampered, and special. That feeling is what you're paying for.

### Why Experiences Are Hard to Commoditize

Physical products can be copied. Flipkart can stock the same phone that Amazon does. But an experience is much harder to replicate. The reason Virat Kohli's favorite restaurant in Mumbai can charge ₹5,000 a plate is not just the food — it's the story, the location, the service, the specific way they make you feel like a VIP. That bundle of feelings cannot easily be copied.

This means that experience-driven businesses, when they do them right, can have extraordinary pricing power and customer loyalty. The flip side: if the experience disappoints, the business collapses fast. There is no hiding behind a product spec sheet.

## 2. Restaurants — The Hardest Business in the World

Let's talk about restaurants. Nearly everyone has dreamed of opening one. "I cook great food," the thinking goes. "I'll open a place, people will love it, I'll make money." This dream dies with brutal regularity.

**Roughly 60% of restaurants fail in their first year. About 80% are gone within five years.**

Why is this business so hard? Let's look at the economics.

### The Restaurant P&L (Profit and Loss)

Imagine you open a mid-range restaurant in Mumbai. You charge ₹500 per person on average. On a good weekend, you serve 100 people. Revenue: ₹50,000 for the day.

Now subtract:

```
Revenue                          ₹50,000   (100%)
Food Cost (ingredients)         -₹17,500   (35%)
Staff Wages (chefs, waiters)    -₹12,500   (25%)
Rent                             -₹8,000   (16%)
Utilities (gas, electricity)     -₹2,500   (5%)
Other (packaging, cleaning)      -₹1,500   (3%)
-------------------------------------------
Operating Profit                  ₹8,000   (16%)
```

That 16% looks okay — until you realize a few things:
- This was a **good** day. On a slow Tuesday, you might serve 30 covers and lose money.
- Equipment breaks. A commercial refrigerator costs ₹2-3 lakh to replace.
- Staff turnover is brutal. Training new chefs costs time and money constantly.
- One bad review on Zomato can empty your restaurant for weeks.

Fine dining restaurants often have even worse economics. The food cost is higher (premium ingredients), the staff-to-table ratio is higher (more waiters per guest), and the restaurant might only serve two turns per day (lunch and dinner). A Michelin-starred chef in Paris may earn critical acclaim but barely break even.

### Why Restaurants Fail

**Reason 1 — The owner thinks they're a cook, not a businessperson.** Great food is necessary but not sufficient. You also need to manage inventory, train staff, control costs, market yourself, and handle the accounting. Most chefs who open restaurants are trained in food, not operations.

**Reason 2 — Underestimating rent.** Restaurant rent in a good location in Mumbai or Bangalore can be ₹2-5 lakh per month. That's before you've sold a single plate of food.

**Reason 3 — No repeat customers.** A restaurant lives or dies on regulars. If people come once and don't come back, you're constantly spending on acquiring new customers.

**Reason 4 — Seasonality and competition.** New restaurants open constantly. The hottest place in town today is yesterday's news in six months.

### What Makes Restaurants Succeed

The restaurants that survive and thrive usually do one or more of the following:

- **Nail a niche and own it.** Saravana Bhavan in Chennai doesn't try to be everything — it does South Indian food at scale, efficiently, consistently.
- **Location, location, location.** The right street, the right foot traffic, the right neighborhood.
- **High table turnover.** A fast-casual place that turns a table 5 times in an evening earns 5x the revenue from the same space as a fine dining place that turns it once.
- **Build a brand.** Biryani by Kilo turned biryani into a premium experience. The old-fashioned clay pot delivery, the story of traditional cooking — they charged ₹600+ for a biryani that could cost ₹120 at a dhaba. The experience justified the price.

## 3. Cloud Kitchens — The Pandemic Innovation

In March 2020, restaurants across India shut overnight. Dine-in was illegal. The restaurants that survived were the ones that could pivot to delivery.

This acceleration forced an idea that was already growing: **the cloud kitchen.**

A cloud kitchen (also called a ghost kitchen or dark kitchen) is a restaurant with no dining room. No tables. No ambiance. No waiters. Just a commercial kitchen optimized for producing food that gets delivered to your door.

### Why Cloud Kitchens Make Business Sense

Think about what a traditional restaurant spends money on:
- Rent for a large, visible, attractive space (premium location = premium rent)
- Interior design and furniture
- Front-of-house staff (hosts, waiters, managers)
- Presentation (nice plates, uniforms, music systems)

A cloud kitchen eliminates almost all of this. You rent a small space in a cheap industrial area. You focus 100% on cooking. Your "storefront" is a listing on Zomato or Swiggy.

```
Traditional Restaurant Rent (Bandra, Mumbai):  ₹3,00,000/month
Cloud Kitchen Rent (industrial zone nearby):   ₹40,000/month
```

That difference of ₹2.6 lakh per month is what makes the economics work.

### The Multi-Brand Cloud Kitchen

Here's where it gets clever. One kitchen can run five "brands" simultaneously. Imagine a cloud kitchen in Koramangala, Bangalore:

- Brand 1: "Biryani Brothers" (biryani specialist)
- Brand 2: "Wok This Way" (Chinese-ish bowls)
- Brand 3: "The Breakfast Club" (morning delivery only)
- Brand 4: "Pizza Underground" (late-night pizza)
- Brand 5: "Salad Days" (healthy office lunch)

All five run from the same kitchen, often reusing the same base ingredients. Each appears as a separate restaurant on Zomato and Swiggy with its own brand, menu, and photos. This is genius: you're maximizing utilization of the kitchen equipment, staff, and delivery window while building multiple revenue streams.

Companies like Rebel Foods (which runs Faasos, Behrouz Biryani, Oven Story, and others) pioneered this model in India and are now valued at over $1 billion.

### The Downside of Cloud Kitchens

The model has real weaknesses. You are entirely dependent on Zomato and Swiggy for discovery. They take 20-30% commission on every order. If they change their algorithm or fee structure, your business changes overnight. There is no walk-in customer, no loyal local who sees your restaurant daily. Building brand loyalty through an app is much harder than through a physical space.

## 4. Restaurant Chains and Franchise Models (McDonald's, Domino's)

If the individual restaurant is the hardest business, the **restaurant chain** is one of the most remarkable business model innovations of the 20th century.

McDonald's doesn't just sell burgers. It sells a system. The system includes:
- A standardized menu (you get the same McAloo Tikki in Delhi and Chennai)
- Standardized kitchen equipment and processes
- Standardized training
- Standardized supply chains

The consistency is the product. When you walk into a McDonald's anywhere in the world, the slight uncertainty of trying a new place is eliminated. You know exactly what you're going to get. This predictability has massive value to customers who are tired, hungry, and don't want to make decisions.

### How the Franchise Model Works

Most major restaurant chains grow through franchising. Here's what that means:

A franchise is essentially a license to use someone else's brand, systems, and recipes to run a business. You (the franchisee) pay:
1. An upfront franchise fee (can be ₹20-50 lakh for a major brand)
2. A percentage of your revenue every month (the "royalty," usually 4-8%)
3. A contribution to the national marketing fund (1-2%)

In exchange, you get the brand, the training, the systems, and the customers who already trust the name.

For the franchisor (McDonald's, Domino's), this is extraordinary: they grow their business using someone else's capital. The franchisee takes the risk of setting up the store and hiring staff. McDonald's just collects fees.

**Domino's in India** (run by Jubilant Foodworks, which is the franchisee for Domino's in India) has over 1,800 stores and is publicly listed. Their "30-minute delivery" promise was one of the most effective marketing promises in Indian food history — it framed a logistical operation as a brand value.

## 5. Hotels — Asset-Heavy vs Asset-Light

Hotels are fascinating because they exist in two completely different business models living under the same name.

### The Old Model: Asset-Heavy

The Taj Mahal Palace in Mumbai is a building. A beautiful, historic, irreplaceable building on the waterfront. The Tata Group owns it. It costs hundreds of crores to maintain. The hotel has to fill those rooms night after night to justify the cost of owning and running that building.

This is the **asset-heavy** hotel model. The hotel company owns or leases the physical property. The economics are like real estate: high upfront investment, steady income if occupancy is high, brutal losses if rooms stay empty.

Key metrics for hotels:
- **Occupancy Rate**: What % of rooms are filled tonight? (Industry average: 60-70%)
- **ADR (Average Daily Rate)**: What is the average price per room per night?
- **RevPAR (Revenue Per Available Room)**: Occupancy Rate × ADR. This is the key health metric.

```
If a hotel has 100 rooms:
  Occupancy: 70% = 70 rooms filled
  ADR: ₹6,000/night
  RevPAR = 0.70 × ₹6,000 = ₹4,200/room/night
  Total nightly revenue = ₹4,200 × 100 = ₹4,20,000
```

### The New Model: Asset-Light (Marriott, Hilton)

Over the last 30 years, the smart global hotel chains figured out a trick: **why own the building if you can just manage it?**

Marriott International manages over 8,000 hotels worldwide. But it owns almost none of them. Here is how it works:

- A real estate investor builds a hotel in Pune
- They pay Marriott a fee to use the "Marriott" brand and management systems
- Marriott sends its managers, its training programs, its booking systems, its loyalty program (Marriott Bonvoy)
- The investor gets the brand recognition and bookings; Marriott gets the management fee (usually 2-4% of revenue)
- Neither takes the other's risk

This is the asset-light model, and it is extraordinarily profitable for Marriott. They grow without deploying their own capital. The risk of an empty hotel lies with the property owner.

### Where Airbnb Fits

Airbnb took the asset-light model to its logical extreme: it owns zero buildings. It is a marketplace. It connects people who have a spare room (or a whole apartment) with people who need a place to sleep. Airbnb's job is just to maintain trust between strangers and take a fee (around 3% from hosts, 14% from guests).

At its peak valuation of ~$100 billion, Airbnb was worth more than Marriott despite owning no property whatsoever. That is the power of being a marketplace platform versus an operator.

## 6. OYO — India's Most Ambitious (and Troubled) Hotel Chain

In 2013, a 19-year-old named Ritesh Agarwal dropped out of college and started OYO Rooms. The idea was elegant: India had millions of small, dingy budget hotels that were unreliable and hard to book. OYO would standardize them — fix the wifi, ensure clean sheets, establish a minimum standard — and list them under the OYO brand.

At its peak, OYO claimed to be the world's largest hotel chain by number of rooms (over 1 million rooms in 80+ countries). SoftBank poured in billions. Ritesh appeared on every magazine cover.

Then things fell apart.

### What Went Wrong at OYO

**Problem 1 — Growth at any cost.** OYO signed contracts with thousands of hotel owners promising them a minimum guaranteed revenue (essentially, "we'll pay you even if your rooms stay empty"). This worked when the company was growing. When COVID hit and no one was traveling, OYO was paying hotel owners for empty rooms it had no money to fill.

**Problem 2 — Hotel owners revolted.** Many hotel owners claimed OYO stopped paying the guaranteed rents. Thousands of hotels delisted from OYO's platform. There were protests. The brand took a serious hit in India.

**Problem 3 — Rapid overseas expansion too early.** OYO expanded to the US, UK, China, Southeast Asia, and Japan before it had figured out the Indian model. Each market had different regulations, customer expectations, and cost structures.

**Problem 4 — The unit economics never worked.** OYO was subsidizing stays to show growth. Cheap rooms drove bookings, but at a loss. When the subsidies had to stop, so did the growth.

OYO has since restructured, laid off thousands of employees, and refocused on profitability. It went public in India in 2023 at a fraction of its peak valuation. The story is a classic cautionary tale about growth-at-all-costs thinking.

**The lesson:** In hospitality, trust and consistency are everything. If hotel owners don't trust you and guests have bad experiences, no amount of venture capital can fix it.

## 7. Food Delivery — Zomato and Swiggy's $30 Billion Question

In 2015, getting food delivered to your home in India meant calling a restaurant directly, hoping someone answered, describing your address (which may or may not exist on any map), and waiting with no information. It was terrible.

Zomato and Swiggy changed this. They built an aggregator platform — a single app where you could discover hundreds of restaurants, order with a few taps, and track your delivery in real time. It felt like magic.

Today, Zomato is a publicly listed company worth roughly ₹1.5-2 lakh crore ($15-20 billion). Swiggy is similarly valued. Together they process hundreds of millions of orders per month. But here's the uncomfortable question that investors ask constantly:

**Is food delivery actually a good business?**

### The Unit Economics of Food Delivery

Let's trace what happens with a single ₹400 order:

```
Customer pays                              ₹400
Platform fee + delivery charge paid by customer  ₹50
Total money collected by Zomato            ₹450

Restaurant pays Zomato commission (~20%)  -₹80
Delivery executive payment                -₹60
Customer acquisition / discounts / ads    -₹30
Platform costs (payment gateway, infra)   -₹10
-------------------------------------------
Contribution per order                    ~₹270 earned, ~₹100 cost = ₹170
But after overheads, marketing, R&D...    Still marginally profitable or breakeven
```

For years, both Zomato and Swiggy lost money on nearly every order. They compensated with discounts and promotions to build the habit, betting that once customers were addicted to the convenience, they could slowly reduce discounts and raise margins.

Zomato turned profitable (on an adjusted basis) in 2023. Swiggy went public in 2024. The story is moving toward "yes, it's a real business" — but it took nearly a decade of burning cash to get there.

### Why Food Delivery Is Hard

The fundamental problem is that food delivery is a **logistics business with food as the product**. Logistics is hard, expensive, and thin-margined. You need delivery executives everywhere at all times. You need fast delivery (under 30 minutes is the expectation). Food is perishable and doesn't travel well.

Additionally, unlike a software company, every single order requires a human being to physically move. You cannot digitize the last mile. This means the business cannot scale with near-zero marginal cost the way a Netflix or WhatsApp can.

### What Makes Zomato Interesting

Despite the unit economics challenges, Zomato has some genuine strengths:
- **Blinkit acquisition (instant grocery in 10 minutes)**: This changed the game. The same delivery infrastructure used for food can deliver groceries, medicines, and eventually anything. This makes the business much larger.
- **Brand and trust**: Zomato has extraordinary brand recognition in India. Its marketing is legendary (their social media and ads are genuinely funny and well-crafted).
- **Data moat**: Zomato knows what millions of Indians eat, when they eat it, how much they spend, and what triggers them to order. That data is worth a lot.

## 8. Tourism as an Economy — India's Untapped Opportunity

Tourism is one of the world's largest industries. Before COVID, it accounted for about 10% of global GDP and 1 in 10 jobs worldwide.

India is one of the most extraordinary travel destinations on Earth. The country has the Himalayas, the Thar Desert, the Kerala backwaters, Rajasthan's palaces, ancient temples, colonial cities, tribal cultures, and over 7,500 km of coastline. And yet India dramatically underperforms its potential as a tourism destination.

**In 2019, France received 90 million foreign tourists. India received about 11 million.** That gap is staggering given that India has far more to offer in terms of diversity.

### Why Does India Underperform in Tourism?

**Infrastructure.** Getting around India is hard. Airports in tier-2 cities were, until recently, poor. Railway connectivity is good but slow. Road quality is inconsistent. The experience of "getting there" deters foreign tourists who are used to European or Southeast Asian ease of travel.

**Cleanliness and safety perception.** India has a perception problem (and partly a real problem) around cleanliness at tourist sites and safety for solo female travelers. This is improving, but it's a significant barrier.

**Promotion.** Thailand spends more on international tourism promotion than India does despite being a fraction of the size. The "Incredible India" campaign is famous, but sustained global marketing at scale has been inconsistent.

### What India Is Getting Right

The Char Dham highway project, new airports in Ayodhya, Dholera, and dozens of other cities, the bullet train corridor, and aggressive promotion of MICE (Meetings, Incentives, Conferences, and Exhibitions) tourism are all moves in the right direction.

**Domestic tourism is already massive.** Indians travel within India in enormous numbers — to religious destinations, hill stations, beaches, and cities. Amarnath, Vaishno Devi, Tirupati — these shrines see millions of visitors per year. This domestic tourism economy is worth hundreds of thousands of crores and is growing fast with rising middle-class incomes.

## 9. QSR vs Fast Casual vs Fine Dining — A World of Differences

Not all restaurants are the same, and the business model differences are enormous.

| Category | Example | Avg Spend (India) | Table Turnover | Margins | Key Focus |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| QSR (Quick Service Restaurant) | McDonald's, Domino's, KFC | ₹200–400 | 5-8x per day | Low per order, high volume | Speed, consistency, cost |
| Fast Casual | Chipotle, Barbeque Nation | ₹400–800 | 3-4x per day | Medium | Quality + speed balance |
| Casual Dining | Pizza Hut (sit-down), Chili's | ₹600–1,200 | 2-3x per day | Medium | Experience + food combo |
| Fine Dining | Masque, Indian Accent | ₹3,000–10,000+ | 1-1.5x per day | High per order, low volume | Experience, exclusivity |

QSR is essentially a manufacturing business. McDonald's kitchen is a production line. Every action is standardized down to the second. The goal is throughput: how many customers can move through this space and leave satisfied in the shortest time? Margins per customer are thin, but volume makes up for it.

Fine dining is essentially a performance. You are paying for a two-hour theater experience where the chef is the director. The kitchen may be tiny. You may serve 30 covers per dinner service. But each cover may pay ₹5,000-15,000, and the chef's reputation commands prices that make the economics work despite low volume.

## 10. Supply Chain in Food — The Long Road from Farm to Fork

Every time you eat a burger at McDonald's, you are consuming the output of a supply chain that started months earlier on a farm somewhere. Food supply chains are among the most complex in the world — and getting them wrong has catastrophic consequences.

### The Chain

```
Farmer → Aggregator/Mandi → Processor/Manufacturer → Distributor → Retailer/Restaurant → You
```

Each step adds cost and time. Each step creates a point of failure. Tomatoes rot. Cold chains break. Trucks break down. Customs documentation gets delayed.

**Cold chain** is a specific supply chain for perishable foods that must stay cold throughout transport — from dairy to meat to fresh vegetables. India has historically had a terrible cold chain infrastructure, which is one reason so much agricultural produce rots before it reaches consumers (estimates suggest 15-20% of India's agricultural output is wasted post-harvest).

### Why Farm-to-Fork Matters for Business

Companies that control more of their supply chain tend to have better margins and better quality control. Amul controls the entire dairy supply chain — from the cooperative farmers who supply milk, to the processing plants, to the cold chain, to the brand. This vertical integration is why Amul can offer consistent quality at low prices, and why it's been profitable for decades.

McDonald's India partner (Connaught Plaza Restaurants) works with specific contracted potato farmers to grow the specific variety of potato needed for their fries. This seems excessive until you realize that inconsistent potatoes mean inconsistent fries, and inconsistent fries destroy the brand promise.

## 11. FMCG — The Business of Everyday Habits

FMCG stands for Fast Moving Consumer Goods. These are products that are sold quickly and at relatively low cost — think toothpaste, shampoo, biscuits, tea, soap, oil, noodles. You use them daily and replace them regularly.

This category is one of the most profitable and defensible businesses in the world. Here is why:

### Why FMCG Is a Great Business

**Habit-based consumption.** You don't think about which toothpaste to buy. You buy the one you always buy. Colgate has used this to maintain market leadership in India for decades. Breaking a habit requires a significant disruption; maintaining a habit is nearly effortless.

**Low unit price, high frequency.** A ₹10 packet of Parle-G biscuits. A ₹5 sachet of Clinic Plus shampoo. Low price = easy to try = easier to convert to habit = purchased again and again.

**Distribution moat.** Hindustan Unilever (HUL) has products available in 8 million+ retail outlets across India — from urban supermarkets to remote village kirana stores. Building that distribution network took decades and cannot be replicated quickly by a new entrant. HUL's distribution network IS its competitive moat.

### Major Indian FMCG Companies

**HUL (Hindustan Unilever):** India's largest FMCG company by revenue. Owns Surf Excel, Dove, Pond's, Lipton, Kwality Wall's, Knorr, and dozens more. Revenue: ~₹60,000 crore. Subsidiary of Unilever UK.

**Nestle India:** Makes Maggi, KitKat, Nescafe, Munch, Milkmaid. The Maggi crisis of 2015 (when the government found high lead levels and banned it) nearly destroyed the brand — and then Nestle's recovery, within months, back to market leadership is one of the great brand crisis management stories in Indian business history.

**Britannia:** Makes Good Day, Marie Gold, Tiger, NutriChoice biscuits plus bread and dairy. Revenue: ~₹16,000 crore. Has been on a remarkable profitability improvement journey under MD Varun Berry.

**ITC:** Primarily tobacco, but also biscuits (Sunfeast), chips (Bingo), noodles (Yippee), and hotels. A fascinating conglomerate that used cigarette profits to build a diversified FMCG empire.

### The D2C Disruption in FMCG

New-age brands like Mamaearth, Sugar Cosmetics, and WOW Skin Science went direct-to-consumer via digital channels (Instagram, YouTube, their own websites) to bypass HUL's distribution network. They targeted younger, urban consumers who are willing to try new things and respond to social media. It worked — several of these brands have reached ₹1,000+ crore in revenue within five years.

The question for these brands is whether they can build distribution at scale (reach tier-2, tier-3 India) and whether they can defend market share when the HULs of the world respond with their own digital marketing. The fight is ongoing.

## Summary

This chapter covered an enormous amount of ground — from the philosophy behind the Experience Economy to the operational detail of restaurant margins, from Airbnb's asset-light model to Zomato's unit economics.

The big ideas to take away:

**1. Experiences command premiums that products cannot.** Starbucks charges 10x for coffee because it sells an experience. Hotels, restaurants, and tourism all compete on how they make people feel, not just what they provide.

**2. Restaurants are brutal businesses.** Low margins, high failure rates, fickle customers, and relentless competition. The ones that survive nail consistency, manage costs obsessively, and build genuine loyalty.

**3. Asset-light beats asset-heavy in the long run.** Airbnb vs traditional hotels. Marriott's franchise model vs independent hotels. The companies that figured out how to grow without owning physical assets built more valuable businesses faster.

**4. Food delivery is real, but hard.** Zomato and Swiggy are genuine businesses, but the path to profitability required years of losses, discipline, and pivots (like Blinkit). They are logistics businesses with thin margins.

**5. FMCG is quietly one of the best business models.** Habit, distribution, and brand create moats that last decades. HUL has been dominant in India for over 80 years. That kind of staying power is extraordinary.

**6. India's tourism potential is vastly underutilized.** The infrastructure, perception, and promotion gaps are real — but they're closing. The domestic tourism economy is already large and growing.

## Exercises and Discussion Questions

1. **Restaurant economics:** You want to open a small cafe in your city. The rent is ₹80,000/month. Your average bill per customer is ₹250. How many customers do you need per day just to cover rent? What other costs have you not accounted for? What would make this business succeed or fail?

2. **Experience design:** Think of a business that sells a commodity (a laundry service, a car wash, a barbershop). How would you redesign it as an "Experience Economy" business? What would you change? What premium could you charge? (Hint: look at how "The Laundress" positioned premium laundry products or how "Dry Bar" turned blow-drying into an experience.)

3. **Cloud kitchen vs traditional restaurant:** A friend wants to open a restaurant. Would you recommend starting as a cloud kitchen or a traditional dine-in place? What are the tradeoffs? Under what conditions does each make more sense?

4. **OYO case study:** In 2019, OYO was celebrated as one of India's greatest startup success stories. By 2021, it was in crisis. What do you think went wrong? What should Ritesh Agarwal have done differently? What lessons do you draw for any business that grows very fast?

5. **Zomato's business model:** Zomato charges restaurants a 20-25% commission. Some restaurant owners say this is too high and makes it impossible for them to be profitable. Zomato says the commission is justified by the customers they bring. Who is right? What would happen if Zomato lowered its commission? What would happen if restaurants tried to leave the platform?

6. **India's tourism gap:** France gets 90 million tourists per year. India gets 11 million despite having arguably more diverse attractions. If you were advising the Indian Tourism Ministry, what three specific things would you prioritize to close this gap? Think about infrastructure, marketing, and policy.

7. **FMCG vs D2C:** HUL has been India's FMCG leader for 80+ years. A new D2C brand like Mamaearth has grown to ₹2,000 crore in a few years via digital channels. Can Mamaearth ever actually threaten HUL's position? What would it take? Or will HUL simply copy the winning brands and use its distribution to crush them?

---

*The food and hospitality industry will always exist as long as humans want to eat, sleep, and travel. What changes is the form, the economics, and who captures the value. The businesses that understand the Experience Economy — that people pay for how things make them feel, not just what they provide — will always find a way to win.*
