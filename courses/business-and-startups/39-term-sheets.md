# Chapter 39: Term Sheets — What the Documents Actually Say

*Most founders sign term sheets without fully understanding them. Some lose millions of rupees because of clauses they didn't read carefully. This chapter fixes that.*

---

## 1. What a Term Sheet Is

A term sheet is a non-binding document that outlines the proposed terms of an investment. It's the agreement in principle — if both sides accept the term sheet, lawyers then draft the actual binding investment documents.

Non-binding means: you can walk away if the final documents don't match. The investor can too. But in practice, if you've signed a term sheet, you're expected to close the deal — walking away is a significant signal.

The term sheet sets two critical things:
1. **Economic terms:** How much money, at what valuation, with what financial rights
2. **Control terms:** Who controls what decisions

Both are equally important. Founders who focus only on valuation often end up surprised by the control provisions.

---

## 2. Pre-Money vs Post-Money Valuation

This is the most common source of confusion in early fundraising.

**Pre-money valuation:** What the company is worth *before* the investment comes in.

**Post-money valuation:** What the company is worth *after* the investment comes in.

**Post-money = Pre-money + Investment**

**Example:**
- Pre-money valuation: ₹40 crore
- Investment: ₹10 crore
- Post-money: ₹50 crore

**Investor ownership = Investment / Post-money = ₹10 crore / ₹50 crore = 20%**

**Why this matters:** If someone says "we're raising at a ₹50 crore valuation," ask: is that pre-money or post-money? A ₹50 crore pre-money at ₹10 crore raised = 16.7% dilution. A ₹50 crore post-money at ₹10 crore raised = 20% dilution.

With SAFEs, there's an additional wrinkle: Y Combinator's post-money SAFE means the investor's ownership percentage is calculated on post-money valuation at the *time of the SAFE*, not at the next priced round. This is subtly different (and requires careful reading).

## 3. Key Economic Terms

### Liquidation Preferences

A liquidation preference determines who gets paid first in an exit (acquisition, IPO, or wind-down).

**1x non-participating preferred:** The investor gets back their money first (1x their investment), and then *participates* equally with common shareholders in the remaining proceeds. This is founder-friendly.

**Example:** Investor puts in ₹10 crore for 20% of company. Company sells for ₹60 crore.
- Investor takes ₹10 crore (liquidation preference)
- Remaining ₹50 crore is split: 20% (investor) + 80% (founders/employees) = investor gets ₹10 crore, founders get ₹40 crore
- Wait — the investor chooses the better option: either take ₹10 crore pref, or convert to common and take 20% of ₹60 crore = ₹12 crore
- Investor takes ₹12 crore (they convert). Founders get ₹48 crore.

**1x participating preferred:** Investor gets back their ₹10 crore first *AND* then participates in remaining proceeds.

**Example:** Same scenario, ₹60 crore exit.
- Investor takes ₹10 crore (liquidation preference)
- Remaining ₹50 crore is split: 20% (investor) + 80% (founders)
- Investor gets ₹10 crore + ₹10 crore (20% of ₹50 crore) = ₹20 crore
- Founders get ₹40 crore

**2x participating preferred:** Investor gets back 2x their investment *AND* participates in remaining.

**Example:** ₹60 crore exit.
- Investor takes ₹20 crore (2x liquidation preference)
- Remaining ₹40 crore split 20/80 = investor gets ₹8 crore, founders get ₹32 crore
- Investor total: ₹28 crore. Founders: ₹32 crore.

The math shows: heavy liquidation preferences dramatically shift proceeds from founders to investors in moderate exits. In a huge exit (10x), liquidation preferences matter less. In a moderate exit (2-3x), they can wipe out founder and employee returns.

**Best for founders:** 1x non-participating preferred. Push back on anything more aggressive.

### Anti-Dilution Provisions

If the company raises a future round at a *lower* valuation (down round), anti-dilution provisions give investors more shares to compensate.

**Broad-based weighted average:** The most common and founder-friendly version. Adjusts the investor's conversion price based on the new round price, weighted by the number of shares issued.

**Narrow-based weighted average:** A bit less favorable. Fewer shares are included in the weighting.

**Full ratchet:** Most aggressive. The investor's price adjusts all the way down to the new round price. This can dramatically dilute founders in a down round.

**Demand in your term sheet:** Broad-based weighted average only. Full ratchet is a serious red flag.

### Pro-Rata Rights

The right to invest in future rounds to maintain their ownership percentage.

Example: Investor owns 15%. You raise a Series B. Investor can invest up to the amount needed to stay at 15% after the B round.

Pro-rata rights are standard and fair — investors who believe in you should be able to keep their stake. The issue is **super pro-rata rights** (the right to *increase* their percentage in future rounds), which can disadvantage founders and future investors.

## 4. Key Control Terms

### Board Composition

The board of directors has the final say on major company decisions:
- CEO hiring/firing
- Acquisition and IPO approval
- Major strategic pivots
- Future fundraising terms

A typical post-Series A board: 2 founder seats + 1 lead investor seat + 1 independent (total: 4)
Post-Series B: 2 founder + 2 investor + 1 independent (total: 5)

**Founder protection:** Ensure you have a majority of board votes at least through Series A. Once investors have board control, they can theoretically remove the founding CEO.

### Protective Provisions

Investors typically get the right to veto certain major decisions, regardless of board composition. Standard (and reasonable) protective provisions:
- Issuing new shares (new financing rounds)
- Selling the company
- Changing the company's structure or governance
- Paying dividends
- Incurring debt above a threshold

**Red flags in protective provisions:**
- Right to approve annual budget
- Right to approve executive compensation
- Right to approve any acquisition above a very low threshold
- Right to approve operational decisions

Protective provisions are normal; controlling day-to-day operations is not.

### Drag-Along Rights

If a majority of investors (and sometimes founders) vote to sell the company, they can force all other shareholders to sell their shares too.

This prevents minority shareholders from blocking an otherwise approved acquisition.

Standard and reasonable — minority shareholders shouldn't be able to hold the whole company hostage.

### Information Rights

Investors have the right to regular financial reporting (usually monthly or quarterly) and access to company information for diligence purposes.

Standard. Don't fight this — investors who are kept in the dark make bad advisors.

## 5. Common Founder-Unfriendly Clauses to Watch For

**1. Full ratchet anti-dilution** — as discussed, devastating in a down round.

**2. Multiple liquidation preferences (2x+)** — shifts exit proceeds dramatically to investors.

**3. Participating preferred without cap** — investors double-dip on every exit.

**4. Broad protective provisions** — investors controlling operational decisions is not governance, it's management.

**5. IPO ratchets** — a guarantee that if the IPO doesn't hit a certain price, investors get more shares. Rare, but punishing.

**6. No-shop clauses that are too long** — after signing a term sheet, you can't talk to other investors for 30-60 days. A 30-day no-shop is reasonable. 90 days lets investors drag their feet.

**7. Right of first refusal on secondary sales** — investors can buy your shares before you can sell to others. Common and somewhat reasonable, but ensure the pricing mechanism is fair.

## 6. How to Negotiate a Term Sheet

**Rule 1: Create competition.** The single most powerful negotiating tool is having multiple term sheets. When one investor knows another is interested, terms improve. Start multiple processes simultaneously.

**Rule 2: Know your walk-away point.** Before negotiating, decide: what terms am I not willing to accept? Going into negotiation without a walk-away point leads to accepting terms you'll regret.

**Rule 3: Understand what matters.** Founders often fight on valuation (vanity) and ignore liquidation preferences (substance). In a 2-3x exit, a 2x participating preferred matters far more than whether valuation was ₹100 crore or ₹120 crore.

**Rule 4: Get a good lawyer.** Not a general corporate lawyer — a startup-specialized one who sees these documents regularly. In India: AZB & Partners, Khaitan & Co, Trilegal, Shardul Amarchand Mangaldas all have startup practices. Budget ₹2-5 lakh for legal work on a seed/Series A round.

**Rule 5: Negotiate on principles, not line-items.** "Our goal is founder-friendly standard terms" is a cleaner negotiating position than going clause by clause.

## 7. The Difference Between Indian and US Term Sheet Practices

**Convertible instruments:** In the US, SAFEs are standard for seed rounds. In India, convertible notes are still more common (partly regulatory), though SAFEs are becoming more accepted.

**Liquidation preferences:** US seed deals are often 1x non-participating. Indian deals sometimes have 1x participating — worth pushing back on.

**Board dynamics:** US boards tend to be smaller and more founder-friendly at early stages. Indian boards can sometimes have more investor representation earlier.

**Protective provisions:** Often more extensive in Indian term sheets, reflecting earlier-stage investment in a less mature legal ecosystem.

**ESOP pool:** US term sheets typically include a pre-funding ESOP expansion (diluting founders, not new investors). Indian term sheets may structure this differently — read carefully.

---

## Summary

- A term sheet sets economic terms (valuation, liquidation preference, anti-dilution) and control terms (board, protective provisions)
- **Pre-money vs post-money:** understand the difference before you agree to a number
- **Liquidation preferences** matter more than valuation in moderate exits — push for 1x non-participating
- **Anti-dilution:** demand broad-based weighted average; reject full ratchet
- Control terms: maintain founder majority on the board through early stages; watch for over-broad protective provisions
- Create competition (multiple term sheets) before negotiating; hire a startup lawyer
