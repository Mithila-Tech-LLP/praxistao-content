# Chapter 36: Angel Investors — The First Money In

*Angel investors take the biggest risk, at the earliest stage, often on little more than belief in a founder. Understanding them is essential for every early-stage startup.*

---

## 1. What an Angel Investor Is

An angel investor is a wealthy individual who invests their own personal money into early-stage startups in exchange for equity.

The word "angel" originally came from Broadway theatre — wealthy patrons who funded shows that couldn't otherwise get made. The same idea: someone who backs a project when institutional money won't.

Key characteristics of angels:
- They invest **personal capital** (not a fund's money)
- They invest **early** (seed or pre-seed stage, before there's much to show)
- They typically invest **smaller amounts** (₹10 lakh to ₹2 crore per deal, though some "super angels" write ₹5-10 crore checks)
- They provide **less oversight** than VCs (no board seats in most cases)
- They are often **operators or former founders** who understand the founder's journey

In India, the angel ecosystem has grown dramatically since 2010, with Mumbai, Bangalore, and Delhi-NCR having the densest networks.

---

## 2. Why Angel Investors Invest

Understanding motivation helps you approach them correctly.

**Financial returns:** Angels expect most investments to fail. But the few that succeed can return 50x, 100x, even 1000x. A ₹25 lakh check in Flipkart's seed round would have returned hundreds of crores. The math works because of these home runs.

**Helping founders:** Many angels are former founders. They remember how hard it was to get the first investor and want to pay it forward. They derive genuine satisfaction from mentoring and helping build companies.

**Being in the game:** Angels stay connected to innovation, new ideas, and entrepreneurial energy. For someone who has exited their own company, angel investing is a way to stay relevant and active.

**Specific domain interest:** A healthcare executive who made money in pharma might angel-invest specifically in health startups — both for returns and for the intellectual engagement.

**The deal flow network:** Being known as an angel gets you into conversations with founders, VCs, and other investors. This network has its own value beyond any single investment.

---

## 3. What Angels Look For

**Team > Everything**

At the seed/pre-seed stage, there's barely a product. Certainly no track record. Angels are investing in the founders' ability to figure it out.

What they assess:
- **Domain expertise:** Does this founder understand the problem deeply?
- **Execution track record:** Have they done hard things before? Built something, shipped products, led teams?
- **Integrity and coachability:** Will they be honest about challenges? Will they take feedback well?
- **Resilience:** Can they handle the inevitable hardships?
- **Communication:** Can they articulate the idea clearly and compellingly?

**Market Size**

Even at the angel stage, investors want to know: if this works, how big can it get? A startup with a maximum possible outcome of ₹50 crore isn't interesting to angels who need 100x returns on portfolio to make their economics work.

"Large market" typically means: ₹1,000+ crore opportunity in India, or $1B+ globally.

**Unfair Advantage**

Why will this team succeed where others have failed or will fail? This could be:
- Unique domain expertise (the founder is a doctor building for doctors)
- Unique relationships (access to distribution that others don't have)
- Unique technology (genuinely differentiated approach)
- Unique insight (understanding the customer in a way others miss)

**Problem Clarity**

Can the founder articulate the problem they're solving clearly and specifically? "Improving healthcare" is not a problem. "Patients in tier-2 cities wait 3-4 weeks for specialist appointments because all specialists are in metro cities" is a problem.

## 4. How to Find Angel Investors in India

### Angel Networks

**Indian Angel Network (IAN):** One of India's oldest and largest angel networks. Members invest individually into deals brought to the network.

**Mumbai Angels:** Strong in Mumbai, focuses on early-stage tech, consumer, and fintech.

**Ah! Ventures:** Connects investors with startups across India.

**Let's Venture:** Online platform for angel and VC investments.

**AngelList India:** The Indian arm of the global platform.

**Calcutta Angels, Chennai Angels, etc.:** City-based networks in Tier-2 cities.

### Individual Super Angels in India

Notable Indian angels who write significant checks and are relatively accessible:
- **Anupam Mittal** (founder of Shaadi.com, Shark Tank India judge)
- **Rajan Anandan** (former Google India MD, partner at Peak XV now)
- **Peyush Bansal** (founder of Lenskart, Shark Tank India judge)
- **Aman Gupta** (co-founder of boAt, Shark Tank India judge)
- **Kunal Shah** (founder of CRED, active angel)
- **Ritesh Agarwal** (founder of OYO, invests in travel and hospitality)

### Warm Introductions (Most Effective)

Cold emails to angels have very low hit rates. Warm introductions — through mutual connections who can vouch for you — work far better.

Build your network before you need it:
- Attend startup events and conferences
- Join accelerator programs (you'll meet investors)
- Be active in startup communities online
- Connect with founders who have raised from angels and ask for introductions

### LinkedIn and Twitter

Direct outreach works when:
- Your message is personalized (you've researched their portfolio)
- You have a specific, compelling hook ("We've reached ₹20 lakh MRR in 4 months without any marketing")
- You're transparent about what you're looking for

## 5. The Angel Investment Process

### Step 1: Introduction and Initial Conversation

Usually 30-60 minutes. The angel is trying to understand the opportunity and assess the founder. This is not a formal pitch — it's a conversation.

Come prepared to explain: the problem, your solution, the market, traction so far, why you, and what you're raising.

### Step 2: The Pitch Meeting (If They're Interested)

More formal. You present your deck. The angel asks detailed questions. They're probing assumptions, assessing founder strength, and deciding whether they want to know more.

### Step 3: Due Diligence

Unlike VCs, angels do lighter diligence. They might:
- Talk to 2-3 of your customers ("Is the problem real? Is the solution valuable?")
- Talk to references who know you
- Review basic financials
- Ask for clarification on specific claims

### Step 4: Term Negotiation and Closing

Angels often invest through simple documents:
- **SAFE (Simple Agreement for Future Equity):** No valuation negotiation now; converts to equity at the next priced round
- **Convertible Note:** Like a SAFE but with an interest component
- **Priced Round:** Actual equity at a negotiated valuation

Closing typically takes 2-6 weeks once the angel is committed.

---

## 6. The Pitch Deck — What to Include

A standard 10-15 slide deck:

1. **Title:** Company name, one-line description, contact
2. **Problem:** Specific, painful, real. Use data or a story.
3. **Solution:** What you've built. How it solves the problem.
4. **Market Size:** TAM (Total Addressable Market), SAM (Serviceable), SOM (Your realistic target). Don't inflate. Be credible.
5. **Traction:** Users, revenue, growth rate, customer testimonials. The most important slide.
6. **Business Model:** How you make money. Clear unit economics if you have them.
7. **Go-To-Market:** How you acquire customers. Which channel are you betting on?
8. **Competition:** Honest assessment. Why will you win?
9. **Team:** Why you? What makes you the right people?
10. **Financials:** 18-month projection (revenue, expenses, headcount). Not Excel gymnastics — a credible plan.
11. **The Ask:** How much you're raising. What you'll use it for. What you'll achieve with it.

**The most common deck mistakes:**
- Too many slides (>15 is too many)
- Market size slides with ridiculous numbers ("Global healthcare market is $10 trillion")
- No traction data (investors want to see some proof)
- No clear "why us" — founders who don't explain their unfair advantage leave investors wondering

## 7. How Much to Raise and At What Valuation

**How much to raise:**
Think backward from milestones: "What do I need to accomplish to raise my next round?" Then: "How much capital does that require, plus 20% buffer?"

Typical seed raises in India: ₹50 lakh to ₹3 crore (pre-Series A bootstrapped companies) or $500K to $2M (if raising in USD terms for international angels/seed funds).

**Pre-seed:** ₹25 lakh to ₹1 crore, for validation and MVP
**Seed:** ₹1-5 crore, for proving initial traction

**Valuation:**
Early-stage valuation is more art than science. References:
- Pre-product, strong team in a large market: ₹10-20 crore pre-money valuation
- Product launched, some users: ₹20-40 crore
- Revenue, early traction: ₹40-100 crore+

Don't obsess over valuation at seed stage. Dilution matters less than getting the right investors and enough capital to prove your thesis.

---

## 8. SAFEs and Convertible Notes

Most angel rounds in India use SAFEs (Simple Agreement for Future Equity) or convertible notes to avoid the complexity and cost of a priced round.

**SAFE:** Y Combinator's creation. The angel invests ₹50 lakh today. No valuation negotiation. At the next priced round, the investment converts to equity at a discounted price (typically 20% discount from the next round's price). Fast, simple, cheap.

**Convertible Note:** Similar, but structured as a loan that converts to equity. Includes an interest rate (typically 8-10%). The interest is usually not paid in cash but rather accrues and converts along with the principal.

**Valuation Cap:** Both SAFEs and notes often have a "valuation cap" — if the next round values the company at ₹200 crore but the cap is ₹100 crore, the angel's investment converts at the ₹100 crore valuation, giving them a better price (more equity).

---

## 9. Managing Angel Relationships Post-Investment

Angels can be enormously valuable beyond their check — if you engage them correctly.

**What angels can provide:**
- Introductions to VCs for your next round
- Introductions to potential customers and partners
- Advice based on their domain expertise
- Credibility ("If X invested, this team is credible")

**How to keep angels engaged:**
- Send monthly or quarterly updates (brief, honest, data-driven)
- Tell them when you need specific help (not vague "stay in touch")
- Be transparent about challenges — they can help if they know
- Celebrate wins with them — they want to feel part of the journey

---

## Summary

- Angel investors invest personal capital into early-stage startups in exchange for equity
- They invest primarily on **team quality** — at the seed stage, there's nothing else to invest on
- Find angels through warm introductions, angel networks, and direct outreach (with a strong hook)
- The process: intro conversation → pitch → light due diligence → SAFE or convertible note → close
- Raise the amount you need to reach your next milestone, not as much as possible
- SAFEs are the fastest, simplest instrument for angel rounds; understand the valuation cap
