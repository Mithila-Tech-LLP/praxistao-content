# Chapter 06: Operations — How Things Get Made and Delivered

---

## Before We Start — The Part of Business Nobody Glamorizes

Everyone wants to talk about strategy. Fundraising. Marketing. The big pitch deck. The billion-dollar idea.

But here is the uncomfortable truth: most businesses don't fail because of bad strategy. They fail because they couldn't actually execute — they couldn't make the thing, get it to the customer, and do it again a million times without falling apart.

Operations is the engine room. It is not glamorous. There are no headlines about a warehouse manager who optimized a picking route and saved a company 18 crores. But without that person, the company might have died.

This chapter is about how businesses actually work — how raw materials turn into finished products, how those products get to your doorstep in 10 minutes, how McDonald's makes the same burger in Pune and Paris, and why Walmart can sell a product cheaper than the factory that made it.

By the end of this chapter, you'll look at a package arriving at your door and see a machine with dozens of invisible moving parts working behind it.

---

## 1. What Operations Actually Means — Turning Inputs Into Outputs

Here is the simplest way to understand operations: it is the process of taking **inputs** and turning them into **outputs** that customers want.

```
INPUTS → [Operations Process] → OUTPUTS

Raw materials + Labor + Energy + Equipment + Information
→ [Organize, Transform, Deliver]
→ Product or Service the customer receives
```

Every business has this at its core, no matter what it does.

- **Apple** takes silicon, aluminum, glass, software code, and thousands of labor hours → turns them into an iPhone you pay 80,000 rupees for.
- **Zomato** takes a restaurant's food, a delivery partner's time, and a smartphone app → turns them into biryani on your desk in 30 minutes.
- **Netflix** takes video content licenses, server infrastructure, and software → turns them into a streaming experience on your TV at 11 PM.
- **Zepto** takes a warehouse full of groceries and a city map → turns them into a bag of chips and atta at your door in 10 minutes.

The *operation* is everything between "we have the raw ingredients" and "the customer got what they paid for."

### Why Operations Is Harder Than It Looks

The challenge is not doing something once. Anyone can deliver one order of biryani. The challenge is doing it a million times, consistently, cheaply, and without mistakes.

Think of it like this: making one great sandwich for your friend is easy. Opening a sandwich shop and making 300 sandwiches a day, each identical, each fresh, each delivered to the right table, while managing the bread supplier, the refrigerator, the staff roster, and the customer queue — that is operations.

**What This Means in Practice:**
When Domino's promises a pizza in 30 minutes, that promise is backed by an entire operational system: standardized dough preparation (no improvising), pre-portioned toppings (no guesswork), a store layout designed to minimize walking steps, delivery radius calculations, and real-time GPS tracking. Domino's is not just a pizza company. It is a logistics and operations company that happens to sell pizza.

## 2. Supply Chain — From Raw Material to Customer's Hands

"Supply chain" is a term that used to be invisible to most people — until COVID hit in 2020 and suddenly everyone learned what it meant when theirs broke.

A **supply chain** is the entire journey a product takes from raw material to the end customer. Every link in that chain is a separate operation, often handled by different companies.

### The Full Journey — A Cotton T-Shirt

Let's trace a basic cotton t-shirt from field to wardrobe:

| Stage | Who Does It | What Happens |
|---|---|---|
| Raw Material | Cotton farmer in Vidarbha | Cotton is grown and picked |
| Processing | Spinning mill in Coimbatore | Cotton is cleaned, spun into yarn |
| Weaving | Textile factory in Tirupur | Yarn is woven into fabric |
| Cut & Sew | Garment factory (often Tirupur or Bangladesh) | Fabric is cut, stitched into a t-shirt |
| Quality Check | Factory QC team | Defective pieces are removed |
| Packaging | Packaging unit | Folded, tagged, bagged |
| Warehousing | Brand's distribution center | Stock held before orders |
| Logistics | Delivery partner (e.g., Delhivery) | Shipped to retail store or customer |
| Last Mile | Local delivery person | Delivered to your door |

Each step adds cost. Each step adds time. Each step is a potential point of failure.

When you buy a t-shirt from H&M for 1,500 rupees, you are paying for the entire chain above — and H&M's margin on top.

### Procurement — Getting What You Need to Make the Thing

**Procurement** is the process of buying the inputs your business needs. This sounds simple. It is not.

A company like Apple spends enormous energy on procurement because the cost, quality, and availability of components directly determines whether they can make iPhones at all.

Apple has suppliers in dozens of countries. Some components — like the screen — come from Samsung (yes, Apple's biggest competitor makes their screens). Others come from small specialized factories in Japan for glass, Taiwan for chips, South Korea for memory.

When one supplier has a problem — a factory fire, a port strike, a natural disaster — it shuts down Apple's production line. This is why big companies obsessively manage **supplier relationships** and always want at least two suppliers for anything critical.

For a startup in India, procurement might just be "where do we buy flour for our bakery at the right price?" But the principle is the same: your inputs are the foundation of everything.

### Manufacturing — Making the Thing

Once you have inputs, you need to transform them. This transformation is manufacturing or production.

Manufacturing decisions include:

- **Make or buy?** Do you manufacture yourself, or do you outsource to a contract manufacturer? Apple designs iPhones but Foxconn in China assembles them.
- **Batch production vs. continuous production?** A bakery makes batches (100 loaves at a time). A refinery runs continuously (24/7).
- **Automation vs. manual?** More automation = lower variable cost but higher fixed cost. Manual labor is flexible but expensive at scale.

### Logistics — Moving the Thing

Making the product is only half the battle. Getting it where it needs to go — that is logistics.

**Amazon's logistics empire** is one of the greatest operational achievements in business history. Amazon built its own:
- Warehouses (called fulfillment centers) positioned within 150 km of most major cities
- Delivery fleet (Amazon Logistics) to stop depending on FedEx and UPS
- Air cargo network (Amazon Air) for overnight delivery
- Last-mile delivery network with drivers who own their routes

Why did Amazon build all of this instead of just using existing courier companies? Because they couldn't rely on outside companies to meet the 1-day and 2-day delivery promises that became their core value proposition. When you compete on speed, you have to own speed.

In India, Flipkart made a similar call. They built Ekart — their own logistics arm — because third-party couriers couldn't handle Flipkart-scale volume or meet Flipkart's delivery standards. Today, Ekart handles over a million shipments a day.

### Last-Mile Delivery — The Hardest, Most Expensive Part

"Last mile" is the final step: getting the product from a local hub to your specific door. It sounds easy. It is the hardest part of the whole chain.

Here is why: the further you spread out deliveries, the more inefficient each one becomes. A truck can efficiently deliver 100 packages to 100 businesses in a business district. But delivering 100 packages to 100 apartments across a city — each at different times, with each person home or not home — is brutally expensive.

Last-mile delivery costs typically account for **53% of total shipping costs** even though it is the shortest distance.

This is why Zomato and Swiggy's economics are so hard. Every delivery within an 8 km radius uses a bike, a delivery partner's time, and a logistics app — and the economics only work if the basket size is high enough or the delivery fee covers the cost. When you order one cup of chai and pay 15 rupees delivery fee, Zomato is probably losing money on that delivery.

## 3. Inventory Management — The Science of Not Running Out (or Having Too Much)

Inventory is the stuff you have sitting around waiting to be used or sold. Sounds simple. Managing it well is a science.

The core tension in inventory management is this:

- **Too little inventory** = you run out of stock, customers can't buy, you lose revenue, customers get angry and go elsewhere.
- **Too much inventory** = you tie up cash in products just sitting on shelves, things expire or go out of style, you pay for warehouse space.

Both are expensive in different ways.

### Just-in-Time (JIT) — Toyota's Revolution

In the 1970s, Toyota figured out a radically different way to manage inventory. Instead of keeping huge warehouses full of parts, they would receive parts from suppliers **just as they were needed** on the assembly line — minutes or hours before they would be used, not weeks.

This is called **Just-in-Time manufacturing**, and it changed the auto industry forever.

**The benefits of JIT:**
- Almost no money tied up in sitting inventory
- Problems are spotted immediately (if a bad part arrives, you catch it right away)
- Flexibility to change what you're making
- Much lower warehouse costs

**The risk of JIT:**
- Any disruption in the supply chain stops production completely
- You have zero buffer if a supplier has a problem

COVID showed the world the dark side of JIT. Car manufacturers had slimmed down to almost zero chip inventory. When chip factories were disrupted by lockdowns, car production stopped. Globally, car makers lost over $200 billion in production in 2021 because they didn't have enough semiconductor chips.

### Buffer Stock — The Old-Fashioned Safety Net

The traditional approach is to keep a **buffer stock** — a reserve of inventory that you hold just in case things go wrong.

If your daily sales are 100 units and your supplier takes 7 days to deliver, you might hold 30 days of buffer stock. You order new stock when you hit 10 days of supply remaining.

This is safer but expensive. Every rupee sitting in a warehouse is a rupee not invested in growth.

The right answer is always context-dependent:
- High-value, long shelf life items: lean inventory makes sense
- Perishables: you have no choice but to minimize stock
- Critical items with unreliable suppliers: hold buffer

### What Zepto Does Differently — 10-Minute Delivery Is an Operations Problem

When Zepto promises 10-minute grocery delivery, they are not doing magic. They have re-engineered inventory management entirely.

Traditional supermarkets: one big store serves a 5 km radius. Everything is there, but it takes 30 minutes to drive there, shop, and return.

Zepto's model: **dark stores** — small 2,000 sq ft warehouses positioned every 2-3 km across a city, stocked with the ~5,000 most commonly ordered items. There are no customers walking around, no checkout queues. Just pickers, shelves, and bikes.

The 10-minute delivery works because:
1. You are never more than 2-3 km from a dark store
2. The picker gets the order in 2-3 minutes (small store = short walking distance)
3. A bike reaches you in 5-7 minutes
4. Done

But the inventory challenge is intense. Zepto needs to predict exactly how much milk, atta, chips, and eggs each dark store needs each day — and they get it wrong sometimes. Run out of milk at 10 PM and customers use Blinkit instead. Order too much and food expires.

This is why Zepto and Blinkit invest heavily in demand forecasting algorithms — software that looks at past orders, time of day, weather, festivals, and local patterns to predict what each store needs to stock every 4-6 hours.

## 4. Quality Control — Making Sure Your Product Doesn't Suck

Quality control (QC) is the process of checking that what you made is actually good enough to give to a customer.

This matters for two reasons:
1. **Direct cost**: a defective product that reaches a customer costs 10x more to fix than one caught at the factory (you pay for the return, the replacement, the customer service call, and the damage to trust).
2. **Brand reputation**: one bad product can undo years of brand building.

### Quality Control Approaches

**Inspect everything (100% inspection):** Check every single unit. Used for high-value, high-stakes products — aircraft parts, medical devices, surgical instruments. Expensive but necessary when failure is catastrophic.

**Statistical sampling:** Check a random sample. If you're making 10 million biscuits a day, you can't check every one. Instead, you pull random samples and test them. If the sample passes, you release the batch. Used by most consumer goods companies.

**Build quality into the process (Total Quality Management):** Instead of checking at the end, you design the process so defects can't happen. Toyota calls this "poka-yoke" — mistake-proofing. Example: designing a USB plug that can only go in one way, so the customer can never insert it incorrectly.

**What This Means in Practice:**
Apple's iPhone manufacturing involves extraordinarily tight tolerances. The aluminum body of a MacBook is machined from a single block of aluminum (no screws, no seams). Apple inspects each body for the tiniest surface imperfections. The rejected MacBook bodies — machined from 1 kg of aluminum and worth several thousand rupees in material — are thrown away because they don't meet visual quality standards. This costs Apple a lot. It is also exactly why customers feel that Apple products feel premium.

## 5. Standard Operating Procedures — How Franchises Work

Here is a question: how does McDonald's in Bangalore make a burger that tastes exactly like McDonald's in Vancouver, Canada?

The answer is **Standard Operating Procedures** (SOPs) — written instructions that tell people exactly how to do every single task, in exactly the right way, every single time.

McDonald's does not just teach people to "make a burger." They specify:
- Exactly how many grams of beef in a patty
- The exact temperature to cook it at
- Exactly how many seconds to cook each side
- The exact sequence of adding condiments
- How to fold the wrapper
- How long a cooked burger can sit before it must be discarded (10 minutes)

Everything is written down, tested, and standardized. This is why a 17-year-old with three days of training can make a McDonald's burger that tastes the same as one made by a 10-year employee.

### Why SOPs Are the Foundation of Scale

Think about what SOPs actually do:

**Without SOPs:** Every outcome depends on the individual person. If your best chef is sick, food quality drops. If your best delivery person leaves, delivery times get worse. The business is held hostage by individual talent.

**With SOPs:** The process holds the knowledge, not the person. You can train new people quickly. You can open 1,000 locations. You can scale without quality degrading.

This is why **franchise businesses** are built entirely on SOPs. When you buy a Domino's franchise in Jaipur, you are not buying the right to make pizza your own way. You are buying access to Domino's systems, processes, recipes, supply chain, and brand. The SOP is the franchise.

Starbucks has SOPs for every drink — how many pumps of syrup, how long to steam milk, the exact temperature of the espresso shot. A barista in Mumbai and a barista in New York follow the same steps. That consistency is the product.

### SOPs Beyond Restaurants

SOPs exist in every type of business:

- **Hospitals**: surgical checklists that must be completed before every operation (inspired by aviation checklists, they've reduced surgical errors dramatically)
- **Banks**: loan approval checklists that ensure every application is evaluated with the same criteria
- **Software companies**: deployment checklists so code releases don't break the product
- **Customer support**: scripts and decision trees so every customer gets consistent help

A good SOP is clear enough that a new employee can follow it without asking questions. A great SOP is designed so the most common mistakes become impossible.

## 6. Technology in Operations — ERPs, Warehouses, Routes

Modern operations run on software. Before software, managing a factory or supply chain meant enormous amounts of paperwork, human error, and time delays. Today, software makes operations vastly faster, smarter, and cheaper.

### ERP — Enterprise Resource Planning

An **ERP** is a single software system that connects all parts of a business's operations: procurement, inventory, manufacturing, sales, HR, and finance — all in one place, all updating in real time.

Imagine Tata Motors. They have suppliers across dozens of countries, factories in India and abroad, dealers across 140+ countries, and thousands of employees. Without an ERP, the left hand literally doesn't know what the right hand is doing. The factory might be making cars that the market doesn't want, while the cars the market wants are out of stock.

With an ERP (Tata uses SAP, which is the world's largest ERP company):
- When a dealer in Chennai sells a car, that sale instantly reduces finished goods inventory
- The production system sees the inventory drop and can schedule new production
- The procurement system sees components being consumed and triggers reorders
- Finance sees the revenue and the cost of goods simultaneously

The whole company operates from one version of reality.

Major ERPs: SAP, Oracle, Microsoft Dynamics. In India, Tally is widely used by SMEs.

### Warehouse Management Systems (WMS)

A **Warehouse Management System** tells workers in a warehouse where every item is, in real time, and gives them the most efficient path to pick orders.

Amazon's warehouses (fulfillment centers) use robots and WMS together. The robots bring shelves of products to human workers, who never have to walk. The WMS tracks exactly which shelf has which product and calculates the fastest path.

Without a WMS, a warehouse worker looks for the product themselves. With a WMS, they get turn-by-turn instructions on a handheld scanner, like a GPS for the warehouse.

### Route Optimization

**Route optimization** is software that calculates the most efficient route for delivery drivers to reach all their destinations.

Zomato's dispatch algorithm has to solve thousands of micro-decisions every second:
- Which delivery partner is closest to the restaurant?
- Can this partner pick up two orders from nearby restaurants and deliver both efficiently?
- What is the fastest route given current traffic?

Poorly optimized routes are money burning on the road. A Zomato delivery partner doing 15 deliveries a day instead of 10 — because the algorithm is better — means 50% more revenue from the same human, the same bike, the same salary.

Google Maps, Ola, and Uber all use route optimization at scale. Uber matches drivers to riders using algorithms that solve the same underlying problem: minimizing the total time for all trips across a city.

## 7. Outsourcing vs. In-Sourcing — What to Control and What to Give Away

Every business has to decide: which activities should we do ourselves, and which should we pay someone else to do?

**Outsourcing** = paying an outside company to handle a function.
**In-sourcing** = doing it yourself, internally.

### The Classic Framework

Ask two questions about every business activity:

1. **Is this a core competency?** Does doing this well give us a competitive advantage?
2. **Can an outside specialist do it better or cheaper?**

```
                    YES - Better/Cheaper Outside    NO - Can't Do It Better
Core Competency     Agonizing tradeoff (rare)       Keep it, own it fully
Not Core            Outsource clearly               Still outsource, save focus
```

**What This Means in Practice:**

**Apple** outsources manufacturing (Foxconn makes the iPhones) but keeps chip design in-house (Apple Silicon is now one of their biggest advantages). Why? Making chips well is a core competency — it directly makes their product faster and more efficient. Manufacturing is not where Apple competes.

**Flipkart** in-sourced logistics (Ekart) because delivery speed was their core battleground. They couldn't outsource their competitive advantage.

**Zomato** outsources restaurant kitchens (they don't cook the food) but in-sources delivery (their own delivery fleet) because delivery reliability is their promise to customers.

### The Risks of Outsourcing

When you outsource, you gain efficiency but lose control. This creates risk:

- **Quality risk**: The outsourced partner may not maintain your quality standards.
- **Dependency risk**: If your outsourced partner raises prices or exits the business, you're stuck.
- **IP risk**: Sharing your processes and products with outsiders can lead to copies.
- **Accountability risk**: When something goes wrong, the customer blames you — not your third-party partner.

Apple's dependence on Foxconn became a PR problem in 2010 when a series of worker suicides at Foxconn's Shenzhen factory made headlines. Apple could say "that's Foxconn's problem, not ours" — but no customer accepted that. The brand owns the outsourcing decision.

## 8. Operational Excellence — How Walmart and Amazon Beat Everyone on Cost

**Operational excellence** means running your operations so well — so efficiently, with so few errors, at such scale — that your cost per unit is lower than any competitor can match.

This is how Walmart and Amazon compete. Not by having unique products. Not by the best marketing. By running the most efficient machine in history at scale.

### Walmart's Operations Story

Walmart's entire business model is: "We will have the same products as everyone else, but we will get them to our stores cheaper than anyone else, so we can sell them cheaper."

To do that, Walmart built:

- **Cross-docking warehouses**: Products don't sit in Walmart's warehouses. They arrive on one truck, get immediately transferred to outgoing trucks, and go directly to stores. Zero storage time. Maximum speed.
- **Retail Link**: A technology system that shared real-time sales data with every Walmart supplier. When Walmart sold a box of Colgate in Kansas, Colgate knew about it instantly and could plan production accordingly. This was revolutionary in the 1980s.
- **Buying power**: Walmart is so large that it can negotiate prices that no small retailer can match. They tell Procter & Gamble: "We want Tide detergent at this price or we stop carrying it." P&G usually complies, because losing Walmart would be catastrophic.
- **Location strategy**: Walmart built stores in small towns and suburbs where land was cheap, not expensive city centers.

The result: Walmart could undercut any local store on price and still make a profit.

### Amazon's Cost Obsession

Amazon's founder Jeff Bezos had a famous chart in early board meetings: a flywheel.

```
Lower prices → More customers → More sellers → More selection → Lower prices
       ↑                                                              ↓
Better customer experience ←←←←←←←←←←←←←←←←←←←←←←←←←←←←←←←←←←←
```

The flywheel spins faster as operations improve. Lower operational costs → lower prices → more customers → scale → even lower costs.

Amazon Web Services (AWS) started because Amazon built data center infrastructure for its own operations so well that it had spare capacity it could rent to others. Today AWS makes more profit than Amazon's retail business. Operational excellence spawned an entirely new business.

## 9. Operations for a Service Business vs. a Physical Product Business

Everything we've discussed so far leans toward physical products — things you make, store, and ship. But the majority of businesses today, especially in tech, sell services. How is operations different?

### Physical Product Operations

- Inventory exists (you can touch it)
- Quality can be inspected before delivery
- Defects can be caught before the customer sees them
- Scale requires physical capacity (more factories, more warehouses)
- Lead time to expand can be months or years

### Service Business Operations

- No physical inventory (you can't pre-make a haircut and store it)
- Quality happens during delivery — the customer experiences production in real time
- You can't inspect a service before delivering it; you can only train the people who deliver it
- Scale often requires people (or software)
- Software services can scale to millions of users without building factories

**What This Means in Practice — Stripe vs. a Factory**

Stripe processes online payments. Their "product" is an API — a piece of software. When a new customer signs up, Stripe doesn't make anything new. The software just handles one more user.

This means:
- Near-zero marginal cost per additional customer
- No inventory
- No shipping
- No physical quality defects

But they have their own operations challenges:
- **Uptime**: If Stripe's systems go down for one hour, thousands of businesses can't accept payments. Operations for Stripe means maintaining 99.99% uptime — less than 53 minutes of downtime per year.
- **Fraud detection**: Stripe needs to detect fraudulent transactions in milliseconds, at a rate of millions per day.
- **Compliance**: Every country has different payment regulations. "Operations" for Stripe's legal team means staying compliant in 40+ countries simultaneously.

**Service quality is people quality.** In a product company, a machine does the work consistently. In a service company, people do the work — and people are inconsistent. This is why service companies invest heavily in training, SOPs, and culture. A Taj Hotel butler's "product" is attention and service. You can't standardize warmth — but you can create conditions where warm, attentive service is the norm.

## Summary — What We Covered

Operations is how businesses actually make and deliver what they promise. After this chapter, here is what you now understand:

| Concept | What It Means | Real Example |
|---|---|---|
| Operations | Turning inputs into outputs, repeatedly, reliably | Apple turning aluminum into iPhones |
| Supply Chain | The full journey from raw material to customer | Cotton → Yarn → Fabric → T-shirt → Your wardrobe |
| Procurement | Buying the inputs you need | Apple buying chips from TSMC in Taiwan |
| Last-Mile Delivery | The final, hardest, most expensive step | Zomato delivery partner at your door |
| Just-in-Time | Receiving inventory exactly when needed | Toyota's assembly line system |
| Inventory Management | The balance between too much and too little stock | Zepto's dark store model |
| Quality Control | Ensuring your product meets standards | Apple's machined aluminum bodies |
| SOPs | Written instructions so anyone can do it right | McDonald's burger assembly procedure |
| ERP | Software that connects all business operations | SAP at Tata Motors |
| Route Optimization | Algorithms that minimize delivery time and cost | Zomato's dispatch algorithm |
| Outsourcing | Paying others to do non-core activities | Apple using Foxconn for assembly |
| Operational Excellence | Running so efficiently that costs are unbeatable | Walmart's cross-docking warehouses |

**The big lesson:** Strategy tells you what to do. Operations is actually doing it. The gap between strategy and operations is where most businesses die.

## Exercises and Discussion Questions

1. **Trace the supply chain of your last online order.** Pick something you bought recently — a book, a phone case, a packet of food. Try to map every step from raw material to your hands. Where do you think the most value is added? Where is the most cost incurred?

2. **Zepto vs. a neighborhood kirana store.** Both sell groceries. Compare their operations across: inventory management, supplier relationships, quality control, and delivery. Why can Zepto deliver in 10 minutes? Why can a kirana survive despite being slower? Is there anything a kirana does operationally that Zepto cannot?

3. **SOP design challenge.** Pick a simple task you do regularly — making chai, packing a bag, cleaning your room. Write an SOP for it that someone who has never seen your home or kitchen could follow and get the exact same result as you. What did you find hard to standardize?

4. **Outsource or in-source?** You are building a food delivery startup. For each function below, decide whether to outsource or in-source, and explain why:
   - Restaurant quality checks
   - Delivery drivers
   - Software development
   - Customer support
   - Payment processing
   - Marketing

5. **The JIT lesson from COVID.** Research one specific industry that suffered from JIT supply chain breakdowns during COVID (automobiles, semiconductors, and pharmaceuticals are good starting points). What happened? What should they have done differently? What are companies doing now?

6. **Walmart vs. a local kirana chain.** Walmart competes purely on operational efficiency and price. A local chain of grocery stores cannot match Walmart's prices. How should the local chain compete? What operational advantages does a local business have that a giant chain cannot replicate?

7. **Service operations design.** Pick a service business you use regularly (a salon, a doctor's clinic, a coaching class, a gym). Map their operations: What are the inputs? What is the process? What are the outputs? Where are the biggest operational bottlenecks? What would you change?
