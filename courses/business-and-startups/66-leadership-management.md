# Chapter 66: Leadership and Management — How to Build and Run Organizations

*At some point, every founder or senior professional needs to shift from doing to leading. This transition — from individual contributor to manager to leader — is one of the hardest in business. This chapter is a practical guide.*

---

## Leadership vs Management — The Real Difference

**Management** is about execution: getting things done through others. Setting goals, allocating resources, measuring performance, removing obstacles.

**Leadership** is about direction and motivation: determining what to do and why, setting culture, and inspiring people to give their best.

Good managers without good leadership execute the wrong things efficiently.

Good leaders without good management have inspiring vision and chaotic execution.

Great founders need both — and the balance required shifts as the company grows.

**Andy Grove (Intel CEO), High Output Management:**
"A manager's output = output of their organization + output of the organizations they influence."

You are not paid for what you personally produce. You are paid for what your team produces.

---

## The Manager's Core Job

**1. Clarity of goals:**
Every person on your team should be able to answer: What am I supposed to accomplish this week? This quarter? How does my work connect to the company's goals?

If they can't answer clearly, the manager has failed.

**OKRs (Objectives and Key Results):** Invented at Intel by Andy Grove, popularized at Google by John Doerr. Three-level structure:
- Company OKRs: What is the company trying to achieve this quarter?
- Team OKRs: What does this team need to do to contribute to company goals?
- Individual OKRs: What does each person need to do?

The power of OKRs: alignment. When the CEO and the junior engineer can both articulate how their work connects, the company moves in the same direction.

**2. Context setting (not micromanagement):**
High-performance management is about providing maximum context with minimum control.

"Here's why we're doing this, here's what success looks like, here's the constraints — now figure out the best way to do it."

This is different from: "Here's exactly how to do it, check in every hour."

Netflix calls this "context, not control." You hire smart people, give them what they need to know, and let them make decisions.

**3. Removing obstacles:**
A manager's most important job is removing blockers for their team. If a team member is stuck on a dependency from another team, the manager should resolve it. If the team needs a tool they don't have, the manager should get it.

Think of yourself as a servant to your team, not a supervisor.

## The Performance Management System

**Setting expectations:** Before evaluating performance, make sure expectations are crystal clear. "Good job" and "needs improvement" are meaningless without specific criteria.

**Regular 1:1 meetings:** Weekly or biweekly one-on-ones with each direct report. This is not a status update meeting. It's a coaching conversation:
- What's going well? What's hard?
- What obstacles do you need me to remove?
- What are you learning? What do you want to develop?
- What feedback do you have for me?

**The SBI feedback model:**
- **Situation:** When this happened...
- **Behavior:** You did/said...
- **Impact:** The effect was...

"When you interrupted Priya in yesterday's meeting, the team saw her idea dismissed without consideration. This affects team psychological safety."

This is better than "You need to be more respectful."

**Performance improvement:** If someone is underperforming, the sequence:
1. Have an honest conversation: here's what I'm observing, here's the impact
2. Agree on specific changes needed and timeline
3. Provide support (training, coaching, removing obstacles)
4. Evaluate whether improvement is happening
5. If not improving: off-board with dignity

Most managers avoid step 1 for too long. Early honest feedback is kinder than letting someone continue in a role they're failing at.

## Building High-Performance Culture

**Culture is what people do when no one is watching.** Not the values on the wall — the decisions people make at 2 AM when they're tired and stressed.

**The Netflix Culture Deck** (written by Patty McCord and Reed Hastings) is the most influential document on startup culture. Key ideas:
- "We want a team, not a family." Teams have performance standards; families don't.
- Hire only people who are exceptional at their role. Don't keep "adequate" performers — they degrade the team.
- Generous with severance. Fire people thoughtfully — they worked hard, they're just not the right fit.
- Give context, not instructions.
- Unlimited vacation (with manager's judgment). Don't track hours; track results.

**Amazon's Leadership Principles:** Amazon's 16 leadership principles (Customer Obsession, Ownership, Invent and Simplify, etc.) are used in hiring, performance evaluation, and decision-making. They make culture explicit rather than implicit.

**The key insight:** Culture is not what you say — it's what you reward and what you tolerate. If you say "customer obsession" but reward internal metrics achievement over customer satisfaction, the real culture is internal metrics.

## Delegation — The Hardest Manager Skill

Most new managers don't delegate enough. They do work that their team should do.

**Why it's hard:**
- You're faster at certain things than your new team member (in the short term)
- You care more about quality than they do (for now)
- It's uncomfortable watching someone do something worse than you would do it
- You're not sure how much to explain vs. let them figure it out

**Why you must delegate:**
- Your time is the scarcest resource. Using it on tasks your team can do means not using it on things only you can do.
- Your team won't grow without doing hard things independently.
- If you're the bottleneck, the team is limited to your personal throughput.

**The delegation framework:**
- **Level 1:** Do exactly this (no decisions)
- **Level 2:** Explore options and recommend one, I'll decide
- **Level 3:** Explore options, decide, and tell me what you're doing
- **Level 4:** Decide and execute, update me if I need to know

Most delegation should be at Level 3-4 after the person demonstrates competence. Most new managers delegate at Level 1-2 when Level 3-4 is appropriate.

## Hiring — The Most Important Manager Decision

**The principle:** Hire slow, fire fast. The cost of a wrong hire is enormous.

**The wrong hire costs:**
- 6-12 months of salary before you admit it's not working
- Team morale damage from watching a poor performer stay
- Work not getting done or getting done badly
- The departed employee's regret and lost career time

**What to look for:**
- **Capability:** Can they do the job? (Demonstrated by past work, not just how they interview)
- **Character:** Will they do it well and honestly? (References, behavioral questions)
- **Cultural add:** Will they make the team better? (Not just "fit" — that can mean "similar to existing team")

**The reference check:** The most underused hiring tool. Don't take references the candidate gave you. Find people who worked with them via LinkedIn and ask directly: "Would you hire this person again? What was their biggest strength? What's one area they should develop?"

**Jeff Bezos's "1,000 employees" standard:** When hiring, ask "Will I be proud if this person becomes 1,000 employees?" Because in fast-growing companies, the people you hire today become the norm-setters for those who come later.

## Managing Up — Working with Your Manager

Much of management advice focuses on managing downward. But "managing up" — effectively influencing your manager and peers — is equally important.

**What managing up means:**
- Giving your manager what they need to succeed: information, early warnings, recommendations
- Understanding your manager's priorities and framing your work in terms of their goals
- Proactively communicating: don't make your manager ask for updates
- Disagreeing with your manager productively: raise issues directly, with data and alternative suggestions

**The manager's worst nightmare:** Surprises. Bad news delivered late. A project that was "on track" until it wasn't. The manager found out about a problem from their manager.

**The cadence:** Weekly updates, structured as: what we accomplished, what we're working on, what's blocking us. This keeps managers informed without requiring constant check-ins.

## Leadership at Larger Scale

As organizations grow, leadership changes:

**Small team (2-15 people):** Leader knows everyone personally, involved in most decisions, culture is informal

**Medium team (15-100 people):** Leader can no longer know everyone. Culture must be codified (values, principles, processes). Management layer emerges.

**Large organization (100+):** Leader primarily manages other leaders. Cultural transmission is now through systems, not direct interaction.

The transition from small to medium is the hardest. Many founders fail here — they keep managing like a small team when the organization needs structure.

**The Andy Grove Principle:** "When the company doubles in size, everything that worked before needs to be reexamined." The systems and processes that work for 20 people don't work for 100 people.

---

## Key Lessons

1. **Your output is your team's output** — focus on making them excellent, not on doing the work yourself
2. **Clarity is the manager's primary tool** — if people don't know what success looks like, they can't succeed
3. **Culture is what you reward and tolerate** — words on walls mean nothing
4. **Hire slow, fire fast** — the quality of your team determines everything
5. **Delegate more than feels comfortable** — if you're the bottleneck, the team can't scale

---

## Summary

- Management is execution; leadership is direction. Companies need both.
- The manager's job: clear goals, context-setting, obstacle removal, coaching
- Netflix (culture deck) and Amazon (leadership principles) built cultures that scale through explicit values
- Delegation is the most underpracticed management skill
- Hiring is the highest-leverage decision a manager makes — invest in it accordingly

## Exercises

1. **Write your team's OKRs** for next quarter. Do they align to the company's goals? Can everyone articulate the connection?
2. **Do a 360 feedback session** on yourself as a manager. Ask 3-5 team members: what should I keep doing, what should I stop doing, what should I start doing?
3. **Read the Netflix Culture Deck** (freely available online). Which principles would you adopt? Which would you push back on?
4. **Practice the SBI feedback model.** Write out a piece of feedback you need to give using the Situation-Behavior-Impact structure.
5. **Audit your delegation.** For each task you did last week, ask: could someone on my team have done this? Why didn't I delegate it?
