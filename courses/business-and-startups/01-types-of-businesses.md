# Chapter 01: Types of Businesses — Sole Proprietorship, Partnership, LLC, Corporation

> "The legal structure you choose on day one quietly shapes everything that follows — who can sue you, who owns your company, how much tax you pay, and whether investors will even talk to you."

---

## 1. Why the Legal Structure of a Business Matters Enormously

Let's say you start a food delivery app tomorrow. You have an idea, you build a rough prototype, and you get your first 50 users. Feels exciting, right? But here's the question nobody asks at this stage: *What kind of legal entity is this thing?*

Most people skip this question entirely. They just "start doing business." And for a while, that works fine. But then something goes wrong — a customer sues you because they got food poisoning, or a co-founder quits and claims they own 50% of the company, or an investor says "I'd love to put in money but you have no legal structure" — and suddenly the question you ignored comes back to bite you, hard.

The legal structure of a business is essentially the **skeleton** of everything that comes after. It determines:

- **Liability** — If your business owes money or gets sued, can they come after your personal savings, your parents' house, your car? Or is the company a separate legal entity that takes the hit?
- **Taxes** — Different structures are taxed very differently. A sole proprietor pays personal income tax on all profits. A corporation pays corporate tax. The difference can be lakhs of rupees per year.
- **Ownership and control** — Can you sell shares? Can you bring in investors? Who gets to make decisions?
- **Credibility** — A registered Private Limited Company looks very different to a bank or investor than "Rahul's Freelance Services."
- **Fundraising ability** — VCs, angel investors, and banks all have preferences. Most institutional investors in India will only invest in a Private Limited company.

Think of it like this: choosing a business structure is like choosing the rules of a game before you play. You can change the rules mid-game, but it's painful and expensive. Get it right early.

## 2. Sole Proprietorship — The Simplest Form

### What It Is

A sole proprietorship is when one person *is* the business. There's no legal separation between you and your company. If you're a freelance designer, a local kirana store owner, a tutor who charges students — you are, by default, running a sole proprietorship.

In India, you don't even need to formally "register" a sole proprietorship. You can just start. Your business income shows up on your personal income tax return. Your business bank account is just your bank account (though you might open a separate one for clarity).

### The Good Parts

**It's incredibly easy to start.** No paperwork, no government filings, no legal fees. You just decide to do business and you're doing business.

**You keep all the profit.** No partners, no shareholders, no board asking you to justify your decisions. You made money, you keep it.

**Tax simplicity.** Business income = your personal income. You file one return.

**Full control.** You make every decision. Nobody can overrule you.

### The Bad Parts

**Unlimited liability.** This is the big one. If your business gets sued, or takes on debt it can't pay back, *you* are personally liable. Creditors can come after your personal savings, your property, your car — everything. The business and you are the same legal entity.

Imagine you run a catering service as a sole proprietor. You serve food at a wedding. 50 people get food poisoning. They sue you for damages worth 40 lakh rupees. That lawsuit is against *you*, not just "the catering business." Your savings, your assets — all exposed.

**Hard to raise money.** Banks are uncomfortable lending large amounts to sole proprietors. Investors won't touch it. There are no shares to issue, no ownership structure that investors can participate in.

**It ends with you.** If you die or become incapacitated, the business legally ceases to exist. There's no separate entity that continues.

**Perceived as less serious.** Right or wrong, a customer, partner, or vendor often takes a registered company more seriously than an individual doing business.

### Real Examples

- **Your neighborhood kirana store.** Suresh Bhaiya's shop on the corner? Almost certainly a sole proprietorship. Simple, low-stakes, local.
- **Freelancers everywhere.** A graphic designer on Upwork, a content writer on Fiverr, a photographer who does weddings — these are typically sole proprietors until they get big enough to formalize.
- **Local tutors, coaches, consultants.** A CA who runs a small practice, a yoga instructor, a fitness trainer.

> **What This Means in Practice:**
> If you're testing an idea, doing freelance work on the side, or running a small local service, starting as a sole proprietor makes complete sense. Keep it simple. But the moment your business starts carrying real financial risk — employees, contracts, significant revenue, physical products — the unlimited liability becomes a real danger and you should consider upgrading your structure.

## 3. Partnership — Two or More People Together

### What It Is

A partnership is like a sole proprietorship, but with multiple people. Two or more individuals agree to run a business together, share profits, share losses, and share decision-making. In India, partnerships are governed by the Indian Partnership Act, 1932.

You can create a partnership with a verbal agreement, though you should absolutely have a written **Partnership Deed** — a document that spells out who owns what percentage, who makes which decisions, how profits are split, and what happens if someone wants to leave.

### General Partnership vs. Limited Partnership

| Feature | General Partnership | Limited Partnership |
|---|---|---|
| Who controls the business | All partners equally (unless agreed otherwise) | General partners control; limited partners don't |
| Liability | All partners have unlimited personal liability | Limited partners' liability is capped at their investment |
| Who can be held responsible | Every partner | Only general partners |
| Common use case | Law firms, medical practices, consultancies | Real estate funds, private equity |

In a **general partnership**, every partner is fully responsible for everything. If your partner signs a bad deal or takes on debt without telling you, you are liable for it too. This is the "joint and several liability" concept — it sounds formal, but it just means creditors can come after any of you for the full amount.

A **limited partnership** is more structured. You have at least one "general partner" who runs the show and bears unlimited liability, and "limited partners" who put in money but don't manage day-to-day operations. Their downside is limited to how much they invested.

### The Good Parts

**Pooled resources.** Two people means two sets of skills, two networks, two sets of capital. Dilnawaz knows the product side; Priya knows sales. Together they're stronger.

**Shared decision-making.** Big decisions are harder when one person is second-guessing themselves. A good partner gives you a sounding board.

**Easy to set up.** Like a sole proprietorship, a general partnership doesn't require complex registration (though it should be registered with the Registrar of Firms for legal protection).

### The Bad Parts

**Shared liability.** Your partner's mistakes are your problem. If they sign a terrible contract, hire someone who commits fraud, or make a bad financial decision — you share the consequences.

**Conflict potential.** Partnerships break up. Friends start businesses together and become enemies over disagreements about direction, money, effort, or values. A bad partnership is worse than a bad marriage because the money is tangled up too.

**Hard to raise investment.** Like sole proprietorships, traditional partnerships aren't the preferred structure for institutional investors.

### Real Examples

- **Law firms and CA firms.** Deloitte, KPMG, McKinsey — at their origin, and many still technically structured as partnerships at the top level.
- **Small businesses started by friends.** Two engineers starting a consultancy together, two doctors opening a clinic, two chefs starting a catering company.
- **Early-stage family businesses.** Many Indian family businesses started as partnerships between family members before incorporating.

> **What This Means in Practice:**
> If you're starting something with a co-founder, a partnership deed is the minimum. But if the business is going to grow, take on employees, or raise money, converting to a Private Limited Company is almost always the next step. The partnership structure is fine for small, stable, professional services businesses. It's not built for scale.

## 4. Limited Liability Company / Private Limited Company — The Most Common Startup Structure

### What It Is

This is where most serious Indian startups live. A **Private Limited Company (Pvt Ltd)** is a separate legal entity — distinct from its founders. It can own property, sign contracts, hire people, sue and be sued, all in its own name.

The critical phrase is **limited liability**. If the company runs into financial trouble, creditors can only go after the *company's assets*, not the founders' personal savings. You, as a founder, are protected.

The "private" in Private Limited means the shares of this company are not publicly traded on a stock exchange. You can't just buy shares of a Pvt Ltd company on Zerodha or Groww. Ownership changes happen privately, between known parties, with regulatory filings.

In the US and many countries, the equivalent structure is called an **LLC (Limited Liability Company)** — similar concept, slightly different legal mechanics. In the UK, it's a **Private Limited Company** as well. The names vary; the core idea is the same.

### Why Almost Every Indian Startup Is a Pvt Ltd

**1. Investor compatibility.** Every major VC fund, angel investor, and accelerator in India will only invest in a Private Limited Company. Why? Because shares are how they get ownership. You can't give an investor equity in a sole proprietorship or a simple partnership. In a Pvt Ltd, you issue them shares. Clean, legal, trackable.

**2. Liability protection.** Founders sleep better knowing their personal assets are protected. If Zepto were a sole proprietorship and something went wrong at scale, the founders would be personally bankrupt. That's not a risk serious founders take.

**3. Credibility and trust.** "XYZ Technologies Private Limited" looks different on a contract than "Rahul's Tech Services." Banks give better terms, enterprise clients sign deals more readily, employees feel more secure.

**4. Perpetual existence.** A company continues to exist even if a founder leaves, sells their stake, or passes away. The entity is separate from any individual person.

**5. ESOPs.** You can offer employees stock options — a share in the company's future success. This is how startups compete with big salaries. Without a proper share structure, you can't do this.

### How Founders Are Protected — An Example

Imagine you start a fintech app as a Pvt Ltd company. You take a loan of 2 crore rupees to build the product. The product fails. You can't repay the loan.

The lender can seize the company's assets — its servers, its IP, its remaining cash. But they cannot take your personal savings account, your car, or your home (assuming you didn't personally guarantee the loan, which sometimes banks require for early-stage companies — something to watch out for).

Now imagine you started the same business as a sole proprietorship. Same failure, same 2 crore loan. The lender *can* come after your personal assets. All of them.

### Key Features of a Pvt Ltd Company

- **Minimum 2 directors, minimum 2 shareholders** (can be the same people)
- **Share capital** — the company has a defined ownership structure via shares
- **Board of directors** — legally responsible for running the company
- **Annual filings** — ROC (Registrar of Companies) filings, annual returns, financial statements
- **Statutory audit** — accounts must be audited by a Chartered Accountant

### Real Examples

- **Zomato** was a Pvt Ltd before it went public in 2021.
- **Zepto** is currently a Pvt Ltd company (or its holding structure equivalent).
- **Razorpay, CRED, Meesho** — all Private Limited Companies until/unless they go public.
- **Flipkart** was a Pvt Ltd for years before Walmart acquired a majority stake, and it remains private today.

> **What This Means in Practice:**
> If you're serious about building a real business — one that raises money, hires employees, signs enterprise contracts, or builds a product — register a Private Limited Company. The cost is about 6,000 to 15,000 rupees with a CA, takes 10-15 working days, and protects you in ways that are completely worth it.

## 5. Corporation / Public Limited Company — Built for Scale

### What It Is

A **Public Limited Company** (in India: **Ltd**, no "Private") is a company whose shares can be publicly traded on a stock exchange like the BSE or NSE. When a company "goes public" or does an IPO (Initial Public Offering), it converts from a Private to a Public Limited Company (or equivalent in its jurisdiction).

In the US and globally, large corporations are structured as C-Corps (like Apple, Google, Tesla) or S-Corps. The defining feature of a corporation is that ownership is **divided into shares**, and those shares can be freely bought and sold by the general public.

### How It Works

When you buy shares of Tata Consultancy Services on the NSE, you are buying tiny pieces of ownership in TCS. If TCS has 100 crore shares outstanding and you own 100 shares, you own 0.00001% of TCS. That's real ownership — it comes with voting rights and a claim on profits (dividends).

**Key players in a corporation:**

- **Shareholders** — own the company via shares; have voting rights on major decisions
- **Board of Directors** — elected by shareholders; responsible for governance and major strategic decisions; hire/fire the CEO
- **CEO and management team** — run the day-to-day operations; answer to the Board
- **Auditors and regulators** — ensure the company follows rules (SEBI regulations in India)

### Why Companies Go Public

**1. Raise enormous capital.** Zomato's IPO in 2021 raised 9,375 crore rupees in a single day. No private fundraising can match that scale.

**2. Give early investors an exit.** VCs and angels who backed a company early eventually want to sell their shares. A public market gives them that opportunity.

**3. Employee liquidity.** Employees with ESOPs can now sell their shares on the open market.

**4. Brand and credibility.** Being listed on a stock exchange adds a layer of public scrutiny, transparency, and prestige.

### The Trade-offs

Going public is not free. It comes with:

- **Quarterly reporting obligations** — you must publicly disclose financials every three months
- **SEBI regulations** — extensive compliance requirements
- **Short-term pressure** — public market investors often care about quarterly numbers, which can conflict with long-term thinking
- **Loss of some control** — now millions of shareholders have opinions about your company

Jeff Bezos famously worried about "Wall Street's short-termism" pressuring him to cut long-term investments. Amazon famously operated at near-zero profit for years, reinvesting everything — something public company CEOs get questioned relentlessly about.

### Real Examples

- **TCS, Infosys, Wipro** — all Public Limited companies on NSE/BSE
- **Zomato** — went public July 2021; IPO price: 76 rupees; opened at 116
- **Nykaa** — IPO November 2021; India's first unicorn founded by a woman to go public
- **Apple, Google, Amazon** — US corporations; their shares trade on NASDAQ

## 6. Not-for-Profit / NGO — Businesses That Don't Maximize Profit

### What It Is

Most people think "business" means "make as much money as possible." But some organizations are legally structured to pursue a social, educational, religious, or charitable mission — and any surplus goes back into the mission, not to owners.

In India, these can be registered as:
- **Section 8 Company** (under Companies Act 2013) — a not-for-profit company
- **Trust** — governed by the Indian Trusts Act; common for religious and charitable purposes
- **Society** — registered under Societies Registration Act 1860; common for clubs, associations, educational institutions

In the US, the equivalent is a **501(c)(3)** tax-exempt nonprofit.

### Do NGOs Make Money?

Yes, all the time — they just call it revenue, and they plow it back into operations. Amnesty International, ISKCON, Teach For India — these are large organizations with significant budgets, paid professional staff, and sophisticated operations. They're "not-for-profit" because there are no shareholders extracting dividends, not because they're always broke.

### Real Examples

- **CRY (Child Rights and You)** — one of India's most well-known NGOs, a registered trust
- **Infosys Foundation** — CSR and philanthropic work by the Infosys family; registered as a trust
- **Wikipedia** — run by the Wikimedia Foundation, a 501(c)(3) nonprofit in the US
- **Khan Academy** — nonprofit providing free education; founded by Sal Khan, backed by Gates Foundation

## 7. Government-Owned Enterprises — BSNL, Air India, ONGC

### Why Does the Government Own Businesses?

This is a question many people don't think to ask. Why does the government run airlines, phone networks, oil companies, and banks? A few reasons:

**1. Strategic industries.** Oil, defense, electricity — governments sometimes consider these too important to leave to private hands. ONGC (Oil and Natural Gas Corporation) was a government monopoly for decades because India's energy security depended on it.

**2. Public goods and services.** Some things are considered essential services — rural telecommunications, postal services in remote areas — where private companies won't invest because profits are too thin. BSNL exists partly to ensure connectivity in places Airtel and Jio don't find profitable.

**3. Historical reasons.** India nationalized many industries post-independence (banks, insurance, steel, coal) based on a socialist economic philosophy. Many of these are still partially or fully government-owned.

**4. Employment.** Government enterprises in India employ millions. Decisions about privatization or restructuring are deeply political.

### The Problems with Government-Owned Enterprises

Air India was a legendary disaster — a government airline that accumulated losses of 70,000 crore rupees before the government finally sold it to the Tata Group in 2022. BSNL was the dominant telecom operator in the 1990s; today it's barely relevant while Jio and Airtel dominate.

Why do government enterprises often underperform? A few consistent reasons:
- **No profit motive** means less pressure to be efficient
- **Political interference** — hiring decisions, pricing decisions, and strategy get tangled in politics
- **Bureaucratic inertia** — slow decision-making in fast-moving industries is fatal
- **Talent** — top engineers and managers often prefer private sector pay

Not all government enterprises fail, of course. ISRO (technically a government department) is world-class. But it operates more like a mission-driven organization than a typical government enterprise.

## 8. Choosing the Right Structure — Decision Framework with Examples

Here's how to think about what structure is right for you.

### The Quick Comparison Table

| Structure | Liability | Taxes | Raising Investment | Complexity | Best For |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sole Proprietorship | Unlimited (personal) | Personal income tax | Nearly impossible | Minimal | Freelancers, tiny local businesses |
| General Partnership | Unlimited (shared) | Partners' personal income | Difficult | Low | Small professional practices |
| Limited Partnership | Limited for LPs, unlimited for GPs | Pass-through | Possible | Medium | Real estate, private funds |
| Private Limited (Pvt Ltd) | Limited | Corporate tax (dividends taxed in shareholders' hands) | Yes — standard for investors | Medium | Startups, serious SMEs |
| Public Limited (Ltd) | Limited | Corporate tax | Yes — public markets | High | Large companies, post-IPO |
| Section 8 / NGO | Limited | Tax-exempt (with conditions) | Grants and donations | Medium | Social enterprises, charities |
| Government Enterprise | Government-backed | Special provisions | N/A | High (political) | Strategic/public service industries |

### The Decision Framework

```
Step 1: Are you alone or with partners?
  → Alone: Sole Proprietorship OR One Person Company (OPC) OR Pvt Ltd (a regular Pvt Ltd needs 2 directors/shareholders; an OPC lets you incorporate solo)
  → With others: Partnership OR Pvt Ltd

Step 2: Do you need to raise investment?
  → Yes: Pvt Ltd (mandatory for serious fundraising)
  → No: Simpler structure may be fine

Step 3: Is personal liability a real risk?
  → Yes (significant contracts, employees, physical products): Pvt Ltd
  → No (very small, low-risk): Sole Proprietorship or Partnership

Step 4: Are you mission-driven, not profit-driven?
  → Yes: Section 8 / NGO / Trust

Step 5: Are you building to scale and potentially go public?
  → Yes: Start as Pvt Ltd, convert to Public Ltd at IPO stage
```

### Real Decision Examples

**Arjun is a freelance developer** doing small gigs on the side. → Sole Proprietorship. No need for complexity. Keep it simple.

**Riya and Karan are building a SaaS product** and want to raise angel funding next year. → Register as a Private Limited Company now. Don't wait.

**A group of doctors wants to open a clinic together.** → Partnership or Pvt Ltd. If any of them have personal assets to protect and they want to grow into multiple clinics, Pvt Ltd.

**A group of students wants to run an NGO for education.** → Section 8 Company or registered Society.

## 9. What Happens When You Get the Structure Wrong

### Cautionary Tale 1: The Sole Proprietor Who Got Sued

A caterer in Pune ran her business as a sole proprietorship for 8 years. She was doing well — 40-50 lakh in annual revenue, several employees, corporate clients. She never felt the need to formalize.

Then a corporate client claimed her food caused illness at their event and sued for 25 lakh in damages. Because she was a sole proprietor, the lawsuit was against her personally. Her personal savings, her car, the fixed deposit she'd been building — all potentially exposed. She eventually settled for 8 lakh out of court, which wiped out her emergency fund.

Had she been a Pvt Ltd, the lawsuit would have been against the company. Her personal assets would have been shielded.

### Cautionary Tale 2: The Partnership Without a Deed

Two friends — let's call them Vikram and Aditya — started a digital marketing agency together. They had a verbal understanding: 50-50 split. No written partnership deed.

After two years and good growth, they disagreed on whether to take on a major client that would require long hours. Aditya wanted to take it; Vikram didn't. The disagreement escalated. Vikram wanted out.

With no written deed, they had no clear mechanism for one partner to exit. No agreed valuation method. No buyout process. What should have been a clean separation became an 18-month legal battle that consumed both of them and nearly destroyed the business.

A partnership deed would have cost 5,000 rupees and two hours with a lawyer. The legal battle cost them each 3-4 lakh and significant mental health.

### Cautionary Tale 3: The Startup That Raised on the Wrong Structure

A founder in Bangalore built an app as a sole proprietorship, got traction, and approached an investor. The investor said, "I love this — let's do a term sheet." Then they discovered the business was a sole proprietorship.

To convert, they had to essentially "start over" — incorporate a new company, transfer IP (intellectual property — the code, brand, etc.) into the new entity, get the IP transfer legally documented, and update all contracts. It took 4 months and cost approximately 2 lakh in legal and CA fees. The investor almost walked away twice because of the delays.

## 10. How to Set Up a Company in India Step by Step

Here's a practical walkthrough of registering a Private Limited Company in India in 2024.

### What You Need to Start

- Minimum 2 directors (can be founders)
- Minimum 2 shareholders (directors and shareholders can be the same people)
- A registered office address in India (can be your home)
- PAN and Aadhaar of all directors

### The Process

```
Step 1: Get Digital Signature Certificates (DSC)
  → Each director needs a DSC (a digital ID for signing government forms)
  → Cost: ~1,500 to 2,000 rupees per director
  → Time: 1-2 days

Step 2: Apply for Director Identification Numbers (DIN)
  → A unique ID number for each director
  → Applied via MCA21 portal (Ministry of Corporate Affairs)
  → Usually done as part of the incorporation process

Step 3: Choose and Reserve a Company Name
  → Apply via SPICe+ form on MCA portal
  → Name must be unique and follow naming guidelines
  → Tip: Have 3-4 name options ready; some get rejected

Step 4: Draft Memorandum of Association (MoA) and Articles of Association (AoA)
  → MoA: States the company's objectives (what business you're doing)
  → AoA: States the internal rules of the company (how decisions are made)
  → Your CA will draft these for you

Step 5: File SPICe+ (Simplified Proforma for Incorporating Company Electronically)
  → This is the main incorporation form
  → Includes simultaneous applications for:
     - PAN (Company's PAN card)
     - TAN (Tax Deduction Account Number)
     - GSTIN (GST Registration, optional at this stage)
     - EPFO and ESIC registration
     - Bank account opening letter

Step 6: Pay Stamp Duty and Government Fees
  → Varies by state and share capital
  → Typically 2,000 to 10,000 rupees for most startups

Step 7: Certificate of Incorporation Issued
  → MCA issues the Certificate of Incorporation with CIN (Company Identification Number)
  → Usually 10-15 working days from filing

Step 8: Open a Current Bank Account
  → Use the Certificate of Incorporation + PAN to open a current account
  → Deposit the paid-up capital (minimum 1 rupee — yes, literally 1 rupee is sufficient legally)

Step 9: Post-Incorporation Compliance
  → Appoint a statutory auditor within 30 days
  → File commencement of business form (INC-20A) within 180 days
  → Register for GST if turnover exceeds 40 lakh/year for goods, 20 lakh for services (lower limits in special category states)
```

### Cost Summary

| Item | Approximate Cost |
|---|---|
| CA/professional fees | 5,000 – 15,000 |
| Digital Signature Certificates | 3,000 – 6,000 (for 2 directors) |
| Government stamp duty | 2,000 – 10,000 |
| Miscellaneous | 1,000 – 2,000 |
| **Total** | **~10,000 – 30,000 rupees** |

Time: 2-3 weeks from start to Certificate of Incorporation.

> **Pro tip:** Use a CA or a startup incorporation service like Razorpay Rize, Vakilsearch, or Startupfilings. They make the process smooth and cost 8,000-15,000 all-in for basic incorporation. It's worth the money.

## 11. Summary

Let's bring it all together.

Every business exists within a legal structure, and that structure shapes everything: how you're taxed, who can sue you personally, whether investors will fund you, and how ownership is managed.

**The seven major types:**

1. **Sole Proprietorship** — You are the business. Simple, zero cost, but you carry all the risk personally. Great for small freelancers and local shops, dangerous for anything with real financial exposure.

2. **Partnership** — Two or more people sharing a business. Easy to set up, powerful if the partners are complementary, but shared unlimited liability and partnership disputes are real risks. Always get a written Partnership Deed.

3. **Private Limited Company (Pvt Ltd)** — The gold standard for Indian startups. A separate legal entity with limited liability. Required for serious fundraising. Worth the setup cost for any business with real ambition.

4. **Public Limited Company (Ltd)** — A Pvt Ltd that has gone public. Shares traded on stock exchanges. Allows access to massive capital but comes with regulatory obligations and public scrutiny.

5. **Not-for-Profit / NGO** — Section 8 Company, Trust, or Society. For mission-driven work where surplus is reinvested, not distributed to owners.

6. **Government Enterprise** — Owned by the state. Exists for strategic, public, or historical reasons. Examples: ONGC, BSNL, Air India (until 2022).

7. **LLP (Limited Liability Partnership)** — A hybrid we didn't cover deeply, but worth knowing: combines the flexibility of a partnership with limited liability. Popular for professional service firms (lawyers, consultants, architects).

**The core decision** for most young entrepreneurs reading this: if you're building something serious, register a Private Limited Company. The protection, credibility, and investment-readiness it gives you far outweigh the 15,000-rupee cost and two weeks of paperwork.

2. **Scenario Analysis:** Your friend wants to start a photography business doing wedding shoots. She'll be working alone. 3. **Partnership Deed Exercise:** Two friends are starting a web development agency together. Write the key clauses you'd want in their Partnership Deed. Think about: profit split, decision-making authority, what happens if one wants to quit, what happens if they disagree, can one partner bring in another partner without consent?

4. **Liability Scenario:** You run a food tech startup as a Pvt Ltd company. Your company takes a loan of 50 lakh rupees. The startup fails. You have 8 lakh in personal savings. Walk through exactly what happens. What can the lender come after? What is protected?
