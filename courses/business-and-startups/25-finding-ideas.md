# Chapter 25: Finding Ideas — How to Spot Problems Worth Solving

*The best startup ideas feel obvious in hindsight and look crazy at first. This chapter teaches you how to find them.*

---

## 1. Why Most Startup Ideas Are Bad (and That's Okay)

The first mistake aspiring founders make is thinking they need to find the perfect idea before starting. This is paralysis. Ideas are not precious. They are abundant. The question is not "do I have an idea" but "how do I find ideas that are worth pursuing?"

Most startup ideas are bad for one of these reasons:
- The market is too small
- The problem is not painful enough
- The solution already exists
- The founder is not the right person to build it
- It requires technology that doesn't exist yet
- It requires a behavior change that won't happen

Having bad ideas first is normal. It's the process of generating and filtering ideas that eventually leads to good ones.

---

## 2. The Spectrum of Ideas — Vitamin vs Painkiller vs Morphine

One of the most useful frameworks for evaluating startup ideas:

**Vitamins** — Nice to have. Users feel good about using it but don't desperately need it. Most productivity apps, social apps, and "gamified" experiences are vitamins. The problem with vitamins: users don't seek them out urgently. They're easy to churn from.

**Painkillers** — Solve a real, specific, painful problem. The user actively feels the pain when they don't have your solution. Razorpay was a painkiller — accepting payments online was genuinely painful before it existed.

**Morphine** — The pain is so severe and immediate that the user cannot function without a solution. A platform that prevents a company from getting sued or losing millions of rupees is morphine.

The further up the spectrum, the easier it is to build a business. Vitamins require massive marketing. Morphine markets itself.

**Exercise:** For your idea, ask — if I took away this product tomorrow, how many users would call me upset? If the answer is "not many," you might have a vitamin.

## 3. Where Good Ideas Come From

Paul Graham of Y Combinator has the clearest thinking on this:

**1. Working at the frontier**

The best ideas come from people who are pushing the limits of what's possible in their domain. If you're a doctor frustrated by hospital software, you see the problem directly. If you're a farmer struggling with market prices, you see the opportunity in AgriTech. The founder who lives the problem has a massive advantage over one who reads about it.

**2. Things that have recently become possible**

Every time a new technology platform arrives, a wave of new businesses become possible. The smartphone created Uber, Ola, Swiggy, Instagram. Cloud computing created Freshworks, Zoho, thousands of SaaS companies. Foundation models (ChatGPT, Claude) are creating a new wave right now. Ask: "What became possible in the last 2-3 years that wasn't possible before? Who is building for that?"

**3. Things that are changing**

New laws. New demographics. New infrastructure. Jio launching 4G at near-zero cost made hundreds of businesses possible — video streaming, fintech, edtech — that simply couldn't work before because data was expensive.

**4. Things people are embarrassed to admit they need**

Sex, mental health, debt, immigration — these are real problems that millions of people face but don't talk about openly. Companies like Headspace (mental health), Hims (men's sexual health), and BetterHelp (therapy) built large businesses in spaces that seemed awkward.

## 4. How to Find Ideas — Practical Methods

### Method 1: Notice Your Own Frustrations

Keep a frustration journal. Every time you find yourself saying "why doesn't this work better?" or "I can't believe there isn't a better solution for this" — write it down. Most good startups started as the founder's own problem.

Deepinder Goyal (Zomato) noticed that finding restaurant menus was painful and digitized them. Sachin and Binny Bansal (Flipkart) were frustrated by Indian e-commerce. Harshil Mathur (Razorpay) was frustrated by payment integrations.

### Method 2: What Do Businesses Do Manually That Could Be Automated?

Visit any type of business — a restaurant, a clinic, a school, a manufacturing plant. Ask them what they spend most of their time doing. Manual, repetitive tasks that require human judgment are opportunities for software.

A clinic that manually calls patients to remind them about appointments. A restaurant that tracks inventory in a physical notebook. A manufacturer that processes invoices by hand. Each of these is a potential software product.

### Method 3: Find the "Before Uber" Moment in an Industry

Uber made something that existed (taxis) dramatically more convenient. Think about industries where the experience is terrible — not because the problem is unsolvable, but because nobody has bothered to build the right product.

The insurance industry in India is painful to navigate. Home loans require visiting multiple banks. Finding a good doctor involves asking relatives. These are "before Uber" moments waiting for the right founder.

### Method 4: Talk to Many Different Kinds of People

Founders who only talk to other founders and investors develop a very skewed view of the world. Spend time with:
- Farmers (the most underserved by technology in India)
- Small business owners (massive market, massive problems)
- Teachers and students
- People in tier-2 and tier-3 cities
- Healthcare workers

Some of India's biggest startup opportunities exist where VCs in Koramangala cafes aren't looking.

## 5. The Startup Idea Matrix

Evaluate any startup idea on two dimensions:

**Axis 1: Market Size** — How many people have this problem? (Huge = tens of millions in India alone. Small = niche but wealthy.)

**Axis 2: Problem Severity × Frequency** — How painful is the problem? How often does it occur?

```
                HIGH Severity × Frequency
                         |
           BEST          |    GOOD
           OPPORTUNITIES |    (niche but painful —
                         |     premium niche business)
Large Market ————————————+———————————— Small Market
                         |
           MAYBE         |    AVOID
           (big market   |    (small market
            but mild pain|     + mild pain)
            — a vitamin) |
                LOW Severity × Frequency
```

The best ideas are in the top-left quadrant: large market, frequent, severe pain. Quick commerce (Zepto, Blinkit) — large market (urban India), frequent need (grocery runs), severe pain (running out of milk at midnight).

## 6. Ideas That Sound Bad But Are Actually Good

One useful mental model: if your startup idea sounds obviously good, many people are already working on it. The best startup ideas often sound crazy at first.

| Idea (at launch) | What it seemed like | What it became |
|------------------|---------------------|----------------|
| Airbnb | "Strangers sleeping in my house? Dangerous." | $75B company |
| Uber | "Getting in a stranger's car?" | $70B company |
| WhatsApp | "Another messaging app?" | $19B acquisition |
| Instagram | "Filtered photos only?" | $1B acquisition, 2 billion users |
| Zepto | "10-minute delivery? Impossible." | $1.4B valuation in 2 years |
| CRED | "Pay credit card bills for rewards? Who cares?" | India's most active premium user base |

The question is not "does this sound crazy?" — it's "is the problem real and are people already doing something clunky to solve it?"

## 7. Why Execution Matters More Than the Idea

A common myth: the idea is the valuable thing, and someone will steal it if you share it.

Reality: the idea is worth almost nothing. Execution is everything.

Evidence:
- When Facebook launched, Friendster, MySpace, and dozens of other social networks existed
- When Google launched, AltaVista, Yahoo, and Excite were dominant
- When WhatsApp launched, BBM, iMessage, and a dozen messaging apps already existed
- When Flipkart launched, Indiatimes Shopping, Rediff Shopping, and eBay India existed

The winner was not the first mover. It was the team that executed most relentlessly.

Share your idea widely. The person who steals it won't execute it as well as you will. And the feedback you get from sharing will be more valuable than the protection of silence.

## 8. How to Evaluate Your Startup Idea — The Checklist

Before committing deeply to an idea, run it through this checklist:

- [ ] Can I name 50+ specific people who have this problem right now?
- [ ] Are these people currently doing something (anything) to solve it?
- [ ] Is the market large enough to build a $100 crore company? ($1B company?)
- [ ] Why hasn't this been solved? (Regulation, technology gap, behavior change required, nobody noticed?)
- [ ] Am I (or is my team) particularly well-positioned to solve this? Why us?
- [ ] Can I get the first 10 customers without spending money on marketing?
- [ ] What's the one thing that must be true for this to work?

If you can't answer most of these, the idea needs more work — or more time spent talking to potential users.

## 9. Case Study: How Razorpay Found Its Idea

Harshil Mathur was at IIT Roorkee, working on a student startup project that needed to accept payments online. He discovered that setting up a payment gateway in India in 2013 required:
- 3-4 weeks of paperwork
- A registered company
- Detailed business plans
- Bank approvals
- Integration that required 200+ lines of code

He thought: "If it's this hard for me, it's this hard for every developer, every startup, every small business in India trying to sell online." He noticed that in the US, Stripe had solved this problem elegantly.

That was the idea: bring Stripe-like payments to India, adapted for Indian payment methods (UPI, netbanking, wallets). Not original. Not unique. But a genuine, painful problem that he was well-positioned to solve (developer himself, understood both the problem and the technology).

---

## Summary

- Most startup ideas are bad — that's fine. Generate many, filter ruthlessly
- The best ideas are **painkillers or morphine**, not vitamins
- Ideas come from: working at the frontier, things that recently became possible, watching industry inefficiencies, noticing your own frustrations
- Evaluate ideas on market size and pain severity × frequency
- Good startup ideas often sound bad at first — counterintuitive is a feature, not a bug
- Execution matters 10x more than the idea itself

## Exercises

1. **Keep a frustration journal for one week.** Write down every moment where you think "there should be a better way." At the end, categorize them: which are vitamins, which are painkillers?

2. **Pick any industry in India** that feels outdated or painful (insurance, healthcare booking, school admissions, farming, trucking). Spend one hour researching what startups exist in that space and what's still unsolved.

3. **Find a "before Uber" moment** — an industry where the experience today is what taxi-taking was like in 2009. Describe it in one paragraph.

4. **Apply the startup idea matrix** to three ideas you or others have. Where do they fall?

5. **Interview a small business owner** (kirana store, local restaurant, clinic). Ask them: what takes the most time? What do you wish was easier? What software do you use, and what do you hate about it?
