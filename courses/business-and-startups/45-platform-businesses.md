# Chapter 45: Platform Businesses — How Platforms Become Monopolies

A platform business creates value by facilitating interactions between two or more distinct user groups. Unlike pipe businesses (which create and sell products or services in a linear value chain), platforms enable participants to create and exchange value.

---

## Platforms vs Pipes

**Pipe business:** Company creates value → pushes to consumers. Linear. Factory makes cars → dealer sells cars → customer buys. Linear value chain.

**Platform business:** Platform facilitates value creation by participants. Apple doesn't make apps — app developers make apps on Apple's platform. YouTube doesn't make videos — creators make videos. Airbnb doesn't own homes — hosts list their homes.

The platform creates the infrastructure, rules, and matching. Participants create the value.

**Why this is powerful:** The platform scales without proportional cost growth. Adding a million more users to Facebook costs almost nothing incrementally. Adding a million more cars to Uber's road network costs nothing (the cars already exist — Uber just connects them).

---

## Why Platforms Become Monopolies

Network effects compound. As the platform grows, it becomes more valuable to all participants. As it becomes more valuable, more participants join. This creates a self-reinforcing loop that becomes nearly impossible to break.

**Facebook:** More users → more social connections → more content → more time spent → more valuable to advertisers → more revenue to invest in platform → more features to attract users. This loop ran for 15 years.

**Windows:** More Windows users → more developers write Windows apps → more apps → more valuable to users → more users choose Windows. This loop kept Microsoft dominant for decades.

**Google Search:** More searches → more data about what people search → better search results → more searches. Plus: more advertisers → more revenue to improve product.

---

## Famous Platform Businesses

**Apple App Store:**
Apple provides the platform (iOS), the store (App Store), and the rules. Developers create apps. Apple takes 30% of every app purchase and in-app purchase.

The power: if you want to reach iPhone users, you have no choice but to go through Apple's App Store. This gives Apple extraordinary power — they can reject apps, change terms, and extract 30% without developers having any real alternative.

**Google Search + AdWords:**
Google built the largest advertising platform in the world by first building the most valuable consumer product (search) and then connecting advertisers to those consumers. The consumer side is free. Advertisers pay. The platform extracts value from this mismatch.

**Microsoft Windows:**
Windows is the operating system platform on which most PC applications run. Microsoft created the rules, the APIs, the developer tools — and took a royalty from hardware manufacturers who shipped Windows.

**LinkedIn:**
LinkedIn owns professional identity. If you need to hire someone or find a job, you need to be on LinkedIn. If you need professional networking, you need LinkedIn. The platform becomes essential infrastructure for professional life, creating lock-in.

---

## Platform Strategy: How to Build One

**Step 1:** Identify two or more user groups with a compelling reason to interact.

**Step 2:** Attract one side first (usually by subsidizing them). Uber paid drivers. YouTube paid (eventually) creators. Google paid users with free search.

**Step 3:** Create value for the second side that depends on the first side being there.

**Step 4:** Build the infrastructure that makes interactions easy, trustworthy, and reliable.

**Step 5:** Monetize once both sides are committed (lock-in makes monetization easier).

**The platform design rules:**
- Enable positive interactions
- Prevent negative interactions (fraud, spam, bad actors)
- Set the rules so participants don't feel taken advantage of (at least early on)
- Provide tools that increase the value participants can create

---

## Jio as a Platform Strategy

Reliance Jio's entry into Indian telecom in 2016 is the most dramatic platform strategy in Indian business history.

**What they did:** Entered the market with effectively free 4G data and voice calls (or ₹0 for months). Acquired 100 million users in 5 months.

**Why it wasn't just telecom:** Jio was never just a telecom company. The data connectivity was the platform. On top of it: JioTV, JioCinema, JioSaavn, JioMart, JioHealth, JioPay, JioCloud. The goal was to become the operating system of Indian digital life.

**The outcome:** India went from one of the most expensive data markets to the cheapest in the world. Entire businesses were created that couldn't exist before (video streaming, fintech, edtech) because data was now free. This is the platform flywheel — by enabling others, Jio made itself more essential.

## Platform Regulation — When Governments Fight Back

Platforms that become monopolies attract regulatory scrutiny. The power to exclude, to change terms, and to extract rent from participants creates tensions.

**Google's antitrust case (US):** The US Department of Justice sued Google for illegally maintaining a monopoly in search. The 2024 ruling found Google guilty.

**Apple's App Store:** Epic Games sued Apple over the 30% fee and the inability to use alternative payment systems. Courts partially agreed, forcing Apple to allow external payment links in some markets.

**EU's Digital Markets Act (DMA):** Passed in 2023, requires large platforms to allow interoperability, alternative app stores, and fair access for third-party services.

**India's DPDP Act:** India's data protection legislation affects how platforms collect and use user data.

The regulatory trend: governments are increasingly treating large platforms as infrastructure that must be regulated like utilities, not as private companies free to set any terms.

---

## When to Build a Platform (vs a Product)

Building a platform is almost always harder than building a product. The chicken-and-egg problem, the regulatory risk, the quality control challenges — these are all formidable.

You should build a platform when:
- You have a large, fragmented supply side that wants to reach a large demand side
- Neither side can efficiently find the other without you
- There are genuine network effects (the more participants, the more valuable)
- You can create a sustainable take rate that participants will accept long-term

You should NOT try to build a platform when:
- You're building a product that solves one specific problem
- There's no genuine network effect
- The "two sides" are actually just your customers and your suppliers

Many startups call themselves "platforms" when they're actually products or services. The word has become a buzzword. Use it precisely.

---

## Summary

- Platforms facilitate interactions between user groups; pipes deliver value linearly
- Network effects make platforms winner-takes-most — growth compounds, moats deepen
- Apple App Store, Google Search, Windows, LinkedIn are the canonical examples
- Jio's platform strategy — free connectivity as the entry point for a digital empire — is India's best example
- Regulatory pressure on large platforms is increasing globally
- Building a platform is harder than building a product; do it only when genuine network effects exist
