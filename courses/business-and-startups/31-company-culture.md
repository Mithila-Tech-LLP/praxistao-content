# Chapter 31: Company Culture — Building It Intentionally from Day One

*Culture is not what you write on the wall. It's what you tolerate.*

---

## 1. What Culture Actually Is

Ask ten people what "company culture" means and you'll get ten answers:

- "The vibe of the office"
- "Our ping pong table and free snacks"
- "Working late every night"
- "How we treat each other"

None of these are wrong, but none are complete.

The best definition: **Culture is the set of behaviors that are rewarded and punished in an organization.**

Not what the company says it values. What it actually rewards. If a company says "we value integrity" but promotes someone who hits their number by lying to customers, the real culture is: results justify the means. If a company says "we value work-life balance" but fires the person who takes a proper lunch break, the real culture is: your time belongs to us.

Culture is set by what leaders *do*, not what they *say*.

---

## 2. Why Culture Gets Set Early and Is Hard to Change Later

Peter Thiel writes in *Zero to One* that the culture of a company is nearly impossible to change once it's established. The early founders' behavior becomes "the way things are done here" — even after those founders have left.

This happens because:
- Early employees model their behavior on founders
- New employees are socialized by existing employees
- Stories about how things were handled in the past become the reference for how things should be handled now
- Compensation, promotion, and firing decisions all encode values over time

This means: what you tolerate in year 1 becomes your culture in year 5. A founder who lets people come in late, cut corners on quality, or dismiss customer complaints is building those behaviors into the company's DNA.

The window to set culture intentionally is very short — roughly the first 20 employees.

## 3. The Netflix Culture Deck — The Most Important Business Document of the 2010s

In 2009, Netflix's Chief People Officer Patty McCord and CEO Reed Hastings published their 125-slide internal culture document publicly. Sheryl Sandberg called it "the most important document ever to come out of Silicon Valley."

It became famous because it was the first time a company articulated explicitly what it actually believed, rather than what sounded nice.

Some of the more challenging ideas:

**"Adequate performance gets a generous severance."**
Netflix would rather pay someone handsomely to leave than keep them at "good enough." Because good enough is not what a world-class team looks like.

**"We are a pro-sports team, not a family."**
Families keep members even when they underperform. Sports teams, when playing for a championship, cut people who aren't performing — even good people — if someone better is available.

**"The best thing we can do for our employees is hire brilliant people for them to work with."**
The biggest perk isn't free food. It's working alongside exceptional colleagues.

**"Freedom and responsibility."**
Netflix had unlimited vacation (no approval needed), no expense report policy (use judgment), no formal review cycles. In return: extraordinary performance was expected, and non-performers were let go quickly.

This was radical in 2009. It's been widely influential — dozens of major companies have rewritten their culture decks based on Netflix's.

## 4. How Founders Shape Culture Through Behavior, Not Words

Every action a founder takes is a cultural signal.

**If the founder:**
- Works on weekends, the implicit message is: so should you.
- Shouts at someone in a meeting, the message is: emotional outbursts are acceptable.
- Takes credit for an employee's idea, the message is: ideas belong to whoever has power.
- Admits a mistake openly, the message is: honesty is safe here.
- Fires a high performer for ethical violations, the message is: values matter more than output.

The most important cultural moments are the negative ones — how does the founder behave when things go badly? When someone makes a mistake? When a customer is wrong? When a competitor launches first?

These moments, watched closely by every employee, define the culture more than any all-hands presentation.

## 5. Toxic Culture — How It Develops and What It Costs

The two most famous toxic startup cultures of the 2010s:

### Uber (2014-2017)

Travis Kalanick built a culture of aggressive competition. The unofficial values included:
- "Always be hustlin'"
- "Toe-stepping" (pushing into others' territories)
- "Let builders build" (engineering above all else)

This created a world-class growth machine and a deeply dysfunctional workplace. In 2017:
- A female engineer published a blog post about systemic sexual harassment that was ignored by HR
- Internal investigation found hundreds of HR complaints that had been covered up
- The board forced Kalanick to resign

The cost: enormous talent exodus, regulatory scrutiny worldwide, and lasting reputation damage.

### WeWork (2019)

Adam Neumann ran WeWork as a cult of personality. His values were his personality — charismatic, visionary, substance-fueled. The culture rewarded loyalty to Neumann personally and punished anyone who questioned his decisions.

The cost: WeWork's IPO collapsed (valuation dropped from $47B to ~$8B in weeks), Neumann was forced out with a billion-dollar exit, and the company filed for bankruptcy in 2023.

**The pattern in both cases:** A charismatic founder whose unchecked behavior became the culture, combined with an investor community that valued growth over governance.

## 6. Building Culture Deliberately — Practical Steps

### Define 5-7 Core Values (Not 15)

Values that are actually useful are:
- Specific enough to make decisions by
- Distinctive (not just "excellence" and "integrity")
- Tested against real situations

**Bad values:** "Excellence," "Innovation," "Customer Focus" (every company says these)

**Good values:** Netflix's "We only keep highly effective people." Stripe's "Think rigorously." Amazon's "Disagree and commit." Zerodha's "Avoid conflicts of interest even when legally allowed."

Before you finalize values, test them: "Would this value cause us to make a different decision than our competitor?" If not, it's not doing work.

### Hire For Values First

In every interview, ask values-based questions. "Tell me about a time you made an ethical decision that cost you something." "Describe a situation where you disagreed with your manager." The answers reveal whether the candidate lives the values you care about.

### Make Values Visible in Decisions

When you promote someone, explain how they lived the values. When you fire someone, if appropriate, explain what value was violated. When you make a hard business decision, explain the values that guided it.

Values that are only spoken, but never used in decisions, are decorations.

## 7. Remote Culture — Managing Teams You Never See

Post-COVID, most startups have some degree of remote work. Building culture without physical proximity requires more intentionality:

- **Over-communicate written culture:** Norms that would be absorbed by osmosis in an office need to be written down
- **Async-first:** Document decisions, write long-form updates, use tools like Notion or Confluence
- **Deliberate social connection:** Virtual coffees, occasional off-sites, shared experiences
- **Meeting culture:** Define what needs synchronous discussion vs what can be async
- **Recognition:** Remote workers are easier to ignore; make appreciation explicit and public

Fully remote companies that work well (Basecamp, GitLab, Automattic) have invested years in building written culture. Their handbooks are public and extensively document how they operate.

## 8. Indian Startup Culture — What's Changing

For most of India's startup history, "hustle culture" was the default. 80-hour weeks were expected. Long office hours were status signals. Work-life balance was a Western concept that didn't fit the pace needed.

This is changing, driven by:
- **Mental health awareness:** High-profile founder burnouts and suicides in the Indian startup ecosystem created awareness
- **Talent competition:** The best engineers have options — they're choosing companies that treat them well
- **International influence:** Founders and employees who've worked at global companies bring different expectations
- **Post-COVID recalibration:** The pandemic forced a rethinking of work and life

The founders who are building great cultures in Indian startups (Zerodha's Nithin Kamath is a notable example) are succeeding by being explicitly intentional: writing down what they believe, hiring against it, and modeling it in their own behavior.

## 9. Case Study: Zerodha's Culture

Zerodha has built India's most successful bootstrapped startup — roughly ₹4,700 crore in profit on ₹8,300+ crore revenue in FY2024 — and has done it with a culture that is unusual in Indian business.

**What makes Zerodha's culture distinctive:**

- **No external capital:** No VC pressure means no pressure to grow at any cost. Profitability is the constraint, which builds fiscal discipline into the culture.
- **Radical transparency:** Nithin Kamath writes publicly about Zerodha's business model, challenges, and decisions. Employees can see the reasoning behind major choices.
- **Distributed teams:** Zerodha has teams in Bangalore, Chennai, and other cities. They've been remote-capable long before COVID.
- **No hierarchy for ideas:** Product ideas that become major features come from customer support teams, not just product managers.
- **Long-term thinking:** In a market where startups raised money to grow, Zerodha grew by compounding slowly. Employees who stayed were patient, which attracted patient employees.

The lesson: culture is the operating system of your company. Zerodha's culture enabled a business model (profitability-first, bootstrapped) that was impossible to execute with a different culture.

---

## Summary

- Culture is what you reward and punish, not what you write on walls
- It is set early — the first 20 employees' behaviors become the company's DNA
- Netflix's Culture Deck is a landmark document: study it, even if you disagree with parts
- Founders shape culture through their actions in difficult moments, not through speeches
- Toxic cultures (Uber, WeWork) are created by unchecked founder behavior combined with misaligned incentives
- Build deliberately: 5-7 specific values, hire against them, use them in real decisions
