## 1. Why Sales Is the Most Important Skill in Business (and Why People Get It Wrong)

Here is a question most people answer incorrectly: what is the single most important function in a company?

Common answers: product, technology, brand, operations. All of these matter. But none of them matter if you cannot get customers to pay you money.

**Sales is the engine that turns everything else into revenue.** You can have the best product in the world. If no one is buying it, you have a hobby, not a business.

This sounds obvious when you say it out loud, but a surprising number of founders and product-focused people underestimate sales — or worse, look down on it. There is a pervasive idea that great products sell themselves. This is almost never true.

Apple is often cited as proof that great products do not need salespeople. But Apple has one of the most sophisticated retail sales operations in the world. Their store employees go through weeks of training specifically on how to guide a customer from curiosity to purchase. The genius bars, the product demos, the "let me show you how this works" conversations — that is all sales. Apple just makes it feel like a natural part of the experience.

### The Most Common Misconception About Sales

Most people think sales means persuading someone to buy something they do not need, through pressure and manipulation. Think of the used car salesman in every movie — fast-talking, shady, pushing a lemon onto an unsuspecting buyer.

This image is both inaccurate and damaging, because it causes smart, ethical people to avoid learning how to sell.

**Real sales, done well, looks like this:**

You have a product or service that genuinely helps people. Your job is to identify the right people who have the problem your product solves, help them understand whether your solution is actually the right fit for them, answer their questions honestly, and make it easy for them to say yes — or to say no clearly, so neither of you wastes time.

That is it. Great salespeople are essentially expert problem-matchers. They find the intersection between "a person who has this specific problem" and "our product, which solves this specific problem."

### Why Sales Gets Complicated

The complication arises because not everyone with the problem is immediately aware they have it, or is aware that a solution exists, or is currently ready to buy, or has budget, or has the authority to make the decision.

This is why professional sales exists as a discipline. If selling were as simple as "find person with problem, show them product, they pay," companies would not spend billions on sales infrastructure.

The professional sales function is the system a company builds to navigate all that complexity — at scale, reproducibly, across hundreds or thousands of potential customers simultaneously.

> **What This Means in Practice**
>
> Stripe, the payments company, started with Patrick and John Collison literally manually going to potential customers and saying "let us set this up for you right now." That hands-on selling — understanding exactly what broke the experience for developers trying to accept payments online — is what shaped the product. Their first 100 customers came from direct, personal selling. Without that, there would have been no Stripe.

## 2. B2B vs B2C Sales — Completely Different Games

Before you can understand anything else about sales, you need to understand that there are fundamentally two different kinds:

**B2B** stands for Business-to-Business. You are selling to another company.

**B2C** stands for Business-to-Consumer. You are selling to an individual person.

These are not just slightly different versions of the same thing. They are almost completely different disciplines with different timeframes, different decision-making structures, different deal sizes, and different tactics.

| Dimension | B2C | B2B |
|---|---|---|
| Who decides | One person, usually | A committee (multiple stakeholders) |
| Deal size | Small (₹500 to ₹50,000 typically) | Large (₹1 lakh to ₹100+ crore) |
| Sales cycle | Minutes to days | Weeks to 18 months |
| Number of customers | Millions | Dozens to thousands |
| Primary driver | Emotion, convenience, price | ROI, security, business need |
| Channel | Ads, app stores, retail | Direct outreach, events, referrals |
| Relationship duration | Short or transactional | Long (contracts, renewals) |

### B2C Sales: Volume and Psychology

When Netflix convinces you to subscribe, they do not send you a salesperson. They show you a 30-day free trial, rely on their library of content to create desire, make sign-up frictionless (just a credit card), and count on inertia to keep you subscribed once you are in.

That is B2C at scale. The "sales" is the product experience itself, combined with marketing that creates awareness and desire. There are no individual salespeople calling 10 million potential subscribers.

B2C sales at the individual level happens in physical retail. When an Apple Store employee helps you decide between a MacBook Pro and a MacBook Air, that is B2C selling. The training is about asking the right questions to understand what you actually need — not pushing the most expensive product.

Zomato's B2C sales team is essentially the app's design, the discount notifications, and the Gold membership offer. Every nudge, recommendation, and "20% off your next order" is a sales action.

### B2B Sales: Complexity and Relationships

Zepto's sales to grocery brands is B2B. Zepto goes to a company like HUL (Hindustan Unilever) and says: "Put your products on our platform and we will handle hyperlocal delivery." That negotiation might take weeks. HUL's procurement team, legal team, finance team, and marketing team are all involved. The contract is complex.

Salesforce selling its CRM software to a large bank is B2B. The deal might be worth ₹20 crore per year. There are dozens of people on both sides of that negotiation. The sales cycle from first meeting to signed contract might be 12 months.

**The key insight:** In B2B, you are not convincing one person. You are navigating an organization where different people have different concerns, different levels of authority, and different definitions of success.

A champion inside the buying company might love your product — but their CFO has concerns about security. Their IT team is worried about integration complexity. Their legal team wants specific contract language. And their procurement team is using you as leverage to negotiate a lower price from your competitor.

Handling all of this simultaneously, without losing momentum, is the craft of B2B sales.

> **What This Means in Practice**
>
> When Sprinto (a compliance automation company) sells to a startup, they are not just talking to the founder. They are talking to the CTO (who cares about integration), the VP of Engineering (who needs to understand the technical workload), and the Head of Finance (who needs to justify the cost). The salesperson's job is to make sure all of those people feel heard and have their questions answered — not just close the founder.

## 3. The Sales Funnel — From Stranger to Customer

The sales funnel is the most important mental model in sales. It describes the journey a potential customer takes from first hearing about you to becoming a paying customer — and it shows you where people drop off at each stage.

It is called a funnel because you start with many people at the top and end up with fewer at the bottom. Most people who hear about your product will never buy it. That is normal. The art of sales is improving the ratio at each stage.

### A Standard B2B Sales Funnel

```
AWARENESS
(10,000 people see your LinkedIn ad or cold email)
         |
         ▼
INTEREST / LEAD
(500 people click or respond — 5% conversion)
         |
         ▼
QUALIFIED PROSPECT
(200 of those 500 are actually a good fit — 40% conversion)
         |
         ▼
DEMO / DISCOVERY CALL
(80 of those 200 agree to a call — 40% conversion)
         |
         ▼
PROPOSAL SENT
(40 of those 80 receive a formal proposal — 50% conversion)
         |
         ▼
NEGOTIATION
(20 of those 40 enter serious negotiation — 50% conversion)
         |
         ▼
CLOSED / WON
(10 of those 20 sign the contract — 50% conversion)
```

From 10,000 people at the top to 10 customers at the bottom — a 0.1% overall conversion rate. This is not bad. In B2B, this is roughly normal for cold outbound sales.

Every sales organization tracks their funnel obsessively because the numbers tell you where the process is broken. If you are getting tons of qualified prospects but very few demo calls, your outreach messages are weak. If you are getting lots of demos but almost no proposals, your discovery calls are not uncovering the right needs. If proposals are going out but no one is closing, your pricing or contract terms might be the problem.

### The Funnel Has Different Names at Different Companies

Different companies use different terminology for funnel stages, which can be confusing. Here is a mapping:

| Stage | Common names |
|---|---|
| Someone who knows about you | Prospect, Lead, Contact |
| Someone you have confirmed has a real need | Qualified Lead, MQL (Marketing Qualified Lead), SQL (Sales Qualified Lead) |
| Someone in active conversation | Opportunity, Deal |
| Someone who has received a proposal | Late-stage Opportunity |
| Someone who has signed | Customer, Closed-Won |
| Someone who said no | Closed-Lost |

"MQL" (Marketing Qualified Lead) means someone the marketing team has identified as showing interest — they downloaded a whitepaper, attended a webinar, or signed up for a trial. "SQL" (Sales Qualified Lead) means a salesperson has validated that this person has a real need, budget, and authority to buy.

## 4. Prospecting — How to Find People Who Might Buy

Prospecting is the work of finding potential customers. It is the top of the funnel. It is also often the hardest, most unglamorous part of sales — and the part that separates great sales teams from average ones.

There are two fundamental approaches:

### Inbound: They Come to You

Inbound leads are people who found you first. They searched on Google, read a blog post, saw a social post from someone who uses your product, or heard about you from a friend. They already know something about you and have raised their hand by visiting your website or signing up for something.

Inbound leads are gold. They are warmer, more informed, and more likely to convert than cold outbound leads. This is why companies invest heavily in content marketing, SEO, and brand building — to generate inbound interest.

HubSpot (the marketing and CRM software company) built their entire company on inbound marketing. They created a massive library of free educational content about marketing — blog posts, guides, certifications — that ranked highly in Google searches. When someone searched "how to do email marketing," they found HubSpot content, got value from it, and then considered HubSpot's software. That is inbound marketing generating inbound sales leads.

### Outbound: You Go to Them

Outbound prospecting is when your team actively reaches out to people who have not shown prior interest. This includes:

- **Cold calling:** Picking up the phone and calling a potential customer you have never spoken to
- **Cold emailing:** Sending a personalized email to someone who has not asked to hear from you
- **LinkedIn outreach:** Connecting with potential buyers on LinkedIn and starting a conversation
- **Events and conferences:** Attending industry events to meet potential customers in person
- **Warm introductions:** Asking your existing customers or network to introduce you to potential buyers

Cold outbound is hard. Most cold emails go unanswered. Most cold calls end in rejection within 30 seconds. But at scale, it works — and for many B2B companies, especially early-stage startups that do not yet have brand recognition, outbound is the only way to build a pipeline.

### How to Write a Good Cold Email

The single most common mistake in cold outreach is making the email about you.

**Bad cold email:**
> "Hi, I am from AcmeSoft. We are a leading provider of enterprise HR automation solutions with 500+ customers across India. We have won 3 industry awards and have a 97% customer satisfaction rating. Our platform helps companies streamline their HR workflows. I would love to schedule a 30-minute call to show you a demo."

This email is entirely about the sender. It gives the recipient no reason to care.

**Good cold email:**
> "Hi Priya, I noticed Razorpay has grown from 200 to 500 employees in the last 12 months — congrats. At that scale, most finance teams I talk to spend 15+ hours a week manually reconciling expense reports. Is that something your team runs into? Happy to share what's worked for a few similar-sized companies."

This email is about the recipient's world, a specific relevant observation, and a question that invites a response. It does not oversell. It opens a conversation.

### What Makes a Good Prospect?

Not everyone with a pulse is a good prospect. Chasing the wrong people wastes everyone's time. Professional sales teams define an Ideal Customer Profile (ICP) — the characteristics that make someone likely to buy, likely to get value, and likely to stay.

An ICP typically includes:

```
Company size: 50-500 employees
Industry: SaaS, fintech, or e-commerce
Location: India
Role of buyer: CTO or VP Engineering
Pain trigger: Recently closed a Series B or expanded their team rapidly
Technology stack: Uses AWS + GitHub (signals tech-forward company)
```

This level of specificity lets your team focus energy on the people most likely to convert, rather than spraying outreach broadly.

## 5. The Sales Call — What Actually Happens

A sales call is not a pitch. This is the most important correction to make. Beginners think a sales call is the moment you show off your product and convince someone to buy. Professionals know a sales call is primarily about listening.

### Before the Call

Preparation is what separates a 20% close rate from a 5% close rate. Before any serious sales call, a good salesperson will:

1. **Research the company** — What does this company do? How big are they? Have they been in the news recently? Did they raise money? Are they hiring (which tells you about growth and where they are investing)?
2. **Research the person** — What is their role? How long have they been there? What did they post on LinkedIn recently? Do they have any public talks or interviews?
3. **Understand likely pain points** — Based on the company's situation, what problems does your product typically solve for similar companies?
4. **Prepare your discovery questions** — What do you need to learn in this call to understand if there is a real fit?

Showing up unprepared to a sales call is disrespectful of the prospect's time and signals that you do not take the relationship seriously.

### During the Call: Discovery

The first part of any sales call — and often the entire first call — should be discovery. This is where you ask questions to understand the prospect's situation, goals, and problems.

**Good discovery questions:**

- "Can you walk me through how you currently handle [the process your product addresses]?"
- "What does the biggest friction point in that process look like for your team?"
- "How are you measuring success in that area today?"
- "What have you tried before? What worked and what did not?"
- "If this problem were solved, what would that mean for you personally / for the team?"
- "Is there a timeline you are working toward? Is there a reason this is coming up now?"
- "Who else is involved in thinking about this problem?"

Notice that none of these questions are about your product. You are mapping their world before you say a word about your solution.

The classic mistake beginners make is jumping straight to the demo. "Let me show you what we do!" — and then they give a generic product walkthrough that does not connect to anything the prospect cares about.

If you spend 20 minutes in discovery first, you know exactly which parts of your product are relevant, which pain points to emphasize, and how to frame your solution in terms of what they have told you matters to them.

### Handling Objections

An objection is a reason the prospect gives for not buying. Objections are not rejections. They are questions in disguise.

| Objection | What it usually really means |
|---|---|
| "It is too expensive." | "I cannot see enough value to justify this price." |
| "We are not ready right now." | "This is not a priority for us." |
| "We already have a solution for this." | "I need to understand what is different about yours." |
| "I need to think about it." | "I have an unspoken concern I am not ready to share." |
| "I need to talk to my team." | "I am not the final decision-maker, or I am not convinced enough to push for it internally." |

The technique for handling objections is not to argue or pressure. It is to acknowledge, explore, and address.

**Example — handling the price objection:**

Prospect: "Honestly, this feels too expensive for us right now."

Bad response: "But we offer so much more than our competitors! Our platform has 50 features and has won three awards..."

Good response: "That is totally fair feedback. Can I ask — when you say it feels expensive, is it more about the absolute number not fitting your budget right now, or about not being sure the ROI would be there? Because those are two different things and I want to make sure I understand."

This question distinguishes between a real budget constraint and a value perception problem. They have completely different solutions. If it is a budget problem, you might offer a smaller starting package. If it is a value problem, you need to paint a clearer picture of the ROI.

### Closing: The Moment of Decision

Closing is the part of the sales process where you ask the prospect to commit. This is where most people get uncomfortable, because it feels like you are putting someone on the spot.

But here is the reframe: if you have done good discovery, understood their problem, shown them a relevant solution, and handled their objections honestly — asking for the decision is a natural next step. You are not manipulating anyone. You are helping them make a decision they already want to make.

**Non-manipulative closing techniques:**

**The Direct Ask:**
> "Based on everything we have discussed, does this seem like the right solution for what you are trying to solve? If so, I can send over the contract today."

**The Next Step Ask:**
> "I know you want to talk to your CTO before deciding. Can we schedule a call with the three of us this week so I can answer their questions directly?"

**The Summary Close:**
> "So you need to solve the reconciliation problem before Q3, you have budget for ₹8-10 lakhs, and the main concern is integration with your existing system. We have handled all three of those. What would you need to feel confident moving forward?"

What you do not do: pressure, create false urgency ("this offer expires today!"), or guilt someone into a commitment.

### After the Call

The call is not over when the call ends. Follow-up is where deals are won and lost.

Within 2 hours of the call, send an email that:
1. Thanks them for their time
2. Summarizes what you discussed (their situation, their needs, the agreed next steps)
3. Includes any materials you promised
4. States clearly what happens next ("I will send the proposal by Friday. You will review it with your team and we will reconnect on Tuesday.")

This confirms alignment, shows professionalism, and creates a written record that is useful for everyone.

## 6. The Sales Process — MEDDIC and Other Qualification Frameworks

A qualification framework is a structured way of assessing whether a prospect is actually worth pursuing, and where you are in the sales process. They are used by sales teams to make sure everyone is evaluating opportunities the same way.

The most famous is MEDDIC (sometimes written MEDDPICC). It was developed at PTC, a software company, in the 1990s and has been adopted by hundreds of enterprise sales teams since.

### MEDDIC Explained

```
M — Metrics
    What are the quantifiable business outcomes the buyer wants?
    ("We need to reduce audit prep time from 3 weeks to 3 days.")
    
E — Economic Buyer
    Who has the authority to actually approve the spend?
    (Not just who you talk to — who can write the check?)
    
D — Decision Criteria
    What are the criteria by which they will evaluate and decide?
    ("Security, integration complexity, and price in that order.")
    
D — Decision Process
    What is the actual process they will go through to make a decision?
    ("Demo → security review → legal review → CFO approval → contract.")
    
I — Identify Pain
    What is the specific, concrete pain this deal is addressing?
    (Generic pain = weak deal. Specific, owned pain = strong deal.)
    
C — Champion
    Who inside the buying company is advocating for your solution internally?
    (A deal without a champion is a deal that will die quietly.)
```

### Why MEDDIC Matters

Without a framework like this, salespeople fall into the trap of "happy ears" — mistaking pleasant conversations for progress. A prospect who enjoys your demos but has not identified their champion, has not defined their decision process, and has no economic buyer in the room is a prospect who will string you along for months and then go quiet.

MEDDIC forces honesty. At any point in a deal, a sales manager can ask: "Do you know who the economic buyer is? Have you met them?" If the answer is no, that deal is at risk regardless of how enthusiastic the prospect sounds.

### Other Frameworks

**SPIN Selling** (Neil Rackham): A framework for structuring questions in order — Situation, Problem, Implication, Need-Payoff. It leads the prospect to articulate their own pain rather than having the salesperson describe it to them.

**BANT**: An older, simpler framework — Budget, Authority, Need, Timeline. Does the prospect have budget? Are they the decision-maker? Do they have a real need? Is there a timeline?

**Challenger Sale**: Research from CEB (now Gartner) found that the best B2B salespeople are not relationship-builders — they are "Challengers" who teach prospects something they did not know about their own business, tailor their message to specific stakeholders, and take control of the sales conversation. The insight: buyers respect salespeople who bring new ideas, not just pleasant personalities.

## 7. CRM — Customer Relationship Management

A CRM is software that tracks every interaction your sales team has with every prospect and customer. It is the operational backbone of any serious sales organization.

The two most dominant CRMs globally are Salesforce and HubSpot.

### What a CRM Actually Does

Think of a CRM as a structured database where you store:
- Every company and contact your team interacts with
- Every email, call, and meeting that happened
- Where each deal currently sits in the funnel
- What the next step is and who owns it
- How much each deal is worth
- When deals are expected to close

Without a CRM, this information lives in salespeople's heads, on spreadsheets, and in email inboxes — fragmented, lost when someone leaves, and invisible to the sales manager trying to forecast next quarter's revenue.

### Salesforce: The Dominant Enterprise CRM

Salesforce was founded in 1999 by Marc Benioff and famously launched with a "No Software" campaign — it was one of the first enterprise tools delivered as a cloud application rather than installed software.

Today, Salesforce has over $34 billion in annual revenue and is used by the majority of Fortune 500 companies. Their CRM stores information on billions of sales conversations and customer relationships.

What Salesforce does well: extreme flexibility and an enormous ecosystem of integrations. It can be configured to match almost any sales process.

What Salesforce struggles with: it is complex and expensive to configure. Many mid-size companies buy Salesforce and then do not fully implement it because the setup requires consultants and months of work.

### HubSpot: The SMB Challenger

HubSpot started as a marketing software company (the inbound marketing pioneer mentioned earlier) and built a CRM on top of their marketing tools. It is significantly simpler to set up than Salesforce and has a generous free tier that makes it accessible to startups.

For a company with 1-100 salespeople, HubSpot is often the better choice. For a large enterprise with complex, customized sales processes, Salesforce tends to win.

### What Good CRM Usage Looks Like

A salesperson updates their CRM after every interaction. Every call has a note. Every email is logged. Every deal has a next step with a date. Every contact record has the right information.

The result: a sales manager can log in on Monday morning and see the state of every deal in the pipeline — what is at risk, what is close to closing, what has gone quiet. The forecast for the quarter is derived from this data.

Bad CRM usage — which is unfortunately common — is when salespeople treat CRM as a chore they do poorly and irregularly, leaving inaccurate data that makes the tool useless.

## 8. Building a Sales Team — SDRs, AEs, Customer Success

Mature sales organizations are not one role — they are a pipeline of specialized roles, each owning a specific part of the customer journey.

### SDR — Sales Development Representative

An SDR is the entry-level role in B2B sales. Their job is exclusively prospecting and qualification — generating and qualifying leads for the salespeople who close deals.

An SDR's daily life looks like:
- Sending 50-100 cold emails
- Making 20-40 cold calls
- Responding to inbound leads to assess fit
- Booking qualified meetings for Account Executives

SDRs typically do not close deals themselves. They exist to fill the top of the funnel so that closers are not spending their time on cold outreach.

**Why split this role?** Because the skills required to do relentless cold outreach (resilience, volume, scripted efficiency) are different from the skills required to run a complex enterprise deal (strategic thinking, relationship management, negotiation). Splitting the role lets each type of person optimize for what they are good at.

### AE — Account Executive

The AE is the closer. They take qualified leads from the SDR or from inbound channels and run the full sales cycle from first conversation through contract signature.

In enterprise sales, an AE might work on 10-20 deals simultaneously over 6-12 month cycles. In SMB sales, an AE might close 5-10 deals per week.

AEs are typically compensated with a base salary plus a commission on deals closed. The higher the deal value, the more complex the role, and the higher the potential earnings.

### Customer Success Manager (CSM)

This role does not exist in most traditional industries. It was invented by SaaS companies and is now one of the most important roles in any recurring revenue business.

The CSM's job begins when the sale ends. Their responsibility is ensuring that the customer actually gets value from the product — which is the only thing that prevents churn (customers canceling their subscription) and drives expansion (customers buying more over time).

Why does this matter so much? Because in a subscription business, a customer who churns after one month costs you the acquisition cost without ever delivering lifetime value. A customer who stays and expands from ₹5 lakh/year to ₹20 lakh/year over three years is worth enormously more.

The metric CSMs are often held to is NRR — Net Revenue Retention. This measures whether your existing customer base is growing or shrinking in revenue terms, independent of new customer acquisition.

```
NRR = (Starting MRR + Expansion - Churn - Downgrades) / Starting MRR × 100%

NRR > 100% = Your existing customers are spending more over time (excellent)
NRR = 100% = Expansion is exactly offsetting churn (okay)
NRR < 100% = You are losing revenue from existing customers (bad)
```

The best SaaS companies — Snowflake, Twilio, Datadog — have historically maintained NRR above 120-130%. This means they grow revenue even if they acquire zero new customers, because existing customers keep buying more.

### Sales Engineer / Solutions Engineer

In technical product sales, there is often a dedicated Solutions Engineer (SE) who accompanies the AE on calls. The AE handles the relationship and the commercial aspects; the SE handles technical questions, builds proof-of-concept integrations, and reassures the customer's technical team.

### A Simplified Sales Org Chart

```
VP of Sales
├── Sales Development Manager
│   ├── SDR (5-10 people)
│   └── SDR (...)
├── Enterprise AE Team
│   ├── Enterprise AE (3-5 people)
│   └── Solutions Engineers
├── Mid-Market AE Team
│   └── Mid-Market AE (5-10 people)
└── Customer Success Team
    └── CSMs (1 per 50-100 customers, depending on complexity)
```

## 9. Sales Compensation — How Salespeople Are Paid and Why It Matters

Sales is one of the few roles in most companies where compensation is directly, explicitly tied to individual output. This creates powerful incentives — and some dangerous misalignments if not designed carefully.

### The On-Target Earnings (OTE) Model

Most sales roles are paid with an OTE structure:

```
OTE = Base Salary + On-Target Commission

Example:
Base salary: ₹15 lakhs/year
On-target commission: ₹15 lakhs/year (if quota is fully achieved)
OTE: ₹30 lakhs/year
```

"On-target" means the commission they earn if they hit 100% of their sales quota. The quota is the target they are assigned — say, ₹2 crore in new business per year.

If they close ₹2 crore, they earn the full ₹15 lakh commission. If they close ₹1 crore (50% of quota), they earn ₹7.5 lakh in commission. If they close ₹3 crore (150% of quota), they might earn ₹22.5 lakh (companies typically offer accelerated commissions above 100% to reward overperformance).

### Why Compensation Design Matters

Sales compensation is not just a cost to the company — it is the behavioral blueprint of the sales team. Whatever you reward, people will optimize for.

**Example of bad incentive design:**

A company pays commissions when a deal closes, with no clawback if the customer churns within 90 days. Result: salespeople close deals aggressively, make promises the product cannot keep, and move on. The customer churns. The company has paid the commission but lost the revenue. Customer Success is left dealing with an angry customer who was oversold.

**Better design:**

Commission is paid in two installments — 50% when the deal closes, 50% when the customer renews at 6 months. Now the salesperson has a financial incentive to close the right customers and set them up for success.

### SDR vs AE Compensation

SDRs are paid mostly base salary with smaller bonuses tied to meetings booked or qualified opportunities generated. Because they do not close deals, commission on closed revenue does not work for them.

AEs have higher commission as a percentage of their total compensation — the best AEs in enterprise sales can earn 2-3x their base salary in commission in a strong year.

## 10. How Startups Build Their First Sales (Founder-Led Sales)

Every successful B2B startup goes through the same unavoidable phase: the founders do all the selling themselves.

This is called founder-led sales, and it is not just a temporary inconvenience — it is a strategic necessity.

### Why Founders Must Sell First

**Reason 1: Learning.** When a founder is on a sales call, every objection they hear is product feedback. Every question a prospect asks reveals what is unclear about the positioning. Every deal that falls through because of a missing feature is a product roadmap input. A hired salesperson filters this information through their own interpretation before it reaches the founders. Founders hearing it directly is irreplaceable.

**Reason 2: Credibility.** In early-stage sales, especially with larger buyers, nothing signals confidence in the product like having the person who built it selling it. A founder on a call says implicitly: "I believe in this enough to sell it myself."

**Reason 3: Understanding the repeatable motion.** Before you hire salespeople, you need to know what works. What message resonates? What ICP converts? What objections come up repeatedly and how do you handle them? You cannot write a sales playbook until you have figured this out through your own experience.

### What Founder-Led Sales Looks Like

In the early days of Freshworks (now a $5 billion company), founder Girish Mathrubootham did almost all of the early customer conversations himself. Not because he lacked the funds to hire salespeople, but because he needed to understand why people would (and would not) buy before he could teach others to sell.

Similarly, when Zepto launched, Aadit Palicha and Kaivalya Vohra spent enormous time on the phone with early grocery brands and dark store operators — understanding their economics and objections — before building a proper supply team.

The job of the founder-seller is to find the first 10-20 customers, understand the pattern of why they bought, distill that into a repeatable sales process, and then hire the first salesperson to execute that process. The worst mistake is hiring salespeople before you know what they are supposed to do.

### When to Hire Your First Salesperson

The general guideline from experienced founders and investors (Paul Graham of Y Combinator, for example) is: do not hire your first salesperson until you have closed at least 5-10 customers yourself. If you cannot sell the product yourself, you do not yet understand why people buy it — and you cannot train someone else to sell it.

When you do hire, hire someone slightly more junior than you think you need. A less experienced salesperson following a clear playbook is far more valuable than a senior salesperson trying to figure out the process themselves.

## 11. Enterprise Sales vs SMB Sales vs PLG (Product-Led Growth)

Not all B2B companies sell the same way. There are three fundamentally different go-to-market approaches, and the right one depends on your product, your buyer, and your price point.

### Enterprise Sales: High Touch, Long Cycles

Enterprise sales targets large companies with complex needs and large budgets. Deals are worth ₹50 lakh to ₹100+ crore. Sales cycles are 6-18 months. There are multiple stakeholders and a formal procurement process.

This is how Salesforce, SAP, Oracle, and most large software companies sell. The ROI for a 12-month sales cycle only makes sense when the deal is large enough.

**The economics of enterprise sales:**

If an AE closes 6 enterprise deals per year at ₹1 crore each, they have generated ₹6 crore in annual recurring revenue. If that AE costs the company ₹80 lakh/year (salary + commission), the ROI is clear.

But enterprise sales requires significant overhead: specialized AEs, solutions engineers, legal and procurement expertise, executive relationship management. It is a high-cost, high-reward motion.

### SMB Sales: Volume and Efficiency

SMB (Small and Medium Business) sales targets smaller companies with smaller budgets. Deals might be ₹1-20 lakh/year. Sales cycles are 1-6 weeks. One decision-maker can usually say yes without a committee.

The key to SMB sales is efficiency. You are not running a 12-month multi-stakeholder process — you are running a high-volume, streamlined motion where an AE might close 5-10 deals per week.

This requires:
- Simple, self-serve elements to reduce time per deal
- Standard contracts with minimal negotiation
- Fast demos (30 minutes, not 2 hours)
- Transparent, published pricing

Freshdesk, Zoho, and Chargebee are examples of Indian B2B companies that have built effective SMB sales motions.

### PLG — Product-Led Growth: The Product Does the Selling

PLG is a go-to-market strategy where the product itself is the primary vehicle for acquisition, activation, and expansion. Instead of a salesperson reaching out and giving a demo, the user signs up, tries the product, finds value on their own, and eventually converts to paid — all without ever talking to a human.

Slack is the canonical example. Slack entered companies through individuals who found it useful. Those individuals shared it with their teams. Teams adopted it. IT departments eventually noticed that 1,000 people were using free Slack accounts and signed an enterprise contract. Sales was not the entry point — the product was.

Other PLG examples:
- **Notion:** Free personal plan, viral sharing of documents and templates, teams upgrade to paid
- **Figma:** Designers started using free Figma, shared designs with colleagues, companies eventually standardized on it
- **Zoom:** Free video calls spread through organizations until enterprises paid for enterprise plans
- **Dropbox:** Referral program gave both parties extra storage, leading to viral growth

**The PLG funnel is different from a traditional sales funnel:**

```
Traditional: Awareness → Lead → Demo → Proposal → Close
PLG: Sign up → Activate (find first value) → Habit → Team adoption → Paid conversion
```

PLG does not eliminate sales — it changes when and how sales enters. In a PLG company, the sales team focuses on "expansion" within accounts that are already using the product, rather than convincing cold prospects. This is sometimes called product-led sales.

| Dimension | Enterprise Sales | SMB Sales | PLG |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deal size | ₹50L - ₹10Cr+ | ₹1L - ₹20L | ₹5K - ₹5L |
| Sales cycle | 6-18 months | 1-6 weeks | Immediate - 3 months |
| Human touch required | Very high | Medium | Low initially |
| Marketing role | Brand, events, content | SEO, paid ads | Viral, referral, in-product |
| Best for | Complex enterprise software | Tools with clear ROI | Collaborative/viral products |
| Examples | SAP, Salesforce | Freshdesk, Zoho | Slack, Notion, Figma |

## 12. Summary

Here is what you learned in this chapter:

1. **Sales is not manipulation.** Done right, sales is matching a product that solves a real problem with the people who have that problem. The best salespeople are problem-matchers, not pressure artists.

2. **B2B and B2C are completely different.** B2B involves larger deals, longer cycles, multiple stakeholders, and relationship-intensive selling. B2C involves volume, psychology, and the product experience as the primary sales vehicle.

3. **The sales funnel** describes the journey from stranger to customer. Most people drop off at every stage. The job of sales is to improve conversion rates at each step.

4. **Prospecting** is finding people who might buy. Inbound leads (who come to you) are warmer and easier to convert than outbound leads (who you cold-reach). An Ideal Customer Profile focuses your energy on the right targets.

5. **The sales call** is mostly about listening. Discovery questions come before demos. Objections are questions in disguise. Closing is asking for a decision — not pressuring.

6. **MEDDIC** and other qualification frameworks force sales teams to be honest about where a deal really stands: who the economic buyer is, whether there is real pain, and whether there is a champion internally.

7. **CRM software** (Salesforce, HubSpot) is the operational backbone of sales. It makes the pipeline visible, forecasting possible, and handoffs smooth.

8. **Sales teams have specialized roles:** SDRs (prospectors), AEs (closers), and CSMs (retention and expansion). Each role requires different skills and different compensation structures.

9. **Compensation design shapes behavior.** Commissions aligned with customer success produce better long-term outcomes than commissions aligned purely with deal volume.

10. **Founder-led sales** is mandatory before hiring salespeople. Founders must understand why customers buy before they can teach others to sell.

11. **Three GTM motions:** Enterprise (high-touch, large deals), SMB (volume, efficiency), and PLG (product as the primary acquisition mechanism). The right choice depends on your product and buyer.
