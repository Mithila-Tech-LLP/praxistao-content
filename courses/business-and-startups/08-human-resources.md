# Chapter 08: Human Resources — Hiring, Managing, and Growing a Team

---

## Before We Start — The Uncomfortable Truth About People

There is a classic mistake that almost every first-time founder makes. They are great at building a product, so they assume the business will work if the product is good enough. They spend months obsessing over features, design, and performance — and they treat hiring as an afterthought. Something to do later, when there's more money, more time, more certainty.

This is a catastrophic error.

The companies you admire — Apple, Netflix, Zomato, Stripe — are not successful because they had better ideas than everyone else. Ideas are cheap. Execution is everything. And execution is done by people.

Steve Jobs said: "The secret of my success is that we have gone to exceptional lengths to hire the best people in the world." Not the most experienced. Not the cheapest. The best — for what Apple needed at that moment. Netflix's culture document, which has been read over 20 million times, says the most important talent management principle is: keep only your best performers, and pay them generously to stay.

This chapter is about the full lifecycle of people in a company — how to find them, how to bring them in, how to help them do their best work, how to pay them fairly, and yes, how to part ways when it doesn't work out. It's also about the legal side of HR in India, which will matter to you the moment you hire your first employee.

Human resources is not a bureaucratic department. It is the operating system of your company. Get it wrong, and nothing else works.

## 1. Why People Are a Company's Most Important Asset (and Most Difficult Challenge)

Let's start with a simple truth: a business is nothing but a collection of people with a shared goal.

A factory without workers is just a building. Software without engineers is just a server running nothing. A brand without designers, marketers, and salespeople is just a name.

People build things. People sell things. People fix things when they break. People have ideas that create new products, cut costs, open new markets. Every euro of revenue, every line of code, every happy customer — it all traces back to a person who made a decision or took an action.

But here's the paradox: people are also the most unpredictable, expensive, and emotionally complex thing you will ever manage.

A machine does exactly what you program it to do. A person brings their mood, their ambitions, their fears, their relationships outside of work, and their judgment — all of which you cannot control. People need to be motivated, not just instructed. They need context, not just tasks. They need to feel like the work matters, not just that they showed up.

### The Cost of Getting People Wrong

Hiring a wrong person is shockingly expensive. Here's a rough breakdown:

| Cost Type | What It Actually Involves |
|---|---|
| Recruiting cost | Job ads, recruiter fees (usually 8–15% of first-year salary), time spent interviewing |
| Onboarding cost | Salary paid during training when output is low, manager time, equipment setup |
| Opportunity cost | The work that didn't get done because the role was empty or filled poorly |
| Team disruption cost | Morale damage, productivity drop when the bad hire affects others |
| Severance cost | Notice period, any separation pay, legal risk if termination is handled badly |

Studies consistently suggest that replacing a mid-level employee costs 50–200% of their annual salary. A bad hire at the senior level can cost much more — not just in money, but in the deals that fell through, the features that shipped broken, or the good employees who quit because they couldn't stand working under a bad manager.

This is why HR is serious work.

## 2. The Hiring Process — From Job Description to Offer Letter

Hiring is a sales process. You are convincing someone to spend the next several years of their life working on your problem. The best candidates have options. They are evaluating you just as hard as you are evaluating them.

Most startups treat hiring like a filter: post a job, wait for applications, filter out the bad ones, pick the least-bad one who is willing to join. This is exactly backwards. The best companies treat hiring like a hunt: define exactly who you need, go find them, build a relationship, and make joining feel like the obvious right decision.

### Writing Job Descriptions That Attract the Right People

Most job descriptions are terrible. They are a wish list disguised as a requirement list. "5+ years of experience, degree from a top university, expertise in 12 different technologies, able to work under pressure." This scares away excellent candidates who don't tick every box and attracts people who are good at looking good on paper.

A good job description does four things:

1. **Explains what the person will actually do** — not vague responsibilities, but real outcomes. "You will own the onboarding funnel, reduce drop-off from 45% to under 25% in six months, and build a team of 3 beneath you."

2. **Explains what success looks like** — so the candidate can self-select. If the job requires someone who works best independently with minimal oversight, say that. If it requires someone who thrives in chaos, say that. The right person will lean forward; the wrong person will self-select out.

3. **Explains what you offer** — salary range (yes, actually say it), equity, remote flexibility, learning opportunities. Companies that hide compensation waste everyone's time and signal that they have something to hide.

4. **Shows some personality** — a job description that sounds like it was written by a legal robot will attract people who want to work in a legal robot company. If your culture is direct and human, write like a direct human.

### Screening and Interviews

The goal of screening is to quickly disqualify people who are clearly wrong so you can spend your time on people who might be right.

**Resume screening:** Look for evidence of doing, not credentials. "Led a team that grew revenue from ₹2 crore to ₹18 crore in 18 months" tells you far more than "MBA from IIM." The work is what matters.

**Phone screen:** A 20-minute call to check basic communication skills, confirm the candidate understands the role, and verify the practical logistics (compensation expectations, start date, location). Don't waste a full interview slot on someone who wants double your budget.

**Skills assessment:** Depending on the role, give a real task. A product manager should be given a case — here is a messy problem, tell us how you'd approach it. An engineer should write some code. A designer should show process, not just portfolio. Keep assessments short (2–4 hours maximum), and pay candidates for their time if you're asking for more than an hour of work. Unpaid assessments are a red flag to the best candidates.

**Deep interviews:** These should be structured, not freeform. Ask every candidate the same core questions so you can compare fairly. Use behavioral questions: "Tell me about a time when you had to make a difficult decision with incomplete information. What did you do?" Behavioral questions reveal how people actually think, not how they perform in a hypothetical.

**Reference checks:** Most people treat references as a formality. That's wrong. Call the references — don't just email — and ask specific questions: "What would you say is their single biggest weakness? What kind of manager do they need to do their best work? Would you hire them again?" References who pause before answering, or who give generic praise, are telling you something.

### Making the Offer

When you find the right person, move fast. Good candidates have other offers. Delay communicates that you are either disorganized or not that excited about them.

An offer letter should clearly state:

```
Role title
Reporting line (who they report to)
Start date
Base salary (annual CTC breakdown in India)
Variable pay / bonus structure
ESOP grant (if applicable — more on this below)
Notice period
Probation period and its terms
Any clauses (non-compete, confidentiality)
```

A verbal offer is not enough. Always follow up in writing within 24 hours. And when someone accepts, celebrate it — send a welcome email, introduce them to the team on Slack, make them feel like they made a great decision before they even walk in the door.

## 3. Onboarding — The First 90 Days

Onboarding is one of the most underinvested areas in most companies. Companies spend weeks and significant money finding someone, and then they show up on their first day to discover there's no laptop, no logins, and nobody expecting them. Their manager is in three back-to-back meetings. They sit awkwardly at a desk for four hours.

This is not a joke. This happens constantly. And it sets the tone for everything that follows.

The first 90 days are when a new employee forms their opinion of the company, figures out who the important people are, learns how things actually work (as opposed to how they were described in the interview), and decides whether they made the right decision joining.

**What good onboarding looks like:**

| Week | Focus |
|---|---|
| Week 1 | Context and relationships — company mission, product walkthrough, meet everyone, understand the org structure |
| Weeks 2–4 | Orientation to the role — how the team works, tools and processes, first small tasks that build confidence |
| Month 2 | Starting to contribute — first real project, feedback loops established, clear short-term goals |
| Month 3 | Independent work — operating with reasonable autonomy, understanding how success is measured, check-in with manager on longer-term growth |

Netflix has a concept called "freedom and responsibility" — they give people enormous autonomy, but that autonomy only works if people have context. Onboarding at Netflix is heavily focused on making sure every person understands the business, the strategy, and the reasoning behind decisions. The freedom makes sense once you have the context.

Zepto, the quick-commerce startup that grew from zero to ₹10,000+ crore in revenue in just a few years, operates at an insane pace. Their onboarding is designed around throwing people into real problems fast — with a buddy system so no one drowns. The pace is self-selecting: if someone is overwhelmed by week two, they probably aren't the right fit for the culture.

## 4. Performance Management — How to Help People Do Their Best Work

Performance management sounds like a bureaucratic HR process. In practice, it's the answer to one question: how do you make sure the right things are getting done, and that people are growing while they do them?

Done well, performance management is the thing that turns a group of talented individuals into a high-functioning team. Done badly, it's a checkbox exercise that wastes time and damages morale.

### OKRs and KPIs — Two Ways to Measure Work

These two terms get confused constantly. Here's the simple version:

**KPIs (Key Performance Indicators)** are metrics you track on an ongoing basis to understand the health of the business. Think of them as your vital signs — heart rate, blood pressure. For a sales team, KPIs might be: number of calls made per day, conversion rate, average deal size, churn rate. These are always running.

**OKRs (Objectives and Key Results)** are goals you set for a specific period — usually a quarter — to make progress toward something important. OKRs are more like a fitness goal: "I want to run a 10K in under 55 minutes in three months." Objectives are qualitative and inspirational. Key Results are measurable and time-bound.

An example for a product team:

```
Objective: Make our checkout experience so good that users brag about it.

Key Results:
  - Reduce checkout drop-off rate from 38% to under 20%
  - Increase NPS (Net Promoter Score) for checkout from 34 to 55
  - Launch saved-address feature with >70% adoption in first 30 days
```

Google famously runs its entire company on OKRs, a system they learned from Intel. Flipkart, Zomato, and most serious Indian tech startups have adopted OKRs. The discipline of setting them forces clarity on priorities — which is more valuable than the tracking itself.

The common mistake is to set too many OKRs. If everything is a priority, nothing is. Three to five OKRs per team per quarter is the right range.

### Performance Reviews — And Why Most of Them Are Broken

The traditional performance review is a biannual or annual meeting where a manager writes an evaluation, discusses it with the employee, assigns a rating, and ties it to a pay decision. In theory, this is useful. In practice, it is widely considered one of the most despised processes in corporate life.

Here's why it breaks:

**Recency bias.** The manager remembers the last two months, not the whole year. If you had a bad November, it doesn't matter that January through September were excellent.

**Manager skill.** Writing a fair, useful performance review requires significant skill and time. Most managers don't have either. They write generic, vague feedback that tells people nothing useful.

**It's tied to money.** When a review determines whether you get a raise, people stop being honest. Managers soften bad feedback to avoid conflict. Employees manage up rather than doing good work.

**Annual cadence is too slow.** If you're doing something wrong, you should hear it in week two, not month twelve.

Better-run companies have moved toward continuous feedback: regular one-on-one meetings between manager and report (weekly or biweekly), real-time feedback on specific projects, and separate conversations about compensation and about development. Netflix, for example, does not have traditional performance reviews — they rely on continuous manager feedback and a "keeper test" (would I fight hard to keep this person if they were thinking of leaving?).

The point of performance management is not to rate people. It is to help people do their best work and grow in their careers. Keep that as the goal, and the process will serve you.

## 5. Compensation and Benefits — How Pay Works

Money is not the only reason people work. But it is the reason they leave. If you underpay someone, they will eventually find someone who pays them what they're worth. All your investment in onboarding, training, and culture walks out the door with them.

### Understanding the Components of Pay

In India, compensation is typically discussed as CTC — Cost to Company. CTC is the total of everything the company spends on you as an employee, which is not the same as what lands in your bank account.

A CTC of ₹12 lakh per year might break down like this:

```
Monthly Breakdown:
  Basic Salary:           ₹40,000/month
  HRA (House Rent Allowance): ₹20,000/month
  Special Allowance:      ₹20,000/month
  PF Employer Contribution: ₹4,800/month
  Gratuity:               ₹1,923/month
  Annual bonus target:    ₹13,440/month equivalent

Total CTC: ₹12,00,000/year
In-hand (take-home): ~₹72,000–80,000/month (after employee PF deduction and tax)
```

Understanding this breakdown matters because it's easy to be confused or misled by CTC numbers. Always ask for the take-home estimate.

**Base salary** is the fixed monthly amount you receive regardless of performance. This is your financial floor.

**Bonus** is variable pay tied to some combination of company performance and individual performance. Bonuses can be quarterly or annual. A 10% performance bonus sounds great — but if the company misses its targets, you might get 0%.

**Benefits** include things like health insurance (for you and sometimes your family), food coupons (which are tax-advantaged), internet allowance for remote workers, learning and development budgets, and gym memberships. These have real monetary value but are often overlooked by candidates.

### ESOP — Employee Stock Options

This one is worth understanding in depth because it can be the most valuable part of your compensation if you join the right startup at the right time.

**What is an ESOP?**

An ESOP (Employee Stock Option Plan) gives you the right — but not the obligation — to buy shares of the company at a fixed price (called the strike price or exercise price) at a future date. You're not getting shares right now. You're getting an option to buy shares later, at today's price.

Here's why this matters: if the company grows significantly, those shares become worth much more than you paid for them. The difference is your profit.

**A simple example:**

Imagine you join a startup in 2024 that grants you 10,000 options at a strike price of ₹10 per share. You have options worth ₹1,00,000 on paper at the time you receive them.

By 2028, the company has grown significantly. The share price is now ₹200 per share. You exercise your options — you pay ₹10 per share for 10,000 shares (₹1,00,000 total), and you receive shares now worth ₹20,00,000. Your gain: ₹19,00,000 before tax.

**Vesting:** You don't get all your options immediately. They vest over time — typically over 4 years with a 1-year cliff. The "cliff" means you must stay at the company for at least 1 year before any options vest. After the cliff, the remaining options vest monthly or quarterly.

```
Standard vesting schedule:
  Year 0–1: 0 options vest (cliff period)
  End of Year 1: 25% vest (cliff)
  Year 1–4: remaining 75% vest monthly (2.08% per month)
  End of Year 4: 100% vested
```

**How Indian Startups Use ESOPs:**

Several high-profile examples show how powerful ESOPs can be:

- When **Zomato** went public in 2021, hundreds of early employees became crorepatis overnight. Employees who had joined at ₹8–12 lakh CTC saw their ESOPs vest into crores of rupees.
- **Zepto**, **Meesho**, **Razorpay**, and **CRED** have all used aggressive ESOP policies to compete for talent against larger companies that pay higher base salaries. They pitch equity upside as the differentiator.
- **Flipkart** employees who had ESOPs when Walmart acquired Flipkart for $16 billion in 2018 received life-changing payouts.

The risk: ESOPs are worth nothing if the company doesn't do well. Many startups that seemed promising in 2019–2021 have shut down or stagnated. Those employees' ESOPs expired worthless.

The principle: ESOPs are a bet on the company. Join if you believe in the company's future. Don't join primarily for the ESOPs if you don't believe in the business itself.

## 6. Firing — How to Let Someone Go With Dignity

Nobody talks about this enough. Letting someone go is one of the hardest things a manager ever has to do, and most managers do it badly because they either avoid it too long or handle it too harshly.

Let's be direct: sometimes someone is not right for the role. Maybe they don't have the skills the job actually requires. Maybe the company's needs changed. Maybe they are a genuinely poor performer. Whatever the reason, keeping someone in a role they're failing in is not kind — it's actually cruel. It denies them the chance to find something they're actually good at.

**Before you fire, ask yourself:**

1. Have I been clear about what success looks like in this role?
2. Have I given specific, timely feedback about what isn't working?
3. Have I provided the support they need to improve?
4. Is this a skills problem (they can't do the job) or a will problem (they don't want to)?

If you have done all four and it's still not working, then letting them go is the right decision.

**How to do it with dignity:**

- **Be direct and brief.** Don't soften it to the point where the person doesn't understand what's happening. "We've decided to end your employment" is kinder in the long run than a 20-minute preamble.
- **Do it in private, not in a group email.** Never fire someone over email unless you have absolutely no other option.
- **Do it on a Wednesday or Thursday.** Firing someone on a Friday leaves them sitting alone over the weekend with no ability to take action. Giving them a weekday allows them to start their job search immediately.
- **Treat the severance seriously.** In India, you are legally required to give notice or pay in lieu of notice (more on this below). Beyond the legal minimum, consider what's fair.
- **Be specific about why.** Not a lecture, but enough so they understand what happened and can grow from it.
- **Handle their exit professionally.** Help them transition, give them a neutral or positive reference if you can, and make sure their last paycheck and documentation are handled promptly.

The companies that handle exits well maintain their reputation. The companies that handle exits badly — surprise layoffs over email, freezing access before telling people, stringing people along — get destroyed on Glassdoor and struggle to hire for years.

In 2022, several Indian startups including Unacademy, Byju's, and Ola laid off thousands of employees. The difference in how they handled these layoffs was stark. Some sent a form email. Others held town halls, offered severance above legal minimums, provided job placement support, and gave employees notice. The ones who handled it well recovered their reputation. The ones who didn't are still living with the consequences.

## 7. Building Culture — What It Really Means

Culture is the word that gets used most in business and understood least. You'll hear it constantly: "we have a great culture," "culture fit," "culture first." Most of the time, people using these words have no idea what they actually mean.

Here is a simple definition: **culture is how people in a company actually behave when nobody is watching.**

Not what's written on a wall. Not what the CEO says in an all-hands meeting. Not the ping-pong table or the free food. The actual behavior — how people treat each other, how decisions get made, what gets rewarded, what gets ignored, what's acceptable and what isn't.

If the company says it values "transparency" but the management team makes major decisions in private and tells employees afterwards, the culture is not transparent. The ping-pong table has nothing to do with it.

Culture is set by leadership, primarily through what they do, not what they say. If the founder works 80-hour weeks and sends Slack messages at midnight expecting responses, the culture will be a 80-hour work week culture whether or not that's ever stated explicitly. If the CEO admits mistakes publicly and celebrates people who raise problems, the culture will encourage honesty.

**What good culture actually produces:**

| Healthy Culture | Toxic Culture |
|---|---|
| People share bad news early | People hide problems until they explode |
| Disagreement happens in meetings, not in corridors | Corridor politics is how decisions really get made |
| People take ownership of mistakes | People blame others and protect themselves |
| New ideas are welcomed and tried | Risk aversion prevents progress |
| People recruit their best former colleagues | People warn their friends away |

Netflix's culture deck is worth reading in full. It articulates something important: culture is not a set of perks. It is a set of behaviors that the company actually practices and enforces. Netflix hires extraordinary people, gives them enormous freedom, and removes people who don't perform — regardless of how likeable they are. That is a culture. You can agree or disagree with it, but it is internally consistent.

Stripe's culture is built around writing. Memos instead of meetings. Detailed written proposals before any major project. The culture of writing forces clarity of thinking and creates a record of decisions. It shapes how the whole company operates.

Culture building is not a separate initiative. It is what happens when you hire well, communicate clearly, treat people fairly, and hold everyone (including yourself) to the same standards.

## 8. Remote Work — Managing Teams You Never See

Remote work went from a fringe perk to the dominant mode of working for knowledge workers, almost overnight, in 2020. Most companies have now settled into some version of hybrid or fully remote. Managing remote teams is a genuinely different skill from managing in-person teams.

The core challenge of remote work is invisible: when you can't see someone working, you lose all the casual context you used to have. You don't bump into them at the coffee machine and learn that they're struggling. You don't overhear them on a call and realize they need help. You don't notice that they haven't come to lunch in two weeks.

This means you have to be deliberate about the things that happen naturally in an office.

**What changes in remote management:**

1. **Communication must be asynchronous-first.** If your team is in different time zones (even just different parts of India — a Bangalore team and a Delhi team have 0 time difference but different rhythms), you can't require everyone to be online simultaneously. Write more. Document decisions. Use tools like Notion, Confluence, or even just Google Docs to create a written record of what was decided and why.

2. **Trust must be explicit.** Remote work only works if managers trust that their people are working without needing to see them. Tracking software (tools that screenshot employees' computers every few minutes) is a sign that the manager doesn't trust their people. It destroys morale and guarantees that only people with no better options will stay.

3. **One-on-ones become more important.** In an office, a manager gets dozens of small data points about each team member every day. In a remote setting, the one-on-one meeting is often the only real window into how someone is doing. These should not be status meetings. They should be genuine check-ins: how are you doing? What's blocked? What can I do to help?

4. **Results matter more than hours.** The most productive remote teams are clear about what success looks like and don't care whether you worked from 9–6 or 11–8. Outputs, not inputs.

**Tools that remote teams actually use:**

```
Communication:  Slack (async messaging), Zoom/Google Meet (video)
Documentation:  Notion, Confluence, Google Workspace
Project tracking: Linear, Jira, Trello, Asana
Code review:    GitHub, GitLab
Design review:  Figma
One-on-ones:    Often just Google Calendar + video
```

Airbnb moved to a "work from anywhere" policy in 2022, allowing employees to live and work in any country. They found that productivity stayed the same or improved, and they could recruit from a much larger talent pool. The constraint was that teams needed to meet in person a few times a year for important decisions and relationship-building.

Zepto, by contrast, operates in-person because their work involves physical dark stores and real-time operations. The right answer is always "what does our work actually require?" not "what is trending?"

## 9. HR in India — PF, ESI, Maternity Leave, and Other Requirements

If you start a company in India and hire employees, the law places specific obligations on you. Ignoring these is not an option — the penalties include fines and, in serious cases, criminal prosecution. This section gives you the foundations.

### Provident Fund (PF)

The **Employees' Provident Fund (EPF)** is a mandatory retirement savings scheme. Once a company has 20 or more employees, it must register with the EPFO (Employees' Provident Fund Organisation).

Both the employer and the employee contribute 12% of the employee's basic salary + DA (Dearness Allowance) each month.

```
If Basic Salary = ₹20,000/month:
  Employee contribution: 12% = ₹2,400/month (deducted from salary)
  Employer contribution: 12% = ₹2,400/month (paid by company, above salary)
  Total PF contribution: ₹4,800/month
```

The employer's 12% is split: most goes to the employee's PF account, but 8.33% (up to a ceiling) goes to the Employee Pension Scheme (EPS).

### ESI (Employees' State Insurance)

ESI provides medical insurance and sickness benefits to employees earning up to ₹21,000/month. Companies with 10 or more employees in most states must register.

- **Employee contribution:** 0.75% of gross salary
- **Employer contribution:** 3.25% of gross salary

ESI-registered employees and their families can access healthcare through ESI hospitals and dispensaries at no additional cost.

### Gratuity

Any employee who has worked continuously for 5 or more years is entitled to gratuity upon leaving the company. The formula:

```
Gratuity = (Last drawn monthly salary × 15 × Number of years worked) / 26
```

If you worked 8 years at a last salary of ₹50,000/month:
Gratuity = (50,000 × 15 × 8) / 26 = ₹2,30,769

### Maternity Leave

Under the **Maternity Benefit (Amendment) Act, 2017**, women employees are entitled to:

- **26 weeks of paid maternity leave** for the first two children (up from the earlier 12 weeks)
- **12 weeks** for the third child onwards
- **12 weeks** for adoptive mothers who adopt a child below 3 months of age
- Companies with 50+ employees must provide a creche facility

This applies to both salaried and contract employees who have worked for at least 80 days in the past 12 months.

### Other Key Requirements

**Shops and Establishment Act:** Governs working hours, overtime, holidays, and employment conditions. Each state has its own version. If you have a physical office, you must register under this Act.

**Sexual Harassment at the Workplace (POSH) Act:** Mandatory for all companies with 10 or more employees. You must have an Internal Complaints Committee (ICC), a posted policy, and annual awareness training. Non-compliance is an offense.

**Professional Tax:** A state-level tax deducted from employee salaries. Varies by state — Maharashtra charges up to ₹200/month, Karnataka up to ₹200/month. Must be remitted to the state government.

**TDS (Tax Deducted at Source):** Employers must deduct income tax from salaries and remit to the government. You must issue Form 16 to all employees annually — this is their record for filing personal income taxes.

> **What This Means in Practice:** Most early-stage startups use payroll software (Razorpay Payroll, Keka, Darwinbox, or Zoho Payroll) to automate all of these calculations and compliance filings. Don't try to manage PF, ESI, and TDS manually in a spreadsheet. The software cost is trivial compared to the risk of getting it wrong.

## 10. Common HR Mistakes Startups Make

Every startup makes HR mistakes. The goal is not to make zero mistakes — it's to not make the same expensive ones that have destroyed other companies before you.

### Mistake 1: Hiring for speed instead of fit

When a startup is growing fast and there's too much work, the pressure to fill roles quickly is immense. You interview someone who seems fine, you're desperate, you make the offer. Six months later you realize they're wrong for the role, you've spent time and money onboarding them, and now you have to go through the whole process again — plus manage the awkward reality of an underperforming team member.

The fix: hold the standard even when you're desperate. A bad hire is almost always worse than a slow hire.

### Mistake 2: No documentation of employment terms

India has well-developed labor law. If you don't have a signed employment contract, you have no written record of the notice period, the role, the salary, or the grounds for termination. In a dispute, this is a serious problem.

Always, always use written employment contracts. Even for your first hire. Even for contractors.

### Mistake 3: Promoting your best performer into management

Your best engineer gets promoted to engineering manager. Your best salesperson becomes sales manager. This seems logical — they're brilliant, why not give them more responsibility? But the skills that make someone a great individual contributor are not the same skills that make someone a great manager. Coding skill doesn't translate to people skills. Sales instinct doesn't translate to coaching ability.

Many companies have ruined both a great individual contributor and a well-functioning team by promoting the wrong person. Always ask: does this person actually want to manage? Do they have the skills? Or are they just the next in line?

### Mistake 4: Ignoring culture until it's too late

Culture is set in the first 10 hires. If your first ten people work hard, communicate honestly, hold each other to high standards, and treat each other with respect — that will compound. If your first ten people play politics, avoid accountability, and build silos — that will also compound. You cannot fix culture at 200 people. You have to build it at 10.

### Mistake 5: Under-communicating during uncertainty

Startups go through hard moments — a fundraising round that takes longer than expected, a product pivot, a major customer loss. When leadership goes quiet during these moments, rumors fill the vacuum. People assume the worst. Key employees preemptively job-hunt. The problem gets worse.

The fix is to over-communicate during uncertainty. You don't need to have all the answers. "We are in a difficult moment, here's what we know, here's what we don't know, here's what we're doing about it" is infinitely better than silence.

### Mistake 6: Conflating loyalty with competence

Early employees often have enormous loyalty to the founder. They joined when the company was small and risky. They worked long hours for low pay. They deserve respect for that. But loyalty does not mean they are still the right person for the role as the company grows. A VP of Engineering who was excellent at 10 people might not be the right leader at 300 people. The role has grown; the person may not have grown with it.

This is one of the most painful HR decisions a founder ever makes. And avoiding it is almost always the more expensive choice.

## Summary

Human resources is the practice of building and maintaining the team that will execute your business strategy. Get it right and you have a compounding asset — great people attract other great people, work gets better, culture strengthens. Get it wrong and every other part of the business gets harder.

The key ideas from this chapter:

- **Hiring is a sales process.** The best candidates have options. You must sell them on why your company is the right place for them.
- **Job descriptions should describe outcomes, not wishlists.** The goal is to attract the right person, not to screen for credentials.
- **Onboarding sets the tone.** The first 90 days determine whether someone thrives or starts doubting their decision.
- **Performance management exists to help people grow**, not to generate ratings. Continuous feedback beats annual reviews.
- **OKRs clarify priorities.** Three to five ambitious goals per team per quarter is more powerful than a long list of tasks.
- **Compensation must be competitive.** People leave when they feel underpaid. ESOPs can be powerful retention tools — but only in startups that actually grow.
- **Firing must be done with clarity and dignity.** Avoiding a necessary termination is always more costly than making it.
- **Culture is behavior, not perks.** It is set by what leadership does, what gets rewarded, and what gets ignored.
- **Remote work requires deliberate communication.** What happens naturally in an office must be designed explicitly when the team is distributed.
- **Indian HR law is not optional.** PF, ESI, gratuity, maternity leave, POSH — these are legal requirements with real consequences for non-compliance.

## Exercises and Discussion Questions

1. **Job description audit:** Find a real job description posted by a company you admire (on LinkedIn or their careers page). Evaluate it against the framework in this chapter. Is it outcome-oriented? Does it show personality? Does it include compensation? What would you change?

2. **Culture detective:** Think of a company you have worked at, interned at, or know well. What was the culture — not what they claimed it was, but what it actually was? Give three specific behavioral examples that illustrate the real culture. How did leadership's behavior shape it?

3. **ESOP calculation exercise:** A startup offers you a job with ₹10 lakh CTC plus 5,000 options at a strike price of ₹100. The company is currently valued at ₹50 crore with 50 lakh shares outstanding. If the company grows 10x in 4 years, what are your options potentially worth at exit? What risks could make them worth zero?

4. **Onboarding design:** You are the HR lead at a 30-person startup. Design a 30-day onboarding plan for a new senior product manager. What do they need to know? Who do they need to meet? What should they deliver by day 30?

5. **Difficult conversation role play:** You are a manager. One of your team members has been underperforming for three months. They are well-liked, they try hard, but the output is consistently below what the role requires. You need to have the conversation about performance improvement. Write out the key points you would make and how you would frame them.

6. **Remote work policy design:** Your 20-person startup is going fully remote. Design a simple remote work policy that addresses: working hours, communication expectations, one-on-one cadence, equipment provision, and in-person meeting frequency. Justify each choice.

7. **Legal compliance checklist:** You have just hired your 15th employee in Bangalore. What legal obligations have you triggered or already had? Create a checklist of everything you need to have in place (PF, ESI, POSH, etc.) and what the consequence of non-compliance would be for each.
