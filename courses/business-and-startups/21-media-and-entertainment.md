# Chapter 21: Media and Entertainment — The Attention Economy

*There is one resource that every media company on earth is fighting for. Not money — money follows from it. Not users — users are a proxy for it. The resource is your attention. Specifically, the hours of your day. You have 24 of them. You sleep for 7 or 8. You work or study for 8 more. That leaves roughly 8 hours of "free" time, and every entertainment company on the planet — Netflix, YouTube, Instagram, Star Sports, Spotify, Hotstar, your local news channel — is locked in a brutal fight to own as much of those 8 hours as possible. This is the Attention Economy, and it is one of the most fascinating and strange industries in modern business.*

## 1. The Attention Economy — Time as the Scarce Resource

Here is a strange thought: the richest people on earth are not mining gold or drilling for oil. They are mining your attention.

Herbert Simon, a Nobel Prize-winning economist, said something in 1971 that turned out to be prophetic: "A wealth of information creates a poverty of attention." He meant that once information becomes abundant — once anyone can publish anything — the scarce thing is no longer content. The scarce thing is the human attention needed to consume it.

Think about it from your own life. How many things are competing for your attention right now? WhatsApp notifications, Instagram reels, a YouTube recommendation, a show on Netflix you haven't finished, cricket highlights, a podcast your friend sent, news on your phone. There are more things to watch, read, and listen to than any human could consume in a thousand lifetimes.

And yet you only have 24 hours.

### Why Time Is More Valuable Than Money Here

When you are broke, you can earn more money. When you run out of time in a day, you cannot get more. This asymmetry is what makes attention so valuable — and so fiercely contested.

The media industry understood this long before the internet arrived. Newspapers in the 19th century figured out that if they could attract a large enough audience, they could sell that audience's attention to advertisers. The content (news) was the bait. The reader's eyeballs were the actual product being sold. The advertiser was the paying customer.

This model — **build an audience with content, sell that audience to advertisers** — is the foundation of almost every major media business in history. Newspapers, TV channels, radio stations, websites, social media platforms. The content might differ wildly, but the business model is often the same.

```
Content → Audience → Advertiser Revenue
```

The other major model is direct payment: you sell the content itself. The audience pays directly for the content they want. Books, movies, music, premium streaming services.

```
Content → Paying Audience → Revenue
```

Most modern media businesses blend both. Netflix charges you a subscription but also runs ads on a cheaper tier. YouTube gives you content for free but makes money from ads, and also sells YouTube Premium for ad-free viewing.

### Measuring Attention

Media companies measure attention in very specific ways:

| Metric | What It Means | Who Uses It |
|--------|--------------|-------------|
| Daily Active Users (DAU) | How many people use the platform each day | All digital platforms |
| Monthly Active Users (MAU) | How many people use it per month | Facebook, YouTube |
| Watch Time / Hours Streamed | Total hours of content consumed | Netflix, YouTube |
| Time Spent Per Session | How long someone stays per visit | Instagram, TikTok |
| Reach | What % of a target population you can reach | TV channels, print |
| Impressions | Total number of times an ad was seen | All advertising |

The most important ratio is DAU/MAU — the "stickiness" ratio. If 100 people use your platform monthly but only 10 use it daily, your stickiness is 10%. If 70 out of 100 use it daily, your stickiness is 70%. The higher this is, the more habitual and embedded your platform is in people's lives.

TikTok's early data showed stickiness above 60%. Instagram was historically around 50-55%. Facebook has declined from its peak but still sits above 50% globally. These numbers represent an enormous claim on human time.

## 2. Traditional Media Business Models

Before streaming, before the internet, before smartphones — how did media companies actually make money? Understanding the old models is essential to understanding why and how they were disrupted.

### Newspapers: The Original Dual Revenue Model

A newspaper has two revenue streams running in parallel:

1. **Subscription / Copy Sales**: Readers pay to buy the paper
2. **Advertising**: Brands pay to put ads in front of readers

Here is the interesting part: in the golden age of print (roughly 1950-2000), the advertising revenue was far larger than the subscription revenue. A newspaper might charge you Rs 5 per copy, but it was collecting lakh of rupees from a single page of advertising. The Times of India at its peak was one of the most profitable newspapers in the world, not because people paid a lot for it, but because it commanded massive advertising rates.

The formula works like this:

```
More Readers → Higher Reach → Premium Advertising Rates → Higher Revenue
```

This is why newspapers would sometimes give papers away at steep discounts — they were buying readers to sell to advertisers.

The vulnerability of this model: if the audience fragments (goes to the internet), or if advertisers find cheaper ways to reach people (Google, Facebook), the entire structure collapses. That is exactly what happened after 2005.

### Television: The Broadcast Model

Television in India was dominated for decades by Doordarshan (the government-owned channel), then by private channels like Zee TV, Star Plus, Sony Entertainment Television.

Free-to-air television (also called broadcast TV) is essentially the same model as newspapers — you give people free content and sell their attention to advertisers. A 30-second ad slot during a primetime show on Star Plus during cricket season can cost several lakh rupees, because tens of millions of people are watching.

**Cable TV** added a twist: viewers pay a monthly cable subscription AND the channel gets advertising revenue. The cable operator takes a cut, the distributor takes a cut, and the channel keeps the rest. This "double dipping" — charging both viewers and advertisers — made TV channels extraordinarily profitable.

**Streaming** is the modern evolution: a pure subscription model with no advertising (or a lighter ad tier). Netflix, Amazon Prime, Hotstar. We'll go deep on this later.

### Movies: The Theatrical Window

A film doesn't just make money once. It moves through a sequence of "windows":

```
Theatrical Release (Cinema)
    ↓ (45-90 days later)
Premium Video On Demand (rent/buy online)
    ↓ (a few months later)
Streaming Platform (Netflix, Prime, Hotstar)
    ↓ (eventually)
Free TV / Satellite
```

Each window represents a different price point and a different audience segment. The people who are most excited about a film pay the most (cinema tickets, IMAX). Those less excited wait for the cheaper streaming version.

This system was designed to maximize revenue extraction across different levels of consumer willingness to pay. The technical term for this is **price discrimination** — charging different prices to different customers for the same product based on their eagerness to consume it.

### Music: Labels, Royalties, and the Factory System

Before Spotify and streaming, the music industry worked on a sale model. You bought a vinyl record, then a cassette, then a CD. The record label manufactured and distributed the physical product, took the majority of the revenue (often 85-90%), and paid the artist royalties on what remained.

A "royalty" is a payment per unit sold or per use. If your song was played on the radio, you received a small royalty. If your album sold 1 million copies, you received a small percentage of each sale.

The record labels — Universal, Sony Music, Warner — essentially operated as venture capitalists. They invested in discovering and developing artists, then earned back (far more than) their investment through album sales. The artists, especially new ones, were often left with relatively little. This created enormous tension that the streaming era made even more complicated.

## 3. The Digital Disruption of Media

### How Netflix Killed Blockbuster

This is one of the most famous business school case studies of the internet era, but the actual story is more interesting than the summary.

Blockbuster Video, at its peak in the early 2000s, had 9,000 stores worldwide, 60,000 employees, and revenue of $6 billion a year. You would walk in, pick a videocassette or DVD, pay a rental fee (around Rs 50-100 equivalent), watch the movie, and return it within a few days. If you returned it late, you paid a late fee. Those late fees alone accounted for nearly $800 million per year — about 16% of Blockbuster's revenue.

In 1997, Reed Hastings co-founded Netflix with a simple idea: rent DVDs by mail with no late fees, and let customers keep them as long as they want. It was a subscription model — pay a flat monthly fee, get unlimited rentals. Netflix would mail you the DVD, you'd mail it back when done.

The insight was both obvious and non-obvious: **late fees were not a feature, they were a punishment.** Blockbuster's business depended on customers behaving badly. Netflix competed by removing that punishment.

Blockbuster had a chance to buy Netflix in 2000 for $50 million. They passed, reportedly laughing the offer off. Netflix at that time was losing money and Blockbuster seemed impregnable.

What Blockbuster missed:
- **The friction of physical stores** — Netflix home delivery was more convenient
- **The late fee resentment** — customers quietly hated Blockbuster, and Netflix gave them an escape
- **The internet as a distribution channel** — Blockbuster's real estate was a cost; Netflix had almost none
- **The data advantage** — Netflix tracked every DVD rental and used that data to understand what people actually watched (not just what they said they liked)

Netflix pivoted to streaming in 2007 — initially as a bonus feature alongside DVD rentals. By 2010, it was obvious streaming was the future. Blockbuster filed for bankruptcy in 2010.

> **What This Means in Practice:** Blockbuster had every advantage — brand, stores, customer relationships, supplier deals — and still lost. They lost because they optimized for their existing business model rather than responding to a better consumer experience. Netflix's streaming pivot is now studied as a masterclass in eating your own business before a competitor does it to you.

### How YouTube Changed TV for Young Audiences

When YouTube launched in 2005, people assumed it was a novelty — a website for cat videos and funny clips. The TV industry didn't take it seriously.

They should have.

What YouTube actually was: **the first television network where anyone could be the broadcaster.** Traditional TV required expensive equipment, broadcast licenses, production studios, and distribution infrastructure costing tens of millions. YouTube required a camera (eventually, just a phone) and an internet connection.

The shift was generational. If you were born after 1995, you grew up in a world where YouTube existed. Why would you watch a TV show at a fixed time when you could watch any video at any time? Why would you follow a broadcaster's schedule when you could subscribe directly to the creators you liked?

For young audiences, "appointment viewing" — sitting in front of the TV at 9pm because that's when your show airs — became alien. The demand for on-demand content was essentially complete generational replacement.

The economics of YouTube shifted power from distributors (TV channels) to creators. A YouTuber with 5 million subscribers could earn more than a primetime TV show host, with far less overhead and no corporate gatekeepers deciding what content was acceptable.

### How Spotify Changed Music

Spotify launched in 2008 in Europe (2011 in the US) with a model that the music industry had never seen: unlimited streaming of almost all recorded music for a flat monthly fee.

The iTunes model (Apple, 2001) had partially disrupted CDs by letting people buy individual songs for $0.99 instead of paying $15 for an album. But Spotify went further — why buy songs at all when you could access everything for $10/month?

From the listener's perspective, this was obviously better. For artists and labels, the math was more complicated. Streaming pays fractions of a cent per stream — typically $0.003 to $0.005 per play. An artist needs roughly 200-250 streams to earn a dollar. This means an artist needs millions of streams just to earn what they once earned from album sales.

The winners in the streaming era have been the major labels (who own vast catalogs and negotiated large upfront deals with Spotify) and Spotify itself. Many mid-tier and independent artists found themselves earning less, not more, from streaming — a controversy that continues today.

## 4. The Indian Media Landscape

India has one of the world's most complex and competitive media markets — because India is not one market. It is many.

You have 1.4 billion people speaking 22 officially recognized languages, spread across states with wildly different cultures, income levels, and media habits. A media strategy that works in Mumbai is irrelevant in rural Bihar. A Hindi-language show might dominate ratings but miss 400 million people who watch exclusively in Tamil, Telugu, Bengali, or Marathi.

### The Major Players

| Company | Type | Key Channels/Platforms | Parent |
|---------|------|----------------------|--------|
| Star India | TV + Streaming | Star Plus, Star Sports, Hotstar/Disney+ Hotstar | The Walt Disney Company (US) |
| Zee Entertainment | TV + Streaming | Zee TV, Zee5 | Essel Group (India) |
| Sony Pictures | TV + Streaming | Sony LIV, SET, Sony SAB | Sony Group (Japan) |
| JioCinema | Streaming | JioCinema | Reliance Jio (India) |
| Colors / Viacom18 | TV + Streaming | Colors TV, MTV India | Reliance + Viacom (India/US JV) |
| Sun TV Network | Regional TV | Sun TV (Tamil), Gemini TV (Telugu) | Kalanithi Maran family |

### The Battle for Cricket

If you want to understand Indian media business, you need to understand one thing: **cricket is not just a sport, it is infrastructure.**

The Indian Premier League (IPL) is the most expensive media property in India by a massive margin. In 2022, the BCCI (cricket's governing body) sold the IPL media rights for Rs 48,390 crore for a 5-year period — about $6 billion. Disney+ Hotstar paid for digital rights; Viacom18/JioCinema got streaming; Star Sports got TV broadcasting.

Why are companies willing to pay such staggering sums? Because cricket — especially IPL — guarantees eyeballs. Not just any eyeballs: live, engaged, appointment-viewing eyeballs that cannot time-shift the way they do with other content. Advertising during live sports commands massive premiums because viewers actually watch the ads.

In 2023, JioCinema broadcast the IPL for free to hundreds of millions of users — a deliberate decision by Reliance to grow its user base rapidly. Disney+ Hotstar lost many of its subscribers when JioCinema acquired IPL streaming rights. This is media as a customer acquisition strategy, not just an entertainment business.

### The Rise of Regional OTT

One of the most interesting underreported stories in Indian media is the explosion of regional language streaming platforms:

- **Aha** (Telugu) — focused exclusively on Telugu content
- **Hoichoi** (Bengali)
- **Koode** (Malayalam)
- **Planet Marathi**
- **Neestream** (Malayalam Christian content)

These platforms understood something global giants like Netflix missed initially: a viewer in Vijayawada who watches exclusively in Telugu is not looking for a subtitled Hindi or English show. They want their own language, their own stories, their own stars. Regional platforms that serve this need can build fiercely loyal audiences that global platforms struggle to win.

## 5. The Streaming Wars — Who Wins in a World with Too Many Subscriptions

In 2023, if you subscribed to Netflix, Amazon Prime, Disney+ Hotstar, Apple TV+, and Sony LIV, you were spending roughly Rs 1,500-2,000 per month on streaming. That's before Zee5, JioCinema, Aha, or any music service.

There is a concept called **subscription fatigue**: when consumers have too many subscriptions and start cancelling. Every service is competing not just for your attention but for your willingness to pay a recurring bill.

### The Math of Streaming

Streaming platforms live and die by a metric called **Subscriber Economics**:

```
LTV (Lifetime Value) = ARPU × Average Months of Subscription
ARPU = Average Revenue Per User per month

If ARPU = Rs 200 and users stay for 24 months → LTV = Rs 4,800

CAC (Customer Acquisition Cost) = Total Marketing Spend ÷ New Subscribers Added

Healthy unit economics require: LTV >> CAC (at least 3x)
```

The problem: acquiring streaming subscribers is expensive. Netflix spent roughly $15 billion on content in 2023 alone — that content is both the product and the advertising. If subscribers cancel after 6 months, Netflix may never recover its content investment from those users.

### The Password Sharing Crackdown

Netflix's decision to crack down on password sharing in 2023 was widely mocked initially. "Netflix is shooting itself in the foot," analysts said. "Users will just cancel."

What actually happened: Netflix added 9 million subscribers in Q2 2023 alone — more than any quarter in years. Why? Because the people using shared passwords had to choose: pay your own subscription or lose access. Many chose to pay. The crackdown worked.

This illustrates a media business truth: a large unpaying user base looks like an asset (lots of users!) but is actually a trap. Monetizing those users, even partially, is often more valuable than keeping them in a "free" state.

### Who Will Win the Streaming Wars?

The honest answer: probably nobody wins cleanly. The likely outcome is:

1. **2-3 global giants survive** (Netflix, Apple, Amazon Prime) because they have enough revenue from other businesses to subsidize streaming losses indefinitely
2. **National champions emerge** in large markets (JioCinema in India, iQIYI in China, Globoplay in Brazil)
3. **Consolidation happens** — weaker players merge or get acquired (the Zee-Sony merger attempt is an example)
4. **Niche platforms thrive** at the edges (Mubi for art cinema, Aha for Telugu, Shudder for horror)

The middle is where it's most dangerous — platforms big enough to need lots of content, but not big enough to afford the best content.

## 6. Music in India — Saavn, Gaana, and Why Spotify Struggles

Music streaming in India is a fascinating case study in local competition versus global giants.

### The Players

**JioSaavn** was formed when Saavn (an Indian music app founded in 2007) merged with Jio Music in 2018. Saavn had built something valuable: a massive catalog of Indian music in regional languages, plus good curation for Indian tastes. JioSaavn is now bundled with Jio telecom plans, giving it near-automatic distribution to hundreds of millions of users.

**Gaana** (owned by Times Internet, part of the Times Group) similarly built a large library of Indian music and competed primarily on free, ad-supported listening — important in a market where many users are unwilling to pay for music.

**Spotify** entered India in 2019 with enormous excitement. It had a global brand, best-in-class recommendation algorithms, and the most popular music app in the world. It immediately ran into trouble.

The first problem: licensing. Spotify had to negotiate separately with Indian music labels — T-Series (the world's largest YouTube channel), Saregama, Tips, Zee Music. T-Series initially refused to license its catalog to Spotify, which is like launching a food delivery app but not having access to McDonald's, Domino's, and Pizza Hut simultaneously. Spotify launched in India without T-Series music.

The second problem: price expectations. Indians are notoriously price-sensitive on subscriptions. Spotify priced its premium plan at Rs 119/month, then cut it to Rs 7/day or Rs 119/month. But most Indian users had become accustomed to free or near-free music through JioSaavn (bundled with their Jio plan).

The third problem: local competition had a head start. JioSaavn and Gaana had spent years building Hindi, Tamil, Telugu, Punjabi, and Bhojpuri catalogs that Spotify had to quickly catch up on.

Spotify has grown in India but remains a distant third or fourth behind JioSaavn and Gaana. This is a recurring pattern: **local players who build for a specific market's tastes and price sensitivities often beat global giants even when the global giant has a better product in the abstract.**

## 7. Gaming — The Biggest Entertainment Industry Nobody Talks About

Here is a number that will surprise most people: the global gaming industry generated approximately **$184 billion in revenue in 2023.** For comparison, the global film industry generated about $33 billion at the box office. The music industry generated about $26 billion.

Gaming is bigger than movies and music combined. By a lot. And it receives a fraction of the cultural attention.

### Why Gaming Is Such a Good Business

Games have several unusual business model properties:

**1. Variable Time Consumption:** A movie is 2 hours. A game can occupy thousands of hours. When someone is deeply engaged with a game, they are not watching Netflix, not scrolling Instagram. They have completely exited the attention marketplace for every other media.

**2. Social Network Effects:** Multiplayer games like PUBG, Free Fire, and Call of Duty are social experiences. Your friends play, so you play. This makes gaming communities extraordinarily sticky — leaving the game means leaving the community.

**3. In-App Purchases and Live Service:** Modern games often follow a "free-to-play, pay-to-win or pay-for-cosmetics" model. The base game is free (lowering barrier to entry), but players spend money on upgrades, skins, characters, or boosts. PUBG Mobile, developed by a Chinese company (Tencent), earned over $3 billion per year at its peak partly through Indian and Southeast Asian players.

**3. Esports:** Professional gaming competitions now fill stadiums. The League of Legends World Championship regularly draws more concurrent online viewers than the NBA Finals. Esports athletes have agents, coaches, training regimens, and sponsors.

### India's Gaming Boom

India had approximately 450 million mobile gamers in 2023, making it one of the largest gaming markets in the world by user count — though revenue per user remains lower than Western markets.

Fantasy sports platforms like **Dream11** (valued at $8+ billion) operate at the intersection of cricket, gaming, and gambling psychology. You pick a team of real players, and your points depend on how those real players perform in real matches. Dream11 has over 130 million users and generates enormous advertising revenue during IPL season.

**MPL (Mobile Premier League)** and **WinZO** have built platforms around casual mobile gaming with real-money tournaments, an innovation particularly suited to India where small-stakes competition is culturally familiar from card games and local sports.

## 8. Podcasts and Newsletters — The Return of Niche Audiences

There is a countermovement happening in media alongside the streaming giants: a return to intimate, niche, direct-relationship media. Podcasts and newsletters represent this countermovement.

### Why Podcasts Work

A podcast is radio you can listen to whenever you want. The format is deceptively powerful for a few reasons:

- **Multitasking-friendly:** You can listen while commuting, exercising, or cooking. Other media (video, articles) compete with these activities. Audio does not.
- **Intimate tone:** A podcast conversation feels personal in a way that TV rarely does. Listeners often describe feeling like they "know" their favorite podcast hosts — a phenomenon called **parasocial relationship.** This builds deep loyalty.
- **Low production cost:** A decent podcast can be made for under Rs 10,000 in equipment. A TV show cannot. This democratizes the medium.

The business model is primarily advertising, with sponsorships typically running Rs 1,000-3,000 per 1,000 listeners (CPM). A podcast with 100,000 monthly listeners can earn Rs 1-3 lakh per episode from sponsors. Some popular creators have also moved to subscription models via platforms like Patreon.

### The Substack / Newsletter Economy

Substack (founded 2017) made it trivially easy for writers to charge readers a monthly subscription for a newsletter. The proposition: "If you have 10,000 readers who love your writing, and 1,000 of them pay Rs 500/month, that's Rs 5 lakh per month — more than most journalists earn at big publications."

This has caused a significant migration of star journalists and writers from mainstream publications to independent newsletters. The trade-off: more money, more independence, but no editorial support and complete self-reliance.

In India, this model has gained traction among finance writers, political analysts, and technology commentators. Platforms like **Beehiiv** and **Ghost** compete with Substack.

## 9. The Creator Economy — When Individuals Become Media Companies

This is one of the most important business story of the 2020s: individual creators — YouTubers, Instagram influencers, TikTok personalities — have become media companies in their own right.

Consider what a "media company" actually does:
- Creates content regularly
- Builds and maintains an audience
- Monetizes that audience through advertising, subscriptions, or product sales

A person with 5 million YouTube subscribers does all of these things. They are, in every meaningful business sense, a media company. They just happen to be a company of one (or a small team).

### How Creator Revenue Works

```
Revenue Streams for a Large Creator:
1. AdSense / Platform Revenue (YouTube shares ad revenue with creators)
2. Brand Deals / Sponsorships (paid promotions in videos/posts)
3. Merchandise (selling branded products to fans)
4. Digital Products (courses, presets, templates)
5. Memberships (Patreon, YouTube Memberships)
6. Affiliate Commissions (earning % of sales when followers buy)
```

A mid-tier Indian YouTuber (1-2 million subscribers, decent engagement) might earn:

- AdSense: Rs 1-3 lakh/month (India CPMs are lower than US)
- Brand deals: Rs 2-5 lakh per branded video, 2-4 videos/month
- **Total: Rs 5-20 lakh/month** — more than most corporate executives

At the top end, creators like Carry Minati, Bhuvan Bam, and Ashish Chanchlani have built businesses worth tens of crores.

### The Vulnerability of Being Platform-Dependent

The risk in the creator economy: creators build their audience on someone else's platform. YouTube can change its algorithm tomorrow and your views could drop 80%. Instagram can deprioritize your content. TikTok can be banned (it was, in India, in 2020).

The smartest creators treat their YouTube channel as the top of a funnel: they build an audience there, then convert fans to direct relationships through email lists, WhatsApp communities, Discord servers, or their own apps — distribution they own, not rent.

## 10. The Business of Bollywood

Bollywood is not just a film industry. It is an ecosystem that spans film production, music (most Bollywood movies have soundtracks that are massive commercial properties in themselves), merchandise, celebrity endorsements, and increasingly, OTT licensing.

### How a Bollywood Film Makes Money

A big-budget Hindi film (say, a Rs 150 crore production budget) typically monetizes through:

| Revenue Stream | Typical Contribution |
|----------------|---------------------|
| Domestic Box Office | 40-50% |
| Overseas Box Office | 10-20% (varies widely) |
| OTT Rights | 20-30% (huge post-pandemic) |
| Satellite TV Rights | 5-10% |
| Music Rights | 3-7% |
| Merchandise / Brand Tie-ins | 2-5% |

The OTT rights element is critical and new. Pre-2019, a film that bombed at the box office was a catastrophic loss. Post-2020 (after the pandemic forced mass OTT releases), OTT platforms became willing to pay large guaranteed amounts for streaming rights — sometimes before a film even finished shooting. This changed the risk calculus for producers entirely.

A film might lose Rs 50 crore at the box office but the producers had already sold OTT rights for Rs 80 crore, making the film profitable overall despite being considered a "flop."

### The Star System and Why It Is Changing

Bollywood has historically been built around stars — Shah Rukh Khan, Salman Khan, Aamir Khan, Akshay Kumar. The theory: a star's fan base is guaranteed audience. Book a star and you book a certain minimum box office.

This logic held for decades but has been cracking. Several big-star films in 2022-23 (Shamshera, Laal Singh Chaddha, Samrat Prithviraj) underperformed dramatically at the box office despite massive star power, while South Indian films like RRR, KGF Chapter 2, and Pushpa crossed Rs 1,000 crore with stars who were not known quantities in the Hindi-language market.

The shift is being driven by streaming. When everything is available on Netflix or Prime, audiences have been trained to watch what they find interesting, not what is "safe." A mediocre film with a big star is a worse experience than a great film with an unknown. Streaming has made quality matter more and star power matter slightly less.

## 11. Summary

The media and entertainment industry is the business of human attention. In a world overflowing with content, the scarce resource is not content itself but the time and focus of human beings.

The key ideas from this chapter:

- **Traditional media** (newspapers, TV, movies) was built on two models: advertising (selling audience attention to brands) and direct payment (charging audiences for content). Most businesses use both.
- **Digital disruption** rewrote the rules. Netflix beat Blockbuster not with better movies but with a better experience. YouTube gave distribution to anyone. Spotify disaggregated the album.
- **India's media market** is uniquely complex — massive scale, multiple languages, price-sensitive consumers, and the singular gravitational pull of cricket as a media property.
- **The streaming wars** are unsustainable for most players. Consolidation and niche specialization are the likely outcomes. The winners will be either global giants (Netflix, Amazon) or deeply local players (JioCinema).
- **Gaming** is the largest entertainment industry in the world and is increasingly the context for social interaction for younger generations.
- **The creator economy** has made individuals into media companies, fragmenting attention further and creating thousands of small, highly loyal audiences rather than a few massive ones.
- **Bollywood** is adapting to an OTT world where guaranteed streaming revenue has changed the risk calculus for producers, and where star power is no longer the reliable box office guarantee it once was.

The underlying tension in all of this: attention is finite. Every new entertainment form — streaming, gaming, podcasts, short video — doesn't grow the total pie of human time. It slices the existing pie differently. Companies that survive will be those that build habits deep enough that users choose them automatically, or that serve a specific audience so well that no general platform can replace them.
