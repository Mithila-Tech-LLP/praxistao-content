# Chapter 41: IPO and Exits — Going Public, Getting Acquired, and What Happens to Founders

*Every startup journey ends in one of three ways: massive success, acquisition, or failure. Understanding exits shapes every decision from day one.*

---

## 1. What an Exit Is

An exit is a liquidity event — the moment when investors and founders convert their equity into cash.

Before an exit, equity is just a number on paper. After an exit, it's money in a bank account.

The three types of exits:
1. **IPO:** The company lists on a stock exchange; shares are sold to the public
2. **Acquisition:** A larger company buys the startup
3. **Wind-down:** The company shuts down (most common exit, but rarely discussed)

---

## 2. The IPO — Going Public

An Initial Public Offering (IPO) is when a private company first sells shares to the public by listing on a stock exchange.

### Why Companies Do IPOs

- **Liquidity for investors and founders:** Early shareholders can finally sell their shares
- **Access to public capital:** Can raise large amounts from retail and institutional investors
- **Currency for acquisitions:** Public company shares can be used to buy other companies
- **Brand prestige:** Being listed on NSE/BSE or NYSE raises visibility and credibility
- **Employee retention:** Listed company ESOPs have a clear market price

### Why IPOs Are Painful

- **Expensive:** IPO process costs ₹50-200 crore in India (investment bank fees, legal, compliance)
- **Time-consuming:** 12-18 months of preparation
- **Regulatory burden:** SEBI requirements, continuous disclosure, quarterly earnings calls
- **Quarterly pressure:** Public markets focus on quarterly results; long-term investments get punished
- **Lock-up periods:** Founders can't sell shares immediately after IPO (typically 6 months)

### The Indian IPO Process

**Step 1: Appoint bankers**
The company hires investment banks (JM Financial, Kotak Mahindra Capital, Axis Capital, etc.) to manage the process.

**Step 2: Prepare DRHP (Draft Red Herring Prospectus)**
The complete document describing the company's business, financials, risks, and proposed use of proceeds. SEBI reviews this (takes 30-75 days typically).

**Step 3: SEBI approval**
SEBI approves the DRHP. The company can then file the final RHP (Red Herring Prospectus) with actual price band.

**Step 4: Roadshow**
Management presents to institutional investors across cities (Mumbai, Delhi, Singapore, New York for larger IPOs) to build demand.

**Step 5: Price band and subscription**
A price band is set. Retail and institutional investors apply for shares over 3 days. Oversubscription is measured (e.g., "Zomato IPO was subscribed 38x").

**Step 6: Listing**
Shares start trading on NSE/BSE. The listing price is determined by supply/demand.

### Famous Indian Tech IPOs

**Zomato (2021):** Listed at ₹116, opened at ₹125. First major Indian tech IPO. Revenue was growing but losses were significant. Debated intensely — "should you invest in a loss-making company?"

**Nykaa (2021):** First Indian unicorn founded by a woman (Falguni Nayar) to list. Strong brand, profitable. Listed at significant premium to offer price.

**PolicyBazaar (2021):** Insurance aggregator. Profitable model with strong market position.

**Paytm (2021):** India's largest IPO (₹18,300 crore raised). Listed at massive discount to issue price. Fell sharply. Widely seen as premature IPO — company wasn't ready for public market scrutiny.

### The Paytm Warning

Paytm's IPO is the cautionary tale of the era. It raised $2.5B in November 2021, the largest Indian IPO ever. The stock opened below the issue price (₹2,150) and fell to ₹440 within 18 months.

What went wrong:
- Business model was complex and hard to explain
- Path to profitability was unclear
- Governance concerns (founder Vijay Shekhar Sharma had outsized control)
- IPO was timed at market peak (October 2021) with aggressive valuation

**The lesson:** An IPO is not a guaranteed success. The public market will price your company rationally over time, regardless of what private investors were willing to pay.

## 3. Strategic Acquisition — Being Bought by a Larger Company

Most startup exits are acquisitions, not IPOs.

Acquisitions happen when a larger company believes that buying the startup creates more value than building the capability organically.

**Why companies get acquired:**

**Technology:** The startup has built something technically valuable that would take 2-3 years for the acquirer to build internally.

**Talent:** Sometimes called an "acqui-hire" — the acquirer primarily wants the team, not the product. Common in AI/ML, where skilled engineers are scarce.

**Market position:** The startup has captured a market or customer segment the acquirer wants.

**Competitive defense:** If you don't buy them, a competitor will. Facebook's acquisitions of Instagram and WhatsApp were explicitly defensive moves — Zuckerberg saw them as existential threats.

**Revenue:** The startup has recurring revenue that the acquirer can cross-sell to their existing customers.

### The Flipkart-Walmart Deal

The largest startup acquisition in Indian history.

**The context:** By 2018, Flipkart and Amazon India were locked in a billion-dollar battle. Both were losing enormous sums. Flipkart's investors (Softbank, Accel, Tiger Global) were looking for an exit after a decade.

**The deal:** Walmart acquired ~77% of Flipkart for $16 billion in August 2018. Total enterprise value: ~$20 billion.

**What happened to stakeholders:**
- Sachin Bansal (co-founder) sold his entire stake and received ~$1B
- Binny Bansal sold most of his stake and received ~$300M+ (later resigned over conduct allegations)
- SoftBank (large shareholder) received ~$4B — making their earlier bet extremely profitable
- Tiger Global received enormous multiples on their early investments
- Flipkart employees with vested ESOPs received significant payments

**The lesson:** For investors who needed an exit after 10+ years, acquisition was the right path. Walmart got an established Indian e-commerce platform. Everyone with equity got liquidity.

## 4. Secondary Sales — Selling Before Full Exit

Secondary sales allow existing shareholders to sell some or all of their shares to new investors, providing partial liquidity before a full exit.

When does this happen:
- Late-stage companies (Series C+) where there is a clear path to exit
- Growth equity rounds where new investors buy from existing ones
- Founder liquidity: Founders sell a portion of their shares to take some chips off the table

Why founders do secondary sales:
- Financial security (you've worked for years; some real money reduces personal risk)
- Reduced urgency to exit (you're not entirely dependent on the company's exit for your livelihood)
- Better decision-making (you can wait for the right acquisition offer)

The typical size: founders sell enough to be financially secure (maybe ₹5-20 crore) while keeping most of their stake.

Secondary sale in India:
- Regulated by FEMA (Foreign Exchange Management Act) when selling to foreign investors
- Requires company consent in most cases
- Tax implications differ from primary investments

## 5. What Happens to Founders After an Exit

### After an IPO

- You're a public company executive now. Governance, quarterly reporting, investor calls — these become major time commitments.
- Your shares are locked up for 6 months. After that, you can sell gradually (insider trading rules apply).
- You're now accountable to millions of shareholders, not just a handful of VCs.

### After an Acquisition

**The earn-out:** Sometimes the full acquisition price isn't paid upfront. Part is contingent on hitting milestones over 2-3 years. This keeps founders engaged but creates uncertainty.

**The retention package:** Acquirers often offer the founding team cash or stock to stay for 2-3 years. This "golden handcuffs" keeps the team through integration.

**The exit from the company:** After retention period, many founders leave. They've completed the journey, taken their money, and are ready for the next chapter.

**The acquisition in India:** Zomato's acquisition of Blinkit (formerly Grofers) in 2022 for ₹4,447 crore kept the Grofers founding team (Albinder Dhindsa) as head of Blinkit. This is common in India — the founders stay on as business unit leaders.

## 6. IPO vs Acquisition — The Choice

When both paths are available, which should a founder choose?

**Choose IPO when:**
- You believe the company is worth significantly more than current acquisition offers
- You want to retain operational control and continue leading the company
- The company has a strong enough business model to withstand public market scrutiny
- The market timing is right (IPO windows open and close)

**Choose acquisition when:**
- The acquisition offer represents fair to premium value
- Integration with the acquirer genuinely makes the business stronger
- You're tired and want liquidity now
- The IPO market is closed or unfavorable

**The founder dilemma:** Many founders who could IPO choose acquisition because:
- The public market scrutiny is exhausting
- The valuation in the acquisition offer is high
- They want to start their next company
- The board is pressuring for a liquidity event

Instagram's founders (Kevin Systrom and Mike Krieger) sold to Facebook in 2012 for $1 billion. In hindsight, Instagram was worth $100B+ as a standalone business. But at the time, the offer was extraordinary and the founders were ready for liquidity.

## 7. The Wind-Down — The Exit Nobody Talks About

90% of startups fail. Most of these fail quietly — not dramatic bankruptcies, but gradual shutdowns.

**How a startup winds down:**
1. Runway is running out and no new funding is available
2. Revenue is not growing enough to sustain operations
3. Founders acknowledge the company won't work and begin wind-down

**The process:**
- Inform employees first (not through a tweet or Slack message)
- Provide reasonable severance if possible
- Inform investors
- Sell assets (IP, equipment) to partially return investor capital
- Officially dissolve the company

**The employee obligation:** A thoughtful founder takes care of employees first during a wind-down — giving adequate notice, helping with references, paying whatever severance the company can afford.

**The acqui-hire option:** Sometimes a company is failing but has a talented team. A larger company might "acquire" the startup primarily to hire the team (at minimal cost). The investors get a small return; the founders and employees get jobs.

## 8. Tax and Regulatory Considerations for Indian Founders

**Long-term capital gains (LTCG):** If you hold shares for 24+ months, gains are taxed at 12.5% (after Rs 1.25 lakh exemption). Less than 24 months = short-term, taxed at income tax rates.

**ESOPs at exit:** When employees exercise ESOPs and then sell at a liquidity event, they typically face two tax events: (1) exercise (perquisite tax on difference between FMV and strike price) and (2) sale (capital gains). This is complex and requires a CA.

**FEMA compliance:** If any part of your investor base is foreign (very common for Indian startups), share transfers must comply with FEMA reporting requirements.

**Repatriation:** If you're an Indian company acquired by a foreign company, there are limits and requirements for bringing funds into India.

Hire a CA and a lawyer with startup/M&A experience well before an exit process starts.

## Case Study: Unacademy/BYJU's — Cautionary Tales

**Byju's:** At peak, India's most valuable startup ($22B). Raised enormous amounts globally. Expanded aggressively into acquisitions (Aakash, WhiteHat Jr., Epic). Then: allegations of financial irregularities, debt defaults, employee layoffs, NCLT proceedings. An IPO that never came. A cautionary tale of growth without governance.

**The lesson:** A startup needs a functioning company, not just impressive valuations. The exit is not the goal — building something real is.

---

## Summary

- An exit converts paper equity into real money: IPO, acquisition, or wind-down
- **IPO process:** DRHP → SEBI approval → roadshow → subscription → listing; expensive and burdensome but highest potential valuation
- **Acquisition:** Faster, certain, often preferred — you get liquidity without public market obligations
- Paytm's IPO failure shows the risk of premature or overvalued public listings
- Flipkart-Walmart shows a well-executed acquisition exit: all stakeholders got liquidity
- **Wind-down** is the most common exit — treat your team well when it happens
