## 1. Strategy vs Planning — The Critical Difference

Here is a question that trips up most people: what is the difference between a strategy and a plan?

They sound like the same thing. They are not.

**A plan is a set of actions you intend to take.** "We will launch in Delhi in Q1, Bangalore in Q2, and Mumbai in Q3." That is a plan. It tells you *what* and *when*.

**A strategy is a choice about *where to compete* and *how to win*.** It answers a harder question: given all the possible things we could do, what specific set of choices will give us a durable advantage over everyone else who wants what we want?

Here is a way to feel the difference:

Imagine two people preparing for the same competitive exam.

**Person A (has a plan):** Studies 8 hours a day. Covers all chapters. Revises on weekends. Takes mock tests.

**Person B (has a strategy):** Notices that 70% of the marks in this exam come from three specific topic clusters. Notices that most candidates spend equal time on all topics. Decides to go extremely deep on those three clusters, get average on everything else, and spend the last two weeks only on speed — because the exam is time-constrained. Builds their study schedule around that logic.

Person B might study the same 8 hours. But their *choices* are different. They have decided what to prioritize and what to de-prioritize. They have designed an approach that takes advantage of a structural feature of the exam that others are ignoring.

That is what strategy does. It does not just tell you to work hard. It tells you *where* to direct that work, based on a theory about how you will win.

### The "What Not to Do" Insight

In 1996, Michael Porter wrote one of the most cited lines in all of business literature: **"The essence of strategy is choosing what not to do."**

This is deceptively deep.

Every company faces unlimited choices. You could serve every customer or just some. You could sell everything or be focused. You could compete on price or on quality or on speed or on experience. You could expand internationally or go deep domestically. You cannot do all of these at once — not because of money, but because the choices conflict with each other.

A budget airline that also tries to offer a luxury experience is not two things at once. It is incoherent. The processes, the culture, the cost structure, the marketing — they all pull in opposite directions. You end up bad at both.

Real strategy forces the discomfort of saying: *we will not do that*.

Apple does not make cheap computers. Zomato does not deliver groceries. In-N-Out Burger does not have a hundred items on the menu. These are not accidents or failures of ambition — they are **strategic choices**. By saying no to certain things, these companies say yes more powerfully to the things they have chosen.

## 2. Michael Porter's Definition of Strategy (and Why It's Still the Best One)

Michael Porter is a professor at Harvard Business School who has been thinking about strategy since the 1970s. His books and papers are among the most read in business history. And his definition of strategy is still the clearest one available.

Porter says: **Strategy is the creation of a unique and valuable position, involving a different set of activities.**

Let's break that down word by word.

**"Unique and valuable position"** — You are not trying to be the best at doing what everyone else is doing. You are trying to occupy a position in the market that is genuinely different and that customers find genuinely valuable. If you are doing the same thing as your competitors, the only way to win is to be incrementally better, which is exhausting and unsustainable. Position means you have carved out a distinct space.

**"Involving a different set of activities"** — This is the crucial part. Your position is not just a marketing claim. It is backed by a whole system of real activities — how you source, how you sell, how you hire, how you deliver — that are configured differently from your competitors. It is easy to say "we are premium." It is hard to build an entire operation that actually delivers premium. The activities are what make the position real.

Porter distinguishes between **operational effectiveness** and **strategy**. Operational effectiveness means doing the same things as your competitors, but doing them better — being faster, cheaper, more reliable. Strategy means doing *different things*, or doing the same things in *fundamentally different ways*.

Operational effectiveness is necessary. It is not sufficient. Why? Because it is imitable. If you are faster, your competitor can hire efficiency consultants and become faster too. But if your entire *system* is different — if your activities reinforce each other in ways that are difficult to replicate — that is much harder to copy.

### The Southwest Airlines Example

Southwest Airlines is Porter's favorite example, and it is worth spending a moment on.

Every major airline in the 1970s was doing the same thing: multiple fare classes, reserved seats, hub-and-spoke networks connecting cities through major airports, meals on board, partnerships with other airlines so passengers could transfer tickets.

Southwest chose a completely different position: **no frills, one class, point-to-point routes, fast turnarounds, single aircraft type (Boeing 737), and ultra-low prices.**

Here is the key: these choices all *reinforce each other*.

- One aircraft type means mechanics only need to learn one system, which reduces costs.
- Point-to-point routes mean planes spend more time in the air and less time waiting.
- Fast turnarounds (Southwest planes were on the ground 20 minutes between flights, versus 1+ hour for competitors) meant more flights per day with the same plane.
- No meal service made those fast turnarounds possible.
- Low costs enabled low prices, which attracted price-sensitive customers who were previously driving or taking buses.

You cannot copy one piece of this. If a traditional airline tried to offer low prices but kept their hub-and-spoke system, their costs would still be high and they would just lose money. The whole system has to change together. That is what makes Southwest's strategy durable.

## 3. What Makes a Strategy Good? The Three Tests

Not all strategies are created equal. You can have a strategy that is clear, internally consistent, and still terrible. Here is a useful framework for testing whether a strategy is actually good.

### Test 1: Does It Create Real Value for Real Customers?

A strategy is not a financial model or an internal planning document. Ultimately, it has to deliver something that customers want badly enough to pay for. So the first test is: **does this strategy allow us to serve a specific group of customers better than anyone else can?**

If your strategy is "we will be the lowest cost provider," does that actually matter to your customers? In a commodity market like steel or shipping, yes — price is often the deciding factor. In a market like luxury fashion or high-end restaurants, no — customers are not primarily buying on price.

The mistake companies make is defining their strategy internally — "we will have the best operations" or "we will be data-driven" — without anchoring it to what customers care about. Good strategy starts with a clear customer choice.

### Test 2: Is There a Trade-off?

This is Porter's test, and it is ruthless. **A strategy that requires no trade-offs is not a strategy — it is a wish.**

"We will be the cheapest *and* the highest quality *and* the fastest" is not a strategy. It is a list of things everyone wants. If you genuinely could be all three, so could your competitors — there is nothing strategic about it.

Real strategy requires sacrifice. You cannot be everything to everyone. The moment you find yourself saying "we want to be excellent at *everything*," stop and ask: what are we giving up? If the answer is nothing, you do not have a strategy.

Budget airlines gave up comfort, service, flexibility, and food. They kept low price and reliability. Fine dining restaurants gave up speed and price. They kept quality and experience. Zepto gave up selection and price discovery. They kept speed (10 minutes) and convenience. Every real strategic position involves something being deliberately left behind.

### Test 3: Does It Fit Together? (Strategic Fit)

A strategy is not one choice. It is a *system* of choices that reinforce each other. Porter calls this **strategic fit**.

Good strategy has fit when: if you tried to change one part of it, the rest would unravel.

Imagine a fast food chain that decides to compete on speed. Everything in the operation has to fit that: a small, simple menu (complex dishes are slow), standardized ingredients (no customization slows things down), trained staff on exactly one task each, kitchen designed for one direction of flow. If the marketing team then decides to add a premium seasonal special that requires custom preparation — that one decision breaks the fit.

Ask yourself: do our choices reinforce each other, or are some of them pulling in opposite directions? A strategy with poor fit is one where the different parts of the business are constantly in tension instead of reinforcing each other.

## 4. Strategy at Different Levels — Corporate, Business Unit, Functional

Strategy does not happen at just one level of a company. It operates at three levels simultaneously, and understanding this prevents a lot of confusion.

### Corporate Strategy — What Businesses Should We Be In?

Corporate strategy is about the **portfolio of businesses** a company chooses to be in. This is relevant for large companies that operate in multiple markets.

Think about Tata Group. They are in steel, automobiles (Tata Motors, Jaguar Land Rover), IT services (TCS), hotels (Taj Hotels), retail (Trent), airlines (Air India), and consumer goods. Their corporate strategy involves asking: which of these should we own? How do they create more value together than they would as separate companies? When should we enter a new business, and when should we exit?

For a startup or small company, corporate strategy is usually not relevant — you are in one business. But as companies grow, this becomes critical.

### Business Unit Strategy — How Do We Win in This Market?

This is the level most people mean when they say "strategy." **Business unit strategy is about how a specific company competes in a specific market.**

How should Zomato win in the food delivery market? How should Stripe win in the developer payments market? What is Airbnb's approach to winning against hotels? These are business unit strategy questions. The tools Porter provides — the Five Forces framework, the generic strategies of cost leadership and differentiation — all operate at this level.

This is the level of strategy we are primarily concerned with in this course.

### Functional Strategy — How Does Each Function Support the Business Strategy?

Below business unit strategy, each major function — marketing, operations, HR, technology, finance — has its own strategy that should **support and align with** the business unit strategy.

If the business strategy is "compete on premium quality and experience," then:
- The **HR strategy** should focus on hiring unusually talented people and paying them well.
- The **operations strategy** should prioritize quality control over speed.
- The **marketing strategy** should invest heavily in brand storytelling and aspirational imagery.
- The **technology strategy** should invest in tools that make the premium experience possible, not cheap tools that introduce friction.

When these are misaligned — when a premium brand cuts its HR budget and starts hiring cheap — the business unit strategy starts falling apart from the inside.

| Level | Question It Answers | Who Makes It |
|-------|---------------------|--------------|
| Corporate | What businesses should we own? | Board, CEO |
| Business Unit | How do we win in our market? | CEO, senior leadership |
| Functional | How does each function support the strategy? | Functional heads (CMO, COO, CTO, etc.) |

## 5. How Real Companies Think About Strategy (Apple, Amazon, Jio)

Abstract concepts become real when you see them applied. Let us look at how three very different companies have made very different strategic choices — and how those choices explain their outcomes.

### Apple: The Integration Strategy

Apple's strategy is built on one core insight that Steve Jobs articulated in 1997 when he came back to save the company: **the problem with most technology is that the hardware and software are made by different companies who do not coordinate deeply with each other.**

Apple's bet was: what if one company controlled both the hardware and the software, designed to work perfectly together?

This led to choices that seemed crazy at the time:
- Apple would not license its operating system to other computer makers (unlike Microsoft, which sold Windows to everyone).
- Apple would design its own chips (eventually).
- Apple would make fewer products, not more — Jobs famously cut Apple's product line from 350+ products to 10 when he returned.
- Apple would charge premium prices and never compete on price.

These choices reinforce each other. Because Apple controls the hardware, it can optimize the software for that exact hardware — this is why iPhones traditionally ran smoother than Android phones with technically superior specs. Because they do not compete on price, they can invest in premium materials. Because they have a smaller product line, they can invest more deeply in each product. Because the ecosystem is integrated, customers who buy one Apple device are drawn toward buying more (the iPhone locks you into iMessage, which pulls your friends toward iPhone; the Mac works seamlessly with the iPhone; AirPods connect instantly to all Apple devices).

The strategic fit is extraordinary. Every choice reinforces every other choice.

The result: Apple is now the most valuable company in history, with profit margins that no other hardware company has ever achieved. In 2023, Apple's iPhone segment alone generated more profit than the entire Microsoft company.

### Amazon: The Flywheel Strategy

Amazon's strategy is built on a diagram that Jeff Bezos sketched on a napkin in 2001. It is called the Amazon Flywheel and it looks like this:

```
Lower Prices
    ↓
More Customer Visits
    ↓
More Sellers Attracted to Platform
    ↓
Wider Product Selection
    ↓
Better Customer Experience
    ↓
Lower Prices (cycle repeats)
```

And alongside this, as the flywheel generates revenue, Amazon reinvests it into:

```
More Revenue → Lower Cost Structure → Lower Prices (again)
```

The insight is that each element of the business *feeds* every other element. Lower prices attract customers. More customers attract sellers. More sellers improve selection. Better selection improves customer experience. Better customer experience justifies more investment. More revenue lowers unit costs. Lower costs enable lower prices.

This is not a strategy about a single product or a single capability. It is a strategy about **building a self-reinforcing system**. Every business decision Amazon makes is evaluated against this flywheel: does this accelerate it or slow it down?

Amazon Web Services (AWS) was not an accident. As Amazon scaled its internal infrastructure, they realized they had built something valuable that others would pay for. AWS is now Amazon's highest-margin business. Prime was a deliberate experiment in changing the psychology of purchasing by eliminating the per-order shipping decision. Amazon Logistics was built because third-party carriers were a bottleneck in the flywheel.

### Jio: The Destruction Strategy

In 2016, Mukesh Ambani's Reliance launched Jio, a telecom network. The strategy was unlike anything India had seen.

The choice was simple and brutal: **give everything away free, build an enormous customer base, then sell them everything else.**

For six months, Jio offered free voice calls and free 4G data to anyone who signed up. Not cheap. Free. The incumbent telecom providers — Airtel, Vodafone, Idea — could not compete. Their business models depended on charging for calls and data. Jio had structured its costs (it had built an all-IP network from scratch, with no legacy infrastructure to maintain) so that it could afford to operate near zero marginal cost.

The result: Jio added 100 million subscribers in 170 days — the fastest ramp-up in telecom history. Competitor revenues collapsed. Vodafone and Idea merged out of desperation. Some smaller players went bankrupt.

Then, once Jio had the customers, Reliance began selling them everything else: JioMart for groceries, JioSaavn for music, JioCinema for video, JioFiber for broadband, financial services, even smartphones. The telecom network was not the product — it was the acquisition channel for everything else.

The strategic insight: in digital businesses, distribution is the most valuable thing. If you can own the distribution (in Jio's case, the actual internet connection going into people's homes and phones), you can sell anything through it. The cost of acquiring a customer through the telecom giveaway was high in the short term and cheap in the long term.

## 6. The Strategy Toolkit Overview

Strategy has its own set of tools and frameworks — ways of analyzing a situation and making decisions. You will learn each of these in depth in the chapters that follow. Here is a preview of the most important ones.

| Tool | What It Does | When to Use It |
|------|-------------|----------------|
| Porter's Five Forces | Analyzes the competitive structure of an industry | Before entering a new market or understanding why an industry is profitable or not |
| SWOT Analysis | Maps Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats | Initial strategic assessment; good for structuring a situation |
| Blue Ocean Strategy | Finds uncontested market space by combining or eliminating industry features | When the current market is overcrowded and commoditizing |
| Competitive Moat Analysis | Identifies durable sources of competitive advantage | Understanding why a business will stay ahead of competitors |
| Value Chain Analysis | Maps every activity in the business to find where value is created and costs incurred | Finding where to improve, where to outsource, where the real advantages lie |
| Jobs to Be Done | Understands what "job" customers are really hiring a product to do | Product strategy, finding new angles that competitors miss |
| OKRs | Translates strategy into measurable quarterly goals | Operational planning and team alignment |

You do not need to use all of these simultaneously. Different frameworks illuminate different aspects of a problem. Learning when to reach for which tool is one of the real skills of strategy.

## 7. What "No Strategy" Looks Like — Byju's as a Case Study

It is easy to describe good strategy in theory. Sometimes the clearest way to understand what strategy is — is to watch a company that had none.

Byju's was founded in 2011 by Byju Raveendran, a teacher who turned his coaching classes into a learning app. By 2021, it was the world's most valuable edtech company, worth $22 billion.

And then it collapsed spectacularly. By 2024, it was bankrupt, facing hundreds of millions in lawsuits, with thousands of employees unpaid.

What happened? The company did not fail because it had a bad product. Many students genuinely liked the learning videos. It failed, in large part, because **it never had a coherent strategy.**

### What Byju's Did Instead of Strategy

**1. It grew everything, everywhere, all at once.**

Byju's was not a focused company with a clear customer and a clear value proposition. It was a machine for spending investor money on growth at any cost. At its peak, it had acquired 16+ companies in a single year — Aakash Educational Services, Great Learning, WhiteHat Jr, Toppr, Epic, Tynker, and more. These acquisitions spanned K-12 tutoring, coding for kids, adult professional education, international markets, and more.

There was no strategic logic connecting them. The strategy appeared to be: "capture as much of the education market as possible before anyone else does." But that is not a strategy — it is a land grab funded by cheap money.

**2. It confused revenue with value creation.**

Byju's sales teams were notorious for pressuring parents into taking large loans — sometimes ₹60,000 to ₹1.5 lakh — to buy learning subscriptions for their children. In many documented cases, parents did not understand that they had taken a loan. The company booked this as revenue.

This is growth without a coherent customer strategy. Who is your core customer? What specific problem are you solving for them better than anyone else? Byju's never had a clear answer. Their customer was "any parent willing to pay," and the sales model was aggressive selling rather than value delivery.

**3. It had no strategic fit.**

WhiteHat Jr taught coding to very young children. Aakash prepared students for IIT-JEE and medical entrance exams. Great Learning served working professionals. Epic was a US-based digital library for young children. These businesses required completely different operations, different teaching approaches, different sales motions, different cultures.

Running them all under one roof created chaos. There were no synergies. The businesses did not reinforce each other. They just consumed capital.

**4. When the market turned, there was nothing durable to fall back on.**

During COVID, every edtech company boomed — because schools were closed and online learning was the only option. But when schools reopened, the unsustainable nature of Byju's growth became clear. Customers who had been locked in by aggressive sales tactics cancelled. The companies they had acquired were not integrating. Cash was burning.

A company with a real strategy has something durable — a genuine competitive advantage, a loyal customer base, a unique capability. When the market turns, those things persist. Byju's had none of these. It had scale and brand, built on spending, not on earning.

### The Lesson

The absence of strategy does not look like chaos. It often looks like ambition. Byju's was growing fast, spending big, acquiring boldly. It had a story that investors loved. But at no point did anyone seem to ask: *what specific position are we building? What are we choosing not to do? How does this all fit together?*

Strategy is uncomfortable because it requires saying no. Byju's said yes to everything — and ended up with nothing.

## 8. How to Develop a Business Strategy — A Practical Framework

Okay. You understand what strategy is. How do you actually build one?

There is no single correct method. But here is a practical five-step process that synthesizes the best thinking in the field. Think of it as a conversation you have with yourself and your team, in order.

### Step 1: Understand Where You Are (Situation Analysis)

Before you can decide where to go, you need to be honest about where you are.

This means:
- **Who are your current customers?** Not "everyone." Who actually buys from you today, and why?
- **What do you do well?** Not what you wish you did well. What do you actually do better than your competitors?
- **What is the structure of your industry?** Is it consolidating or fragmenting? Are margins high or low? Who has power — buyers, suppliers, or you?
- **What are the major trends that could change the game?** Regulatory changes, technology shifts, demographic changes.

This is where tools like Porter's Five Forces and SWOT analysis are genuinely useful — as structured ways to organize your thinking about the situation.

### Step 2: Identify Strategic Options

Given your situation, what are the distinct strategic paths available to you?

A key insight here from Roger Martin, a strategy scholar, is to think in terms of **"where to play"** and **"how to win"**:

```
WHERE TO PLAY:
  - Which customers?
  - Which geographies?
  - Which products/services?
  - Which channels?

HOW TO WIN:
  - Cost leadership? (cheaper than everyone)
  - Differentiation? (better than everyone at something specific)
  - Focus/Niche? (be the best for a specific narrow customer)
```

Lay out two or three genuinely distinct strategic options. Not variations on a theme — real alternatives that involve different customers, different capabilities, different trade-offs.

### Step 3: Test Each Option Against the Three Tests

For each option, ask:
1. Does it create real value for real customers?
2. Does it require genuine trade-offs (i.e., what are we giving up)?
3. Does the system of choices fit together?

Eliminate options that fail these tests.

### Step 4: Choose — and Commit

This is the hardest step, because choosing means giving things up.

Once you have evaluated your options honestly, **choose one** and commit to it. Not "we will try both and see." Not "let's keep our options open." A real strategic choice is a commitment that you will direct your resources — money, time, attention, talent — in one direction.

The number of good companies that have been destroyed by indecision and hedging is large. It is emotionally comfortable to "keep options open." It is strategically corrosive.

### Step 5: Cascade Into Functional Strategy and Goals

Once the strategic choice is made, communicate it and cascade it:
- Every functional team (marketing, product, operations, finance) should be able to explain how their decisions are in service of the strategy.
- Set goals (OKRs or KPIs) that measure progress on the strategic direction, not just operational efficiency.
- Revisit the strategy annually, or whenever a significant external change happens.

```
STRATEGY DEVELOPMENT PROCESS

[1] UNDERSTAND          [2] IDENTIFY            [3] TEST
    Current position   →   Strategic options   →   Against 3 tests
    Customers               Where to play           Creates value?
    Capabilities            How to win              Requires trade-offs?
    Industry forces                                 Strategic fit?
        |
        ↓
[4] CHOOSE              [5] CASCADE
    Commit to one      →   Into functions
    strategic path         Set goals
                           Communicate
                           Revisit annually
```

## 9. Strategy in Startups — When and Why to Be Flexible (Pivots)

Everything we have discussed so far is mostly applicable to established businesses with defined markets and known customers. Startups are different, and it is worth being honest about that.

**In the early stage of a startup, you do not have enough information to write a full strategy.**

You do not know who your best customers are. You do not know what they will pay. You do not know which channels work. You do not know what your real competitive advantage is. Writing a ten-page strategic plan in this context is not strategy — it is fiction.

This is why startup methodology (which we will cover in depth in Part 4) emphasizes learning over planning. You run experiments. You talk to users. You ship fast and watch what happens. You are trying to *discover* the strategic position, not execute one you already know.

But — and this is crucial — **the discipline of strategy still applies at the startup stage, just differently.**

Even early-stage startups have to make choices:
- Which customer segment should we focus on first? (Not all of them.)
- What is the single problem we are solving? (Not every problem the customer has.)
- What metric tells us if we are succeeding? (You need one number, not twenty.)

The startup version of strategy is not about writing a five-year plan. It is about **being clear on your current hypothesis** — "we believe that X customer has Y problem and will pay Z for our solution" — and running the most efficient test of that hypothesis.

### What Is a Pivot?

A pivot is when a startup realizes its current hypothesis was wrong and deliberately shifts to a different one.

Famous pivots:
- **Instagram** was originally a location check-in app called Burbn. They noticed users only really cared about the photo sharing feature. They stripped everything else out and became Instagram.
- **Slack** was originally an internal tool built by a gaming company called Glitch that failed. The communication tool they had built for their own team turned out to be the valuable thing.
- **YouTube** originally launched as a video dating site ("Tune In, Hook Up"). When users started uploading all kinds of videos, the founders pivoted to a general video platform.
- **Twitter** started as a podcast directory platform called Odeo. When iTunes launched and killed their core idea, they pivoted to the short status update concept.

A pivot is not failure. It is the process of discovering a better strategic hypothesis by discarding a worse one. The ability to pivot effectively — changing direction without losing all your momentum — is one of the most important skills a startup founder can have.

The key distinction, however, is between a **pivot** (a deliberate change in strategy based on learning) and **drift** (randomly chasing whatever seems interesting or whatever investors seem excited about). Byju's was not pivoting. It was drifting.

### When Should a Startup Solidify Its Strategy?

Generally, once you have found **product-market fit** — the moment when you have undeniable evidence that a specific group of customers love your product and are pulling for more — it is time to stop experimenting with your core direction and start executing.

Before product-market fit: explore, experiment, pivot.
After product-market fit: focus, execute, build moats.

The mistake many founders make is treating the post-PMF company like a pre-PMF startup — continuing to experiment widely instead of going deep on the thing that is working.

## 10. Summary

Let us bring everything together.

**Strategy is the set of choices that determine where you compete and how you win.** It is different from planning (which is about execution). It is different from operational excellence (which is about being good at what competitors are also doing). Strategy is about *position* — carving out a unique space that is valuable to customers and durable against competition.

The best definition comes from Michael Porter: **strategy is the creation of a unique and valuable position, involving a different set of activities.**

A good strategy passes three tests:
1. It creates real value for real, specific customers.
2. It involves genuine trade-offs — it requires you to give something up.
3. Its choices fit together and reinforce each other.

Strategy operates at three levels: corporate (what businesses should we be in?), business unit (how do we win in this market?), and functional (how does each function support the strategy?).

Great companies like Apple, Amazon, and Jio all have strategies with deep internal logic — systems of reinforcing choices that are difficult to copy. Companies without strategy, like Byju's, often grow fast but collapse because the growth has no foundation.

To build a strategy, work through the five steps: understand your situation, identify real options, test them against the three tests, choose one and commit, and cascade into functions and goals.

In startups, strategy is discovered more than planned — but the discipline still applies. Pivots are valid. Drift is not.

The single most important habit a strategist develops is the ability to say no to good things in order to say yes to great ones.

## Exercises and Discussion Questions

1. **The Trade-off Test**: Pick any company you know well — it could be a startup, a local business, or a global brand. Write down three things they deliberately do NOT do. For each one, explain why not doing it is actually a strategic advantage. (If you cannot find three things they say no to, that might tell you something about their strategy.)

2. **The Fit Analysis**: Think about a company whose strategy you admire. Map out at least five of its key activities (how it hires, how it prices, how it distributes, how it markets, how it builds its product). Draw arrows between activities that reinforce each other. Do they fit together, or are there tensions?

3. **The Byju's Post-Mortem**: If you were a strategic advisor at Byju's in 2019, before the collapse, what would you have recommended they do differently? Be specific: what would you have told them to stop doing? What would you have told them to double down on?

4. **Where to Play / How to Win**: You want to start a food business in a city of your choice. Write down three genuinely distinct strategic options (not variations on the same theme — real alternatives with different customers, different economics, and different trade-offs). For each one, identify the core trade-off (what you are giving up) and the core advantage (why this position is defensible).

5. **The Amazon Flywheel**: Amazon's flywheel is one of the most powerful strategic concepts in business history. Your challenge: pick a different company and draw their flywheel. What is the self-reinforcing loop that, once started, keeps getting stronger? If you cannot find a flywheel, what does that tell you about the company's strategic position?

6. **Startup Pivot or Drift?**: Instagram, Slack, YouTube, and Twitter all pivoted successfully. Find one company that you believe *drifted* rather than pivoted — that changed direction not because of learning, but because of confusion or investor pressure. What was the outcome? What should they have done instead?

7. **Strategy vs Plan**: You are launching a tutoring business for high school students preparing for competitive exams. Write a *plan* (a list of actions) for the first three months. Then write a *strategy* (a position and the choices that define it). What is the difference between the two documents you just wrote?
