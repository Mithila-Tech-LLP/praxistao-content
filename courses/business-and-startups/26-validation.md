# Chapter 26: Validation — Talking to Users Before You Build Anything

*The most expensive mistake in startups is building something nobody wants. This chapter teaches you how to avoid it.*

---

## 1. The Most Expensive Mistake in Startups

CB Insights analyzed hundreds of failed startups and found the #1 reason they failed: **no market need** — building something customers didn't want.

Not running out of money. Not being beaten by a competitor. Not bad technology. The product didn't solve a real problem.

The tragedy is that this is almost entirely preventable. Most of these founders could have discovered the problem before writing a single line of code — if they had talked to users honestly.

Validation is the art of learning what's true about your business *before* you've invested months building it.

---

## 2. What Validation Is (and What It Isn't)

**Validation IS:**
- Learning from the market whether your hypotheses are correct
- Reducing the biggest risks in your business model *before* committing large resources
- Finding the gap between what customers say they want and what they actually do

**Validation IS NOT:**
- Getting people to say "that sounds interesting"
- Showing friends and family your idea and asking if they like it
- Running a survey with 500 random people
- Building a small version of the product to see if people use it (that's an MVP — different thing)

The problem is that validation *feels* like it's happening when it isn't. People are polite. They say your idea is interesting. They don't want to hurt your feelings. You walk away thinking you've validated your idea when you've actually just confirmed that people can be kind.

---

## 3. The Mom Test — How to Ask Questions That Don't Lie to You

Rob Fitzpatrick wrote a short, brilliant book called *The Mom Test* that every founder should read before doing any user research.

The core idea: **your mom will lie to you about your startup.** Not maliciously — she loves you and wants to be supportive. But if you ask her "do you like my idea?" she'll say yes even if she would never use it.

The Mom Test says: **ask questions that your mom couldn't lie to you about, even if she wanted to.**

### Bad Questions (that lead to lies):

"Do you think this is a good idea?" — Of course they'll say yes.

"Would you pay for this?" — People are terrible at predicting their own future behavior.

"How much would you pay for this?" — Hypothetical prices are worthless.

"What features would you want?" — Customers are not product designers. They'll give you a wishlist, not priorities.

### Good Questions (that reveal truth):

"How do you currently solve this problem?" — If they have no current solution, the problem might not be painful enough.

"How much time/money does this problem cost you per week?" — Specific, past behavior. Not hypothetical.

"The last time this happened, what did you do?" — Concrete, specific, past actions are highly predictive.

"Can you walk me through your workflow for X?" — Watching someone's actual process reveals problems they don't even know they have.

"Have you ever tried to solve this before? What happened?" — Prior attempts show urgency and the graveyard of failed solutions.

## 4. How to Do Customer Interviews Properly

### Who to Talk To

Not your friends. Not your family. Not people who already like you.

Talk to:
- People who already have the exact problem you're solving
- People in the role you're targeting (the actual decision-maker, not their assistant)
- People who are actively spending money to solve the problem imperfectly

Finding these people:
- Reddit communities related to your problem
- LinkedIn (message people with the job title you're targeting)
- Facebook groups
- Offline events and meetups
- Show up where they work (ask for 15 minutes at a clinic, construction site, school)
- Warm introductions from your network

Aim for at least 25-30 interviews before drawing conclusions.

### What to Ask

Structure each 30-minute interview:

**Opening (5 min):** Learn about them. "Tell me about your role. How long have you been doing this? What does a typical day look like?"

**Problem exploration (15 min):** Dig into the problem area. "How do you currently handle X? Walk me through the last time you dealt with Y. How much time does this take?" (The Mom Test questions)

**Specific pain (5 min):** Identify the most painful part. "What's the worst part of this process? If you could fix one thing, what would it be?"

**Existing solutions (5 min):** "What tools/solutions have you tried? What do you like/hate about them?"

**Never mention your product** until you have all the information you need. If you mention it too early, they start responding to your idea rather than telling you the truth.

### Taking Notes and Finding Patterns

Record (with permission) or take detailed notes. After 10-15 interviews, look for:
- **Problems mentioned repeatedly** — three people independently mentioning the same pain is signal
- **Unexpected problems** — problems you didn't know about that seem widespread
- **Jobs-to-be-done** — what is the underlying goal users are trying to achieve?
- **Current solutions** — the "good enough" alternatives you'll be competing with

## 5. Smoke Tests and Landing Pages — Fake-It-Till-You-Make-It Validation

A smoke test asks: "Will people take action before the product exists?"

The classic version: build a landing page describing your product, with a waitlist or "Sign up" button. Run some cheap ads or post in relevant communities. Measure how many people sign up.

This isn't about lying — the page should say "Coming soon" or "Join the waitlist." You're measuring intent, not actual purchase.

**What counts as validation:**
- High sign-up rate (10%+ of visitors)
- People actually paying in advance (most powerful signal)
- People sharing the page with friends unprompted

**What doesn't count:**
- People saying they'd use it in a survey
- High social engagement on a post describing the idea

**Indian examples:**

Before Zepto built its full app, the founding team tested with a WhatsApp group — customers would send messages, a small team would fulfill orders manually. Crude, but it showed the behavior was real before they invested in technology.

## 6. Concierge MVP — Doing It Manually Before Building Technology

The concierge MVP is delivering the service *manually* before automating it.

You become the "software" — doing by hand what the eventual product will do automatically. This lets you:
- Validate demand quickly
- Learn exactly what the product needs to do
- Serve early customers without any technology investment

**Examples:**

**Airbnb:** Before building a platform, the founders rented out their own apartment. They personally photographed it, listed it on Craigslist, met guests, handled payments, and managed issues. This taught them everything about what hosts and guests needed.

**Zappos:** Before building an inventory system, founder Nick Swinmurn would go to local shoe stores, photograph the shoes, post them online, and when someone ordered, he'd go buy the shoes and ship them. No inventory risk. Pure validation that people would buy shoes online.

**Urban Company (then UrbanClap):** Before a scalable platform existed, the founders personally called service professionals, matched them with customers, coordinated schedules by phone. They did this for hundreds of bookings before automating.

## 7. How Dropbox Validated With a Video

Drew Houston of Dropbox had a technical product (file sync across devices) that was hard to demo.

Before building the full product, he made a 3-minute video showing what the finished product would do. He posted it on Hacker News.

Overnight, signups went from 5,000 to 75,000.

That was validation: people saw the demo, understood the value immediately, and wanted it. No product built. Just a video.

**The lesson:** You don't need the product to validate demand. You need to show enough of the value proposition that people can decide if they want it.

## 8. How Much Validation Is Enough?

There's no formula, but here's a practical guide:

**Minimum to proceed to building your MVP:**
- 25+ customer interviews, with consistent themes across them
- At least 10 people who say "I would pay for this right now" (and you can follow up with them)
- At least one person who has given you money or signed a letter of intent

**Warning signs to keep validating:**
- You can only convince people who already know you
- The problem is real but people are tolerating it (not actively trying to solve it)
- Everyone's use case is slightly different — no consistent problem emerges

**Signs you've over-validated (yes, this exists):**
- You've been talking to customers for 6+ months without building anything
- You keep finding "one more thing to validate" to avoid the risk of building
- Your learning has plateaued (last 5 interviews told you nothing new)

---

## 9. Pivoting Based on What You Learn

Validation often tells you that your original hypothesis was wrong. This is good — you learned cheaply.

The question becomes: what to do with this information?

**When to pivot:**
- The problem is different from what you assumed (but still interesting)
- A different customer segment cares much more
- The solution needs to be fundamentally different

**When to refine (not pivot):**
- You were right about the problem, wrong about a feature
- The messaging was wrong, not the product
- You were talking to the wrong subset of the target customer

**When to stop:**
- After 30+ interviews across multiple customer types, you can't find consistent, painful problems
- Every potential customer already has a solution they're happy with
- The problem exists but nobody is willing to pay to solve it

## 10. Case Study: How Zepto Validated 10-Minute Delivery

**The hypothesis:** Urban Indian households would pay a premium for grocery delivery in under 10 minutes, if the experience was reliable.

**The uncertainty:** Would people actually order groceries on demand? Or would they plan ahead (weekly grocery runs)? Would they pay a premium? Could 10 minutes be reliably achieved?

**Validation approach:**
- The founders (both 19 years old) manually tested the concept in Mumbai using a WhatsApp group
- Customers would message the group, a small team would pick and deliver from a nearby inventory point
- They ran this for weeks before building any technology
- What they learned: urban Indians did order on impulse (running out of milk, wanting snacks now), they valued speed over price, and 10 minutes was achievable within a 2km radius with the right inventory

**What they built after validation:** The full dark store model, with software, operations, and logistics optimized around the validated behavior.

**The result:** Raised $200 million in their first year. One of India's fastest-growing startups.

---

## Summary

- The #1 startup killer is building something nobody wants — entirely preventable through validation
- **The Mom Test:** ask about past behavior, not future hypotheses; your interviewee can't lie to you about what they actually did
- Customer interviews require talking to strangers, not friends; asking open questions; and looking for patterns across many conversations
- **Smoke tests** (landing pages, pre-orders) reveal whether people will act on their interest
- **Concierge MVPs** let you validate the business model manually before building technology
- Know when to stop validating and start building — excessive validation is also a failure mode
