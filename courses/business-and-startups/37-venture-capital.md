# Chapter 37: Venture Capital — How VC Works, Who Gets It, and Why

*Venture capital is the fuel of the startup economy. It's also widely misunderstood. This chapter explains exactly how VC works — from LP to founder.*

---

## 1. Where Does VC Money Come From?

Venture capital firms don't invest their own money. They raise money from **Limited Partners (LPs)** and invest that on behalf of LPs, keeping a fee and a share of the profits.

**Who are LPs?**
- University endowments (Harvard, Yale, Princeton manage billions in endowments, ~15-20% goes to VC)
- Pension funds (CalPERS, GPIF — managing retirement savings)
- Family offices (ultra-wealthy families who invest as a business)
- Insurance companies
- Sovereign wealth funds (GIC Singapore, Abu Dhabi Investment Authority)
- Foundations (Ford Foundation, Gates Foundation)
- Fund of funds (investors who invest in VC funds rather than companies directly)
- High-net-worth individuals

**The Fund Structure:**

A VC firm raises a fund: "We're raising a ₹500 crore fund." LPs commit capital. The fund has a 10-year life:
- Years 1-4: Deployment (investing in startups)
- Years 4-7: Active portfolio management (helping portfolio companies, leading follow-on rounds)
- Years 7-10: Harvesting (exits happen, capital is returned to LPs)

**The Fee Structure — "2 and 20":**
- **2% management fee:** 2% of committed capital per year, paid to the fund to cover salaries and operations. On a ₹500 crore fund: ₹10 crore/year.
- **20% carry:** The VC firm keeps 20% of profits after returning LP capital. On a ₹500 crore fund that returns ₹2,000 crore: profit is ₹1,500 crore, VC keeps 20% = ₹300 crore.

This creates a specific incentive: VCs need fund-level returns, not just successful individual investments.

---

## 2. The VC Math — Why VCs Need 100x Returns

This is the most important concept for understanding why VCs behave the way they do.

A typical VC fund invests in 20-30 companies. The expected outcome:
- **15-20 companies (60-70%):** Die or return less than invested
- **5-8 companies (20-30%):** Return 1-3x (small wins)
- **2-4 companies (10%):** Return 10-30x
- **1-2 companies (5%):** Return 50-100x+

The entire fund's returns come from these 1-2 companies. Everything else is rounding error.

**Example math:**
A $100M fund needs to return at least $300M (3x) to LPs to be considered a "good" fund (after fees and carry).

If the fund has 25 investments averaging $4M each, and 20 return nothing, and 5 return various amounts... one company needs to return $100M+ (25x) to make the math work.

This means: a VC who invests in your startup needs to believe your company could be worth $500M-$1B+ for their $4M check to matter. If your startup can only reach ₹100 crore in value, it's not interesting to most VCs — even if it's a great business.

**The implication for founders:** VC is not right for businesses with moderate ambition. If you want to build a ₹100 crore company, you don't need — and shouldn't want — VC. If you want to build a ₹10,000 crore company, VC is a tool that might make sense.

## 3. Major Indian VCs and Their Profiles

### Peak XV Partners (formerly Sequoia India)
The most prestigious VC firm in India. Has invested in Zomato, Razorpay, Byju's, Meesho, CRED, Freshworks, and dozens more. Known for backing companies very early and staying through IPO. Manages multiple funds including a seed program (Surge).

### Accel India
Bangalore-based, one of the longest-running US VCs in India. Portfolio includes Flipkart (early investor), Swiggy, BrowserStack, Clevertap, and many SaaS companies. Strong in early to mid-stage.

### Elevation Capital
Formerly Saif Partners. Has invested in Meesho, Paytm, Unacademy, ShareChat. Known for early-stage conviction bets on consumer and fintech.

### Blume Ventures
India-focused, early-stage. Portfolio includes Grey Orange (robotics), Unacademy, Dunzo. Known for backing first-time founders and being more founder-friendly than larger funds.

### Matrix Partners India
Portfolio: OYO, Ola, Dailyhunt, Stanza Living. Strong consumer and enterprise track record.

### Global VCs Active in India

**Tiger Global:** Aggressive cheque writer, minimal governance demands, famously fast. Was the most active growth-stage investor in India in 2019-2021.

**SoftBank:** Known for the Vision Fund and making controversial, very large bets (OYO, WeWork globally). Has had spectacular successes and failures.

**General Catalyst, Lightspeed, Bessemer:** US firms with significant India presence.

## 4. How VCs Make Decisions

### The Funnel

A mid-sized VC firm might see:
- 1,000+ pitch decks per year
- Take 200 first meetings
- Do deeper diligence on 40
- Invest in 8-10

The rejection rate is >99%. This is not personal.

### What They Look For

**At Seed Stage:**
- Team quality (most important at this stage)
- Market size (must be large)
- Initial traction (some evidence the thesis is real)
- Differentiation (why won't this get crushed by competition?)

**At Series A:**
- Product-market fit signals (retention curves, NPS, organic growth)
- Repeatable acquisition (at least one channel that scales)
- Clear path to ₹100+ crore in revenue
- Team that can execute at scale

**At Series B and beyond:**
- Proven business model with improving unit economics
- Clear growth strategy
- Management team being built out

### The "Conviction Test"

Every major VC investment requires the partner making the case to the partnership. The question is not "is this a good company?" but "can I credibly defend this investment to my partners, and do I believe it could return the fund?"

A $100M fund investing $4M needs a potential $100M+ outcome. The partner must believe that's possible.

## 5. The Funding Process

### Step 1: Building Relationships Before You Need Money

The best fundraises start 12-18 months before you need capital. Build relationships with VCs by:
- Sending quarterly updates even before fundraising
- Asking for introductions to potential customers (give before you ask)
- Being genuinely helpful to their portfolio companies

### Step 2: Getting Warm Introductions

Cold emails to VCs have very low hit rates. The most effective route: find someone (another founder, advisor, angel investor) who can make a personal introduction and vouch for you.

### Step 3: The Process

1. First meeting (30-45 min): Is this interesting at all?
2. Second meeting (60-90 min): Deeper dive into product, market, team
3. Partner meeting: You present to the full partnership
4. Diligence: Customer calls, reference checks, financial review
5. Term sheet
6. Closing

Timeline: 6 weeks to 6 months. The fastest fundraises happen when you create competitive urgency (multiple VCs are interested simultaneously).

## 6. The VC-Founder Relationship After the Check

When a VC invests, they typically get:
- Equity (the purpose of the investment)
- A board seat (at Series A+)
- Protective provisions (they can block certain major decisions)
- Information rights (regular financial reporting)
- Pro-rata rights (the right to invest in future rounds)

The board seat is the most impactful. The board approves major decisions: hiring/firing the CEO, major strategic pivots, M&A, fundraising terms. Having VCs on your board is a governance relationship, not just a financial one.

**The best VC-founder relationships:**
- Investor adds value beyond money (introductions, advice, recruiting help)
- Open, honest communication about challenges
- Aligned on long-term vision
- Investor respects founder's judgment on product and market

**The worst VC-founder relationships:**
- Investor adds pressure without helping solve problems
- Founder hides bad news to avoid difficult conversations
- Misaligned timelines (founder wants to build 20 years; investor needs exit in 7)
- Investor replaces founder with a "professional CEO" at a key moment

## 7. VC Red Flags — Signs of a Bad Investor

Not all VCs are equal. Signs of a problematic investor:

- **Moves very fast without doing real diligence:** They might not have done the work to truly believe in your company
- **Promises a lot of help but is vague about specifics:** "We have great networks" without naming specific connections
- **Aggressive terms with lots of control provisions:** Liquidation preferences >1x, ratchets, full-ratchet anti-dilution
- **Invests in your competitor** (some do this without disclosure)
- **Treats you like a hire, not a founder:** Board dynamics where the VC makes operating decisions they don't understand

The best way to check: call founders of their existing portfolio companies. Ask: "What is this investor like when things go badly?"

## 8. The Alternative — Y Combinator and Accelerators

**Y Combinator (YC)** is the world's most prestigious startup accelerator:
- 3-month program in San Francisco
- Invests $500K for 7% equity
- Alumni include Airbnb, Dropbox, Stripe, Coinbase, Reddit

For Indian founders, YC is one of the best early-stage paths. Indian YC alumni: Razorpay, Meesho, Clear (ClearTax), Groww, Zepto, and dozens more.

**Other accelerators in India:**
- **Antler India:** Early-stage, equity-for-program model
- **100X.VC:** Indian seed fund with a standardized SAFE model
- **CIIE (IIM Ahmedabad):** Social enterprise focus
- **NSRCEL (IIM Bangalore):** IIM Bangalore's incubator

## 9. Is VC Right for Your Business?

VC makes sense when:
- Your market is large (potential for $500M+ revenue)
- Your business needs capital to build before it can earn (heavy infrastructure)
- Speed matters and a competitor can win with first-mover advantage
- You're comfortable with the governance and dilution trade-offs

VC does NOT make sense when:
- You can build profitably without external capital
- Your market is moderate in size
- You want full control and flexibility
- You're not comfortable with a 7-10 year VC timeline and exit pressure

Most businesses should never take VC. VC-backed startups are a tiny fraction of all successful businesses. The glorification of VC in startup culture creates a distorted view: the path to success requires raising money from investors. It doesn't.

---

## Summary

- VC firms raise from LPs and invest in startups, keeping 2% management fee + 20% of profits
- The VC math requires 100x returns on some investments to make the fund economics work
- This means VCs only invest in businesses with potential to be worth billions — not every business qualifies
- The major Indian VCs: Peak XV, Accel, Elevation, Blume, Matrix
- The fundraising process: relationships first, warm intros, meetings, partner meeting, diligence, term sheet
- VC is NOT right for most businesses — it's a specific tool for specific ambitions
