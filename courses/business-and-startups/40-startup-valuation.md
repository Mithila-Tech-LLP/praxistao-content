# Chapter 40: Startup Valuation — How Startups Get Valued at Millions Before Making a Rupee

*Startup valuation is part art, part negotiation, and part fiction. This chapter explains all three parts.*

---

## 1. The Valuation Problem

Traditional businesses are valued based on their earnings. A company that earns ₹10 crore per year might be worth ₹70-100 crore (7-10x earnings).

But most startups don't have earnings. Many don't have revenue. Some haven't even launched yet. How do you value something that currently generates nothing?

The answer: **you value the potential, not the present.**

This is philosophically important. When an investor values your startup at ₹50 crore with ₹0 in revenue, they're saying: "I believe there is a 50% chance this becomes worth ₹100 crore, plus upside possibilities." They're pricing a probability distribution of outcomes, not current cash flows.

This is also why startup valuations can seem absurd, collapse rapidly when expectations change, and why the same company can be worth very different things to different investors.

---

## 2. Valuation for Traditional Businesses vs Startups

| Traditional Business | Startup |
|---------------------|---------|
| Valued on earnings (P/E ratio) | Valued on revenue multiples or comparables |
| Predictable cash flows | Unpredictable, potentially explosive growth |
| Present performance drives value | Future potential drives value |
| Methodical DCF (discounted cash flow) | Comps + negotiation + gut |
| Low uncertainty = precise valuation | High uncertainty = wide range of outcomes |

The more a startup starts to look like a real business (predictable revenue, profitability), the more traditional valuation methods apply.

---

## 3. Common Startup Valuation Methods

### Method 1: Revenue Multiples (Most Common for Post-Revenue Startups)

Valuation = Annual Revenue × Sector Multiple

**Common multiples (approximate, vary by market conditions):**
- SaaS companies: 5-15x ARR (in good markets, 20-30x for high-growth)
- E-commerce: 1-3x GMV or 2-5x Net Revenue
- Fintech: 5-20x revenue depending on margin and growth
- Consumer internet: 5-15x revenue

**Example:** A SaaS company with ₹10 crore ARR growing 100% YoY might be valued at:
- At 10x ARR: ₹100 crore
- At 15x ARR: ₹150 crore

The multiple is driven by growth rate, gross margin, NRR, and market size. Higher growth → higher multiple.

### Method 2: Comparable Transactions (Comps)

"What have similar companies in similar stages been valued at recently?"

If 5 comparable Indian SaaS companies raised Series A rounds at 8-12x ARR, your Series A should be in that range.

This is the most practically used method in VC. Investors know their market. They can quickly benchmark your company against others they've seen.

**Challenge:** Finding true comparables is hard. Every company is "different." Founders almost always believe their company deserves a premium to peers.

### Method 3: VC Method (Backward from Expected Exit)

This is how VCs actually think about early-stage investments.

1. Estimate the company's value at exit (5-7 years from now)
2. Work backward using expected return multiple
3. Adjust for dilution from future rounds
4. Arrive at a current valuation

**Example:**
- Expected exit in 6 years: ₹1,000 crore
- Required return multiple for a seed investor: 50x
- Required current value for 50x return: ₹1,000 crore / 50 = ₹20 crore post-money
- With ₹2 crore investment: pre-money valuation = ₹18 crore

This is why "you need to be a potential unicorn" for VCs to invest at attractive terms — the VC math requires a large exit to justify the risk.

### Method 4: DCF (Discounted Cash Flow)

The theoretically "correct" method for any business. Project future cash flows, discount them to present value using a discount rate that reflects risk.

**Why it doesn't work well for startups:**
- Cash flows are highly speculative 5+ years out
- The discount rate is arbitrary (15%? 25%? 50%?)
- Small changes in assumptions lead to huge valuation swings
- It gives a false sense of precision to what is inherently uncertain

Most VCs don't use DCF for early-stage investments. It's used more in late-stage or IPO contexts.

## 4. Why Startup Valuations Are Often Made Up

Here's the honest truth: seed-stage valuations are essentially negotiated outcomes, not calculated ones.

There is no formula that objectively produces "₹50 crore pre-money" for a company with 500 users and ₹5 lakh MRR. The valuation is set by:
- What the founders are willing to accept (minimum they'll take to give up X% equity)
- What investors are willing to pay (maximum they'll pay to get Y% of a company they believe in)
- The current market environment (what are comparable companies raising at?)
- How much competition there is for the deal

If a founder is very impressive, has unique insights, and multiple investors are competing to invest — the valuation will be high. If the founder is average, traction is modest, and there's only one interested investor — the valuation will be low.

This is normal and expected. Don't treat the valuation as an objective assessment of your company's worth.

## 5. The Unicorn — $1 Billion Valuation Explained

A "unicorn" is a startup valued at $1 billion or more (privately). The term was coined by venture investor Aileen Lee in 2013 because such companies were as rare as mythical unicorns.

As of 2024, India has 100+ unicorns, including:
- Flipkart (before Walmart acquisition)
- Ola, Oyo, Zomato, Swiggy, CRED, Meesho, Razorpay, Zepto, Groww, Paytm, Nykaa, Freshworks, and many more

**The unicorn hype problem:**

Unicorn status became a marketing achievement, not just a financial one. Companies rushed to achieve it. Investors with large funds (SoftBank, Tiger) were willing to invest at inflated valuations to earn the headline and deploy capital.

The result: many Indian unicorns were "valuation theater" — the $1B number reflected optimistic assumptions in a zero-interest-rate world, not actual business fundamentals.

When the market corrected in 2022-2023, many unicorns saw their valuations cut 50-80%. Some are no longer unicorns on paper.

## 6. Paper Valuation vs Real Valuation

This is one of the most important distinctions founders must understand.

**Paper valuation** is what your company is "worth" based on the last funding round's price per share. If you raised at a ₹1,000 crore valuation and you own 40%, your paper wealth is ₹400 crore.

**Real valuation** is what you'd actually receive if the company was sold today, after accounting for:

**Liquidation preferences:** Investors get paid first. If you raised ₹200 crore total with 2x participating preferred, investors are entitled to ₹400 crore before founders get anything. If the company sells for ₹500 crore, founders get ₹100 crore — not ₹200 crore.

**Preference stacks:** Each round of funding adds a layer. In a complex cap table with multiple rounds and different liquidation preferences, the "preference stack" can mean founders and employees receive very little unless the exit is large enough.

**The waterfall calculation:**

```
Exit Price: ₹500 crore

Step 1: Pay Series B (₹100 crore at 2x participating) = ₹200 crore
Remaining: ₹300 crore

Step 2: Pay Series A (₹50 crore at 1x participating) = ₹50 crore + share of remaining
Remaining: ₹250 crore

Step 3: Pay Seed (₹10 crore at 1x non-participating) = converts to common
Remaining: distribute pro-rata among all common and converted preferred

Step 4: Founders + employees (common stock) share what remains
```

The lesson: a ₹500 crore exit doesn't mean every shareholder gets 500/valuation × their ownership %. Read your cap table and preference stack carefully.

## 7. How the Same Company Can Be Worth Different Things

**Strategic value vs financial value:**

A startup developing a payment technology might be worth ₹500 crore to a financial investor based on its revenue multiple. But it might be worth ₹2,000 crore to HDFC Bank, which would gain competitive advantage by owning it. Strategic acquirers pay a premium for synergies.

**Different horizons:**

A VC who needs to return their fund in 7 years might value a company at ₹200 crore because they need an exit at a reasonable time. A family office with a 20-year horizon might value it at ₹400 crore because they're willing to wait for a larger outcome.

**Different risk tolerance:**

A fund that is looking to make a safe investment (diversified portfolio, conservative LPs) will value more conservatively than a high-conviction fund swinging for home runs.

## 8. Startup Valuation by Stage — Benchmarks for India

These are rough benchmarks as of 2024, subject to market conditions:

| Stage | ARR/GMV | Typical Pre-Money Valuation |
|-------|---------|---------------------------|
| Pre-seed (idea) | ₹0 | ₹5-15 crore |
| Seed (MVP, early users) | ₹0-5 lakh/month | ₹15-50 crore |
| Series A (PMF, growing) | ₹20-100 lakh/month ARR | ₹75-250 crore |
| Series B (scaling) | ₹100-500 lakh/month | ₹500-1,500 crore |
| Series C+ (growth stage) | ₹500 lakh+ /month | ₹2,000+ crore |

These vary significantly based on growth rate, market, and macro conditions.

## 9. The Indian Unicorn Correction (2021-2023)

India created 44 unicorns in 2021 alone — an unprecedented pace driven by:
- Global zero-interest rates (money looking for returns)
- COVID-driven digital adoption acceleration
- Competitive VC deployment pressure ("deploy before someone else does")

Then in 2022, interest rates rose globally. Capital became expensive. Revenue multiples compressed. Companies that had raised at 20-30x revenue were suddenly valued at 5-8x.

**What happened:**
- Byju's valuation dropped from $22B peak to ~$1B (and bankruptcy proceedings began)
- OYO cut valuation from $10B to $2.7B for IPO
- Paytm listed at ₹2,150 in 2021, fell to ₹430 by late 2022
- Multiple unicorns did down rounds
- "Decacorn" ambitions became "just be profitable" realities

**The lesson:** Startup valuations are deeply tied to macroeconomic conditions. In a zero-interest-rate world, everything is worth more because the discount rate is low. When rates rise, future cash flows are worth less today.

---

## Summary

- Traditional businesses are valued on earnings; startups are valued on future potential
- Common methods: revenue multiples, comparable transactions, VC method (backward from exit)
- Seed-stage valuations are largely negotiated, not calculated
- **Paper valuation ≠ real valuation** — liquidation preferences and preference stacks can dramatically reduce what founders and employees actually receive
- The unicorn craze of 2021 created inflated valuations; the 2022-2023 correction was a reality check
- Always model your exit scenario with the actual preference stack — not just "% of valuation"
