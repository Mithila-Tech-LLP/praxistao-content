# Chapter 07: Accounting for Non-Accountants

---

## Before We Start — Why a Founder Must Understand This

Here is a story that plays out dozens of times a year in India's startup world.

A young founder starts a business. They hire a chartered accountant to handle the books. The business grows. The founder focuses on product, sales, hiring. A year later, the investor asks to see the financials. The founder sits across the table and realizes, with a cold sweat, that they don't actually understand what they're looking at. The accountant prepared it. The lawyer filed it. But nobody explained it to them.

Then comes the question: "Your revenue is up 60% but your cash is down 40%. Why?" And the founder has no answer.

That company is in trouble — not because of the business, but because the founder lost the ability to understand their own business from a financial perspective.

Accounting is not just "finance stuff for accountants." It is the language your business speaks about itself. Every transaction, every decision, every strategy eventually shows up in the numbers. If you can't read those numbers, you are flying blind.

You don't need to become an accountant. You will hire one. But you need to understand what they're telling you well enough to ask smart questions, catch problems early, and make good decisions.

This chapter will give you that foundation.

## Section 1: Why Every Business Person Must Understand Accounting

Let's start with a simple truth: **accounting is just organized storytelling about money.**

Every time money moves in or out of a business, a story is told. Who paid? For what? When? To whom? How much? Accounting is the discipline of recording those stories consistently so that you can, at any point, answer the question: "How is the business actually doing?"

The problem is that "how the business is doing" is surprisingly hard to answer without accounting. Here's why.

Imagine you run a small software company. This month you signed three big contracts worth a total of Rs. 30 lakh. You are feeling great. But here's the reality:

- One client will pay in 90 days.
- One client will pay in 30 days but already owes you money from last month.
- One client paid upfront but the project runs over 6 months, so you can only count Rs. 50,000 this month.

Without proper accounting, you might think you made Rs. 30 lakh this month. You actually only made about Rs. 50,000 this month by accounting rules — and you might have a cash crisis coming in 60 days when payroll is due.

That gap — between what you think is happening and what is actually happening — kills businesses.

### What Accounting Actually Tells You

There are three fundamental things accounting tells you about any business:

1. **What the business owns and what it owes** (the balance sheet)
2. **Whether the business is making or losing money** (the income statement / P&L)
3. **Whether the business has enough cash to survive** (the cash flow statement)

We covered balance sheets and income statements in Chapter 04 and 05. This chapter goes deeper into the principles that govern how those statements are built — and how to spot when they've been manipulated.

### The Famous Case: Satyam Computers

In 2009, Satyam Computer Services — one of India's top IT companies at the time — collapsed in one of the biggest accounting frauds in Indian history. The chairman admitted to fabricating Rs. 7,136 crore of cash that didn't exist. The stock fell 78% in a single day.

How did this happen? The company reported excellent revenues and profits for years. Investors, bankers, and employees trusted the numbers. But the accounting was fraudulent — inflated revenues, fake bank balances, non-existent clients.

The lesson isn't that accounting is dangerous. The lesson is that understanding accounting well enough to ask hard questions protects you. Every investor, board member, and senior employee should have been asking: "Show me the actual bank statements. Let me verify the customer list." Most didn't, because they didn't know what to look for.

Understanding accounting is self-defense.

## Section 2: The Fundamental Accounting Equation

Everything in accounting flows from one equation. It is 500 years old and has never been wrong.

```
Assets = Liabilities + Equity
```

That's it. Memorize it. Tattoo it on your brain. This equation is the foundation of every balance sheet ever made.

Let's break down each term in plain English.

### Assets: Everything the Business Owns

An asset is anything valuable that the business controls and expects to get future benefit from.

| Type | Examples |
|------|---------|
| Cash | Money in bank accounts |
| Accounts Receivable | Money customers owe you |
| Inventory | Products waiting to be sold |
| Equipment | Laptops, machinery, vehicles |
| Buildings | Office space you own |
| Intellectual Property | Patents, software you built |
| Investments | Shares in other companies |

### Liabilities: Everything the Business Owes

A liability is a future obligation — money you will have to pay to someone else.

| Type | Examples |
|------|---------|
| Accounts Payable | Money you owe suppliers |
| Loans | Bank loans, investor debt |
| Salaries Payable | Wages earned but not yet paid |
| Tax Payable | Taxes accrued but not yet paid |
| Deferred Revenue | Money received for work not yet done |

### Equity: What's Left for the Owners

Equity is the residual. After you subtract everything the business owes (liabilities) from everything it owns (assets), what's left belongs to the owners.

```
Equity = Assets - Liabilities
```

If a company has Rs. 100 crore in assets and Rs. 60 crore in liabilities, the equity is Rs. 40 crore. That is theoretically what the owners would receive if the company liquidated everything today.

### Why Does This Equation Always Balance?

This is the question that trips people up. The answer is elegant.

Every transaction affects the equation in a way that keeps it balanced — because every financial event has two sides.

**Example:** Zepto takes a bank loan of Rs. 50 crore.
- **Assets go up** by Rs. 50 crore (cash in the bank)
- **Liabilities go up** by Rs. 50 crore (they now owe the bank)
- The equation stays balanced. ✓

**Example:** Zepto uses Rs. 10 crore of that cash to buy refrigerated warehouse equipment.
- **Cash (asset) goes down** by Rs. 10 crore
- **Equipment (asset) goes up** by Rs. 10 crore
- Net change in assets: zero. Equation stays balanced. ✓

**Example:** Zepto sells Rs. 2 crore of groceries for profit of Rs. 40 lakh.
- **Cash (asset) goes up** by Rs. 2 crore
- **Equity goes up** by Rs. 40 lakh (the profit)
- **But where does the other Rs. 1.6 crore come in?** Those are liabilities and costs that were expended — the cost of goods sold, which reduced an asset (inventory) or created a liability.

Everything balances. Every time. That's not magic — it is the structure of double-entry bookkeeping, which we'll cover next.

## Section 3: Double-Entry Bookkeeping — The 500-Year-Old System That Still Runs Everything

In 1494, an Italian friar named Luca Pacioli published a mathematics textbook. One chapter described a system for merchants in Venice to track their trades. That system is called double-entry bookkeeping.

Today, every company from a street vendor in Mumbai to Apple Inc. uses a version of this system. It has not been fundamentally changed in 530 years. That is remarkable.

### The Core Idea

Every financial transaction affects at least two accounts. There is always a "from" and a "to." You record both sides, and if your records are correct, everything balances.

The two sides are called:
- **Debit** (Dr) — the left side of the ledger
- **Credit** (Cr) — the right side of the ledger

Here is the confusing part that trips everyone up: "debit" does not mean money goes out, and "credit" does not mean money comes in. Those words have their everyday meanings in banking, but in accounting they just mean left and right.

What debit and credit actually mean depends on the account type:

| Account Type | Debit Does This | Credit Does This |
|---|---|---|
| Assets | Increases it | Decreases it |
| Liabilities | Decreases it | Increases it |
| Equity | Decreases it | Increases it |
| Revenue | Decreases it | Increases it |
| Expenses | Increases it | Decreases it |

You do not need to memorize this table right now. Accountants spend years getting fluent with it. What you need is the concept: every transaction has two entries, they must balance, and this is how we catch errors and fraud.

### A Real Example: Stripe Processes a Payment

Let's say Stripe processes a Rs. 10,000 payment from a customer on behalf of a merchant. How does this get recorded?

```
Debit:  Cash (Asset) .............. Rs. 10,000
Credit: Revenue (Revenue) ......... Rs. 9,700
Credit: Fees Payable (Liability) ..   Rs. 300
```

Three lines. All balance (10,000 = 9,700 + 300). Stripe earned Rs. 9,700 in revenue and owes Rs. 300 to the payment network (like Visa). The cash in their account went up by Rs. 10,000.

### Why This Matters for You

The beauty of double-entry bookkeeping is that it is self-checking. If your debits and credits don't balance, you know there's an error somewhere. Before computers, bookkeepers would spend days hunting for a single missing entry. Today, software handles the arithmetic — but the principle is the same.

More importantly: the double-entry system makes fraud harder. To inflate revenue, a fraudster must also inflate something on the other side — fake assets, fake receivables. When auditors know what to look for, these manipulations leave tracks. This is why accounting standards and audits matter so much.

## Section 4: Revenue Recognition — When Does a Sale Become Money?

This is one of the most important and most abused concepts in accounting.

The question sounds simple: when does a sale count as revenue? You made a sale, right? Count it.

But it's not that simple. And the gap between when cash arrives and when revenue is recognized has destroyed careers and companies.

### The Revenue Recognition Principle

A sale counts as revenue when two conditions are met:

1. You have **delivered** the product or service (the obligation is fulfilled)
2. You have **earned** the right to the payment (the customer has accepted it)

This sounds obvious. But look at how tricky it gets in practice.

**Example: Netflix**

You pay Netflix Rs. 649 for a month of streaming. Netflix gets your money on Day 1. But can they count all Rs. 649 as Day 1 revenue?

No. By accounting rules, they recognize Rs. 649/30 per day — roughly Rs. 21.63 per day — as they deliver the service. If they reported all your annual subscription on Day 1, their January revenue would look enormous and July revenue would look tiny. That would be misleading.

So when you pay for a year upfront, Netflix books the full Rs. 7,788 as **deferred revenue** (a liability!) and recognizes it Rs. 21.63/day for 365 days.

Wait — a liability? Yes. Because until they deliver the service, they technically owe you the streaming. If Netflix shut down tomorrow, they'd have to refund your unused subscription. So it's a debt they owe you.

**Example: A Real Estate Developer**

A developer in Mumbai sells 500 flats worth Rs. 2 crore each = Rs. 1,000 crore in bookings. Buyers pay 10% upfront. Can the developer book Rs. 1,000 crore revenue?

No. They've received Rs. 100 crore in cash, and the remaining Rs. 900 crore is a future promise. By accounting rules, revenue is only recognized when the flat is completed and handed over. Until then, the Rs. 100 crore received is — again — deferred revenue (liability).

This is why real estate developers can look "cash rich" while having massive liabilities to deliver.

**Example: Subscription SaaS Company**

A startup sells an annual software subscription for Rs. 12 lakh to a corporate client on January 1.

```
Revenue recognized: Rs. 1 lakh per month × 12 months
January 1 - Cash collected: Rs. 12 lakh
January 1 - Revenue recognized: Rs. 1 lakh
Deferred revenue (liability): Rs. 11 lakh
```

Each month, Rs. 1 lakh shifts from "deferred revenue" to "actual revenue" on the income statement.

### Why This Gets Abused

Companies under pressure to show growth sometimes "pull forward" revenue — recognizing it before it's properly earned. This is one of the most common forms of financial manipulation.

Signs to watch for:
- Revenues spike dramatically in Q4 every year (end-of-quarter stuffing)
- Deferred revenue keeps shrinking without explanation
- Revenue growth doesn't match actual customer growth
- Cash collected doesn't match revenues reported

## Section 5: Accrual vs Cash Accounting — The Important Difference

There are two fundamentally different ways to do accounting. Which one you use changes almost everything about how your financials look.

### Cash Accounting: Simple but Incomplete

In cash accounting, you record revenue when cash is received and expenses when cash is paid. That's it.

**Example — Cash Accounting:**
You deliver a project worth Rs. 5 lakh in March. The client pays in May.
- March P&L shows: Rs. 0 revenue
- May P&L shows: Rs. 5 lakh revenue

This is how most people instinctively think about money. And for a small freelancer or a vegetable vendor, it's perfectly fine.

### Accrual Accounting: More Complex, More Accurate

In accrual accounting, you record revenue when it is earned (work delivered) and expenses when they are incurred (obligation created), regardless of when cash moves.

**Example — Accrual Accounting:**
Same project worth Rs. 5 lakh delivered in March, paid in May.
- March P&L shows: Rs. 5 lakh revenue (recorded when earned)
- March Balance Sheet shows: Rs. 5 lakh in Accounts Receivable
- May Balance Sheet shows: Rs. 5 lakh cash (when paid), AR disappears

The income statement is more accurate — it shows the business earned money in March, when the value was created.

### Side-by-Side Comparison

| Scenario | Cash Accounting | Accrual Accounting |
|---|---|---|
| You make a sale but haven't been paid | Not recorded yet | Recorded as revenue + AR |
| You pay for 12 months of office rent in January | Rs. 12L expense in January | Rs. 1L expense each month |
| You receive advance payment for future work | Revenue in January | Deferred Revenue (liability) until delivered |
| Complexity | Low | High |
| Good for | Very small businesses | Any business over Rs. 50L turnover |
| Required by | — | All companies, GST-registered businesses |

### What This Means in Practice

India's Companies Act and Income Tax Act require businesses above certain thresholds to use accrual accounting. Most serious startups use it from day one.

The reason is simple: cash accounting can make a business look very healthy (because clients have paid upfront) or very sick (because clients are slow to pay) even when the underlying business is strong. Accrual accounting smooths this out and gives a more accurate picture.

A company can show strong profits on accrual accounting and still run out of cash — because the profits are sitting in "accounts receivable" (money owed to them) rather than in the bank. This is why you always need to look at cash flow alongside the P&L.

## Section 6: Depreciation — Why Assets Lose Value on Paper

Imagine Zomato buys a delivery motorbike for Rs. 1 lakh. This bike will be used for 5 years and then scrapped for Rs. 10,000.

Should Zomato count the full Rs. 1 lakh as an expense in Year 1? That doesn't seem right — the bike will be used for 5 years.

Should they count Rs. 0 as expense in Years 2-5? That doesn't seem right either — the bike is losing value every year through wear and tear.

The answer is depreciation: **the process of spreading the cost of an asset over its useful life.**

### The Straight-Line Method (Most Common)

```
Annual Depreciation = (Cost - Salvage Value) / Useful Life

= (Rs. 1,00,000 - Rs. 10,000) / 5 years
= Rs. 90,000 / 5
= Rs. 18,000 per year
```

So every year, Zomato records Rs. 18,000 as a depreciation expense. The bike shows up on the balance sheet, but its "book value" decreases each year:

| Year | Book Value |
|------|-----------|
| 0 (purchased) | Rs. 1,00,000 |
| 1 | Rs. 82,000 |
| 2 | Rs. 64,000 |
| 3 | Rs. 46,000 |
| 4 | Rs. 28,000 |
| 5 | Rs. 10,000 (salvage value) |

### Other Depreciation Methods

**Declining Balance Method:** Depreciate faster in early years (when the asset is most useful), slower in later years.

**Units of Production:** Depreciate based on how much the asset was actually used. A machine that makes widgets depreciates based on number of widgets made.

### Depreciation Is a Non-Cash Expense

Here is a very important concept: **depreciation reduces profit on paper but does not reduce cash.**

When Zomato records Rs. 18,000 depreciation expense, no money leaves the company. The bike was paid for in Year 1. The depreciation is just an accounting entry that spreads that original cash payment across the useful life of the asset.

This is why on the Cash Flow Statement, depreciation is added back to profit. The profit was reduced by depreciation, but no cash actually left.

**Why does this matter?** Companies with lots of fixed assets (factories, servers, aircraft) can show low net income but very strong cash flows. Airlines, for example, depreciate enormous aircraft fleets every year. Their "profit" looks small but their actual cash generation can be huge.

### Amortization: Depreciation for Intangible Assets

Software companies have a version of depreciation called amortization. It works the same way but applies to intangible assets — patents, software licenses, acquired customer bases, trademarks.

When Flipkart spent years building its logistics software, that R&D eventually gets capitalized (treated as an asset) and amortized over its useful life. This is why tech companies often have significant "amortization of intangibles" on their income statements.

## Section 7: Common Accounting Tricks and How to Spot Them

Accounting has rules. But rules have loopholes. And under pressure to show growth, management sometimes pushes those loopholes past their limits.

You don't need to be a forensic accountant. But knowing the common tricks protects you as an investor, employee, board member, or business partner.

### Trick 1: Revenue Stuffing (Channel Stuffing)

The company ships products to distributors at the end of a quarter that the distributors didn't order — or gives them extremely generous return policies. Revenue gets booked immediately. Next quarter, the unsold products come back.

**Red flag:** Inventory builds up at distributors (sometimes disclosed in notes), receivables spike at quarter-end, returns spike in the following quarter.

**Real example:** This was a core part of Luckin Coffee's fraud. They created fake sales through related parties and manufactured revenue that never actually occurred.

### Trick 2: Capitalizing Expenses

Instead of recording a cost as an expense (which hits profit immediately), a company treats it as an asset (which gets "depreciated" over years). This makes current profit look higher.

**Red flag:** Rapidly growing "capitalized costs" on the balance sheet. For a software company, if they suddenly start capitalizing huge amounts of "internal software development costs," investigate.

**Real example:** WorldCom, in one of history's biggest frauds, capitalized Rs. 38,000 crore of normal phone network maintenance costs as capital assets, artificially inflating profits.

### Trick 3: Cookie Jar Reserves

In good years, a company overprovisions for bad debts, losses, or liabilities — creating "cookie jar" reserves. In bad years, they release these reserves back into income to prop up profits.

**Red flag:** Provisions and reserves that don't correspond to actual business activity. Smooth earnings that are suspiciously consistent year after year.

### Trick 4: Revenue Before Delivery

Recognizing revenue too early — before the full service is delivered or the customer has accepted the product.

**Red flag:** Large deferred revenue that keeps shrinking. Revenue growth that doesn't match unit growth or customer count. Ask: "How many units did you sell?" vs "What is your revenue?" If those two numbers are moving in different directions, be curious.

### Trick 5: Related Party Transactions

Company A sells to Company B at inflated prices. Company A's founder privately owns Company B. Revenue looks strong; money is actually just moving in a circle.

**Red flag:** Large revenues from "related parties" — entities controlled by the same founders or management. These must be disclosed in financial statements, so read the notes carefully.

### How to Protect Yourself

| Question to Ask | What You're Checking |
|---|---|
| Do revenues match cash collections? | Early revenue recognition |
| Are receivables growing faster than revenue? | Fake or uncollectible sales |
| What's in the "other assets" line? | Capitalized costs |
| How are reserves changing? | Cookie jar manipulation |
| Who are the related parties? | Circular transactions |
| Does the auditor have a clean opinion? | Audit red flags |

## Section 8: Taxes and Business — What You Must Pay, What You Can Deduct

Taxes are unavoidable. But understanding them helps you pay what you legally owe — and not a rupee more.

### The Types of Business Taxes in India

| Tax | What It Is | Who Pays |
|---|---|---|
| Income Tax (Corporate Tax) | Tax on net profit | All companies |
| GST | Tax on goods/services sold | GST-registered businesses |
| TDS (Tax Deducted at Source) | Withholding tax on payments | Anyone making certain payments |
| Professional Tax | State-level tax on employment | Employers (varies by state) |
| Customs Duty | Tax on imported goods | Importers |

### Corporate Tax Rates in India (as of 2024-25)

- **Domestic companies (general):** 25% (for turnover up to Rs. 400 crore)
- **Large domestic companies:** 30%
- **New manufacturing companies:** 15% (concessional rate for new factories)
- **Minimum Alternate Tax (MAT):** 15% on book profit (ensures profitable companies pay some tax even with deductions)

These rates apply to **net profit** — after all legitimate deductions.

### What Can You Deduct?

The principle is: any expense that is **wholly and exclusively** incurred for the purpose of earning business income can be deducted.

**Commonly deductible expenses:**
- Salaries and employee benefits
- Rent for office space
- Utilities (electricity, internet)
- Marketing and advertising
- Professional fees (legal, accounting)
- Depreciation on assets
- Loan interest (for business loans)
- Research and development expenses
- Travel expenses for business purposes
- Cost of goods sold

**Not deductible:**
- Personal expenses mixed with business (founders often abuse this)
- Capital expenditure (it's capitalized, then depreciated — so it's indirectly deducted over time)
- Penalties and fines
- Certain gifts and entertainment above limits

### TDS — The Most Confusing Tax for New Founders

TDS (Tax Deducted at Source) means that when you pay certain parties — contractors, professionals, rent above Rs. 2.4 lakh/year — you must deduct a percentage at source and deposit it with the government.

**Example:** You pay a freelance designer Rs. 50,000. You must deduct 10% TDS = Rs. 5,000. You pay the designer Rs. 45,000 and pay Rs. 5,000 to the government on their behalf. The designer then claims that Rs. 5,000 as a tax credit when they file their own taxes.

Many early-stage founders mess this up badly. Missing TDS obligations creates penalty interest and notices from the Income Tax department. Get a CA to set up your TDS compliance from Day 1.

## Section 9: GST in India — How It Works for Businesses

GST (Goods and Services Tax) was launched in India in July 2017 to replace a complicated web of state and central taxes. It is now the primary indirect tax for businesses.

### The Core Idea: Tax Only the Value Added

GST is designed so that the final consumer pays the tax — not the businesses in the middle. Businesses collect GST from their customers but can offset it against the GST they've already paid their suppliers. This offset is called **Input Tax Credit (ITC)**.

Let's trace a packet of chips through the system at 18% GST:

| Stage | Party | Value | GST Collected | GST Paid | GST Deposited to Govt |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Potato farmer → Factory | Rs. 10 | Rs. 1.80 | Rs. 0 | Rs. 1.80 |
| 2 | Factory → Distributor | Rs. 30 | Rs. 5.40 | Rs. 1.80 (ITC) | Rs. 3.60 |
| 3 | Distributor → Retailer | Rs. 50 | Rs. 9.00 | Rs. 5.40 (ITC) | Rs. 3.60 |
| 4 | Retailer → Consumer | Rs. 80 | Rs. 14.40 | Rs. 9.00 (ITC) | Rs. 5.40 |
| **Total** | | | | | **Rs. 14.40** |

The consumer pays Rs. 14.40 in total GST (18% of Rs. 80). Each business in the chain only pays the GST on the value they added. The system is self-policing — businesses claim ITC only when their suppliers have reported and paid GST, so there's incentive at every stage to file correctly.

### GST Rates in India

| Category | Rate |
|---|---|
| Essential food items (fresh vegetables, milk, eggs) | 0% |
| Basic food (packaged food, edible oils) | 5% |
| Standard goods and services | 12-18% |
| Luxury goods, tobacco, vehicles | 28% |
| Additional cess on sin goods | 1-20% on top of 28% |

### GST Registration — When You Need It

You must register for GST if:
- Your annual turnover exceeds **Rs. 40 lakh** (goods) or **Rs. 20 lakh** (services) in most states
- You sell interstate (regardless of turnover)
- You sell through e-commerce platforms (regardless of turnover)

Once registered, you must file **GSTR-1** (outward supplies) and **GSTR-3B** (summary return) monthly or quarterly.

### The Startup Perspective

Many early-stage startups register for GST voluntarily — even below the threshold — because it lets them claim ITC on their purchases (office rent, software subscriptions, services). This can meaningfully reduce costs when you're spending a lot to set up.

The downside: it creates compliance obligations (monthly/quarterly filings). Missing GST filings creates a chain of problems — penalties, ITC loss for your customers, blocked banking relationships.

## Section 10: Working with Accountants — What to Expect, What to Ask

Most founders hire their first accountant too late and brief them too poorly. Here's how to do it right.

### What a CA Actually Does (vs. What You Think They Do)

There's a difference between a bookkeeper, an accountant, and a Chartered Accountant (CA):

| Role | What They Do | When You Need Them |
|---|---|---|
| **Bookkeeper** | Records daily transactions | From Day 1 |
| **Accountant** | Prepares financial statements, payroll, TDS | From first employee or first invoice |
| **CA (Chartered Accountant)** | Auditing, complex tax advice, regulatory compliance | Annual audit (mandatory above certain size), fundraising, major decisions |
| **CFO** | Strategic financial leadership | Series A onwards |

### How to Brief Your Accountant Well

Most founders hand over a box of receipts and say "handle it." This is a recipe for bad outcomes. Here's what you should provide and discuss:

1. **Your business model** — How exactly do you make money? What triggers a "sale" in your context?
2. **Revenue recognition policy** — When should a subscription be counted? A project milestone? A delivery?
3. **Expense categories** — Walk through each type of expense so they're coded correctly
4. **Equity structure** — Who owns what? Any stock options? Any convertible notes?
5. **Funding received** — Every tranche of investment, with documentation
6. **Bank accounts** — All accounts, with reconciliation at least monthly

### Questions to Ask Your Accountant Every Quarter

A good accountant is a business advisor, not just a compliance officer. Ask:

- "What does our runway look like if revenue stays flat?"
- "Are there any tax optimization opportunities we're missing?"
- "What will our tax liability be this year? Can we plan around it?"
- "Is there anything in our books that an investor or auditor would flag?"
- "Are we compliant on TDS and GST? Any notices pending?"

### Red Flags in an Accountant

- They file returns at the last minute every time
- They can't explain why a particular number looks the way it does
- They've never proactively flagged a compliance issue to you
- Financial statements take more than 3 weeks to prepare after month-end
- They don't keep you informed about changing tax rules

### The Founder's Minimum Financial Knowledge

You don't need to do accounting. But you must be able to:

1. Read and understand a P&L — know if you're profitable
2. Read a balance sheet — know what you own, what you owe
3. Read a cash flow statement — know how long your money lasts
4. Understand your GST and TDS obligations at a high level
5. Spot if your books "look wrong" and know what questions to ask

This chapter has given you most of that foundation. The rest comes from actually looking at your own financials every month — not once a year at audit time.

## Summary

Accounting is the language a business uses to describe itself in numbers. You don't need to speak it fluently — but you need to understand it well enough to know when something is wrong.

Here are the core ideas from this chapter:

- **The accounting equation (Assets = Liabilities + Equity)** always balances. Every transaction affects it in a balanced way — this is the foundation of double-entry bookkeeping.
- **Double-entry bookkeeping** records every transaction twice — a debit and a credit. This makes the system self-checking and makes fraud harder.
- **Revenue recognition** determines when a sale counts. Revenue is recognized when earned (service delivered), not when cash arrives. Deferred revenue is a liability.
- **Accrual accounting** records revenue when earned and expenses when incurred — not when cash moves. It's more accurate than cash accounting and required for serious businesses.
- **Depreciation** spreads the cost of a long-lived asset over its useful life. It reduces profit on paper but does not reduce cash.
- **Common accounting tricks** include channel stuffing, capitalizing expenses, cookie jar reserves, and related-party transactions. Knowing the patterns lets you spot the fraud.
- **GST** is a value-added tax — each business in a chain pays only on the value they added, not the full transaction amount. Input Tax Credit makes this work.
- **TDS** is a withholding tax — you deduct it from payments to contractors and professionals and deposit it with the government.
- **Working with accountants** requires you to actively brief them, ask hard questions quarterly, and maintain enough knowledge to spot when something is off.
