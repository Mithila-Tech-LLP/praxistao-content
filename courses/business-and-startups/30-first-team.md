# Chapter 30: The First Team — Who to Hire, When, and How

*Your first 10 employees will define your company's DNA for the next decade. Most founders don't take this seriously enough.*

---

## 1. Why Early Hires Matter So Much

In a large company, one bad hire is a manageable problem. In a 5-person startup, one wrong person can sink the entire operation.

The reasons:
- Early employees set the cultural norms that others follow
- Early employees are training each other — bad habits spread
- Early employees often have large equity stakes — a departure is expensive
- Early employees influence the company's capability profile for years

Sam Altman (CEO of OpenAI, formerly president of Y Combinator) says: "The most important thing you do as a CEO is hire the right people. The second most important is firing the wrong ones quickly."

Most founders focus on the first sentence and ignore the second.

---

## 2. The Golden Rule: Hire Slow, Fire Fast

**Hire slow:** Interview extensively. Reference-check obsessively. Run a trial project. See them under pressure. Sleep on it. The cost of a wrong hire is 3-6x their annual salary in lost time, disruption, and the cost of finding a replacement.

**Fire fast:** If someone is clearly wrong — wrong skills, wrong values, wrong attitude — the kindest thing you can do for them and the company is to address it quickly. The longer a bad fit stays, the more damage accumulates. Other employees notice bad performance that goes unaddressed and start to question the standards.

The reason founders don't fire fast: it feels mean. It's uncomfortable. You hope they'll improve. The reality: they rarely improve without clear feedback, and by the time you've decided someone needs to go, you usually should have made the decision 3 months earlier.

---

## 3. Who to Hire First — The Minimum Viable Team

### For Tech Startups

Before product-market fit, your minimum viable team is:
- **1-2 engineers** who can build the core product fast
- **1 founder who can sell** (usually the CEO)

You don't need a marketing person, an HR person, a finance person, or a business development person. These can wait.

After initial PMF signals:
- 1-2 more engineers to accelerate product
- 1 dedicated customer success person (to learn from users in depth)
- Potentially 1 sales person (to test scalability of founder-led sales)

### For Non-Tech Startups

If your startup is operations-heavy (quick commerce, logistics, services marketplace):
- An operations lead who has run similar logistics
- A ground-level team for the physical service
- Technical help (can be outsourced initially)

### The Generalist vs Specialist Question

**Before PMF:** Hire generalists. People who can wear 5 hats. The job description will change completely in 6 months. You need adaptability above all.

**After PMF:** Hire specialists. Once you know what the job actually is, hire the best person for that specific job.

The mistake: hiring specialists before you know what you're building. You end up with highly paid people doing work that will be obsoleted by the next pivot.

## 4. How to Hire Without Money — Using Equity and Vision

Early-stage startups often can't pay market salaries. The tools:

**ESOPs (Employee Stock Options):** Grant employees the right to buy shares at today's price in the future. If the company grows, those shares become worth much more. (See Section 5 for details.)

**Below-market salary + equity:** A common structure. "I can pay you ₹8 lakh a year when market is ₹15 lakh, but I'll give you 0.5% of the company with 4-year vesting." This makes sense for someone who believes in the company's potential.

**Vision and mission:** The best early employees join for the opportunity to build something meaningful, to learn more than they would anywhere else, and to be part of something. You're selling all of this, not just a paycheck.

**Domain experience:** "You've spent 10 years in fintech. Nobody knows this problem better than you. This is your chance to actually fix it." For domain experts who are frustrated with large companies, this is genuinely appealing.

## 5. ESOPs — Employee Stock Option Plans Explained

ESOPs are one of the most important and most misunderstood tools in the startup world. Many Indian employees have been burned by ESOPs they didn't understand.

### How ESOPs Work

1. The company grants you the **right to buy X shares at a fixed price** (the "strike price")
2. The strike price is usually the fair market value at the time of the grant
3. Options vest over time (typically 4 years with 1-year cliff)
4. When you **exercise** the option, you pay the strike price and become a shareholder
5. You realize value when the company has a liquidity event (acquisition or IPO)

### Example With Numbers

You join Startup X in 2022. You're granted 10,000 options at a strike price of ₹10/share.

In 2026, Startup X is acquired for ₹100/share.

Your options: you pay ₹10 × 10,000 = ₹1 lakh to exercise. Your shares are worth ₹100 × 10,000 = ₹10 lakh. Net gain: ₹9 lakh.

If the strike price was ₹10 and the acquisition price was ₹8, your options are "underwater" — worthless.

### Why Indian Employees Often Lose

1. **They don't exercise in time** — options typically expire 90 days after leaving the company. Most employees don't have ₹1-5 lakh to exercise, so they forfeit.

2. **They don't understand liquidation preferences** — in a down exit, preferred shareholders (VCs) get paid first. If the VCs have a 2x liquidation preference and the company sells for less than 2x invested capital, common shareholders (employees) get nothing.

3. **They join late-stage companies with high strike prices** — if you join a unicorn at a ₹100/share strike price, the company needs to exit at much higher for your options to be valuable.

4. **They leave before vesting** — common, and results in forfeiting unvested options.

### What to Ask When Negotiating ESOPs

- What is the current strike price?
- What is the company's most recent valuation (409A or last funding round)?
- What percentage of the fully diluted company do my options represent?
- What is the liquidation preference of existing investors?
- What is the exercise window after leaving?

## 6. Where to Find Early Employees

**Warm references (best):** Your own network, your existing employees' networks. "Who's the best frontend engineer you've ever worked with?" Referral hires are faster to join, higher quality on average, and less likely to leave early.

**LinkedIn:** Direct outreach. Effective if the company has a compelling story. Your message needs to be personal (not copy-paste) and specific about why you're reaching out to them.

**College networks:** IIT, NIT, BITS, Delhi University, and other strong programs. For fresh graduates willing to work for lower salary + equity, this is underrated. They often have more drive and less baggage.

**Startup communities:** People who participate in startup communities (Slack groups, WhatsApp communities, events like Headstart, TechSparks) are self-selecting for startup interest.

**AngelList/Wellfound:** The dedicated platform for startup hiring. Less noise than LinkedIn.

**Twitter/X:** Many skilled engineers and designers have public profiles. Direct, thoughtful messages work.

## 7. The Hiring Process for Startups — Fast and Judgment-Heavy

Unlike large companies with 8-round interview loops, startup hiring should be:

**Fast:** First contact to offer in 1-2 weeks maximum. The best candidates have options. If you take 6 weeks to make a decision, you'll lose them.

**Practical:** Include a real work sample. Ask them to do a small task related to their actual job. A designer shows you a portfolio. An engineer does a 2-3 hour take-home project. A sales person does a mock pitch.

**Judgment-heavy:** Trust your gut on cultural fit. Technical skills can be learned; character can't.

**Reference-checked seriously:** Call 3 references who have actually managed this person. Ask: "What were they like when things were going badly? Were they coachable? Would you hire them again?" The answers reveal things that interviews don't.

A good hiring process for a startup:

1. Screening call (30 min): Cultural fit, basics, motivation
2. Work sample (take-home or live): Actual skills
3. Culture/values interview (60 min): With a second founder or senior team member
4. Reference calls (2-3)
5. Offer

---

## 8. Cultural Fit vs. Skill — Which Matters More?

Both matter. But the tradeoff is:

**High skill, bad culture fit:** Damages the team. One toxic star player can destroy morale, cause others to leave, and poison the culture. Netflix's culture deck says explicitly: "adequate performance gets a generous severance."

**Good culture fit, improvable skill:** Can be managed. If someone is smart, humble, hardworking, and aligned on values — you can teach almost everything else.

The exception: for highly specialized technical roles (rare AI researchers, specific domain experts), you sometimes need to take on more cultural risk because the skill is truly irreplaceable.

---

## 9. Building a Team of A-Players

Steve Jobs famously said: "A players hire A players. B players hire C players." His logic: insecure managers hire people who won't threaten them. Confident, high-performing leaders hire people who might be better than them in specific domains.

This compounds. A team of A players challenges each other, raises the bar, and attracts more A players. A team of B players creates work that requires management and oversight — and chases away A players who don't want to babysit.

How to attract A players:
- Build something genuinely interesting and ambitious
- Have a clear, compelling mission
- Create an environment where they have ownership and can see the impact of their work
- Pay competitively (or compensate with strong equity)
- Give them room to grow — A players leave when they're not learning

---

## Summary

- Your first 10 employees define your company's DNA — invest enormous care here
- Hire slow (interview rigorously, reference-check), fire fast (don't let bad fits linger)
- Before PMF: hire generalists; after PMF: hire specialists
- Use ESOPs to attract talent you can't pay market salaries; ensure employees understand what they're signing
- Find candidates through warm references first, then LinkedIn, college networks, and communities
- Cultural fit matters at least as much as skill — one toxic hire can destroy a team
