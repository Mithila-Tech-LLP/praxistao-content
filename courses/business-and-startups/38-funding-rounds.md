# Chapter 38: Funding Rounds — Seed, Series A, B, C Explained

*Each funding round is not just money — it's a signal, a milestone, and a new set of expectations.*

---

## 1. Why Startups Raise in Rounds

A startup doesn't raise all the money it will ever need upfront. It raises in stages, for three reasons:

**Risk reduction for investors:** Investing ₹1 crore at the seed stage is less risky than investing ₹100 crore. Each round is triggered by demonstrated progress — the company has earned the right to more capital by de-risking the venture.

**Valuation accretion for founders:** At seed stage, your company is worth ₹20 crore. After proving the model, it might be worth ₹200 crore. You raise your next round at the higher valuation, diluting yourself less per rupee raised.

**Milestone-based capital deployment:** Companies raise for specific milestones: "We'll use this money to reach ₹5 crore ARR, which will support a Series A at a higher valuation." This disciplines spending.

---

## 2. Pre-Seed — The Very Beginning

**What it is:** The earliest possible round. Often just the founders' own savings or money from friends and family.

**Typical size:** ₹10 lakh to ₹1 crore (sometimes $50K-$250K from angels)

**What the startup has:** An idea, maybe an MVP in progress, no customers yet

**What investors are buying:** Potential. Entirely a bet on the team.

**Common instruments:** Personal savings, friends & family, sometimes an angel who writes a very small SAFE

**Indian context:** Many Indian founders bootstrap entirely through this phase or use personal savings + small family support. The "friends and family round" is less common in India than the US because there's less cultural familiarity with the practice.

---

## 3. Seed Round — Getting to Proof

**What it is:** The first formal investment. The goal: build the MVP and get enough users or revenue to prove the concept.

**Typical size in India:** ₹50 lakh to ₹5 crore ($100K to $1M)

**What the startup has:**
- MVP built or nearly built
- Some early users (maybe 100-1,000)
- Clear hypothesis about market and business model
- Full founding team in place

**What investors are buying:** Validation potential. Can this team find product-market fit?

**Common investors:**
- Angel investors
- Micro-VCs (Blume, Kae Capital, 100X.VC, Better Capital in India)
- Y Combinator (globally)
- Family offices

**Common instruments:** SAFE, convertible note, or priced round at ₹15-50 crore pre-money valuation

**What the money is used for:**
- Completing the product
- Hiring 2-5 key people
- Getting to 100-500 paying customers or meaningful usage

**Milestone to unlock Series A:**
- Clear product-market fit signals
- Growing MRR (₹10-50 lakh/month for SaaS) or user base
- One acquisition channel that appears scalable

---

## 4. Series A — Finding and Proving Product-Market Fit

**What it is:** The first major institutional round. You've proven something works — now you need to figure out if it can scale.

**Typical size in India:** ₹5-20 crore ($1-4M); top-tier Series A in competitive sectors can be $5-10M+

**What the startup has:**
- Product-market fit demonstrated (retention, NPS, organic growth)
- Revenue growing month-over-month
- At least one working acquisition channel
- Team of 10-30 people

**What investors are buying:** Confidence that this can become a large business. They need to believe in the path to ₹100-500 crore in revenue.

**Common investors:**
- Dedicated early-stage VCs (Accel, Matrix, Kalaari, Stellaris)
- Later-entry into the same company by seed investors

**Valuation:** ₹75-300 crore pre-money, depending on traction and market

**Board dynamics:** Series A typically brings 1 investor board seat + 2 founder seats. Board governance becomes real.

**The Series A crunch:** In India (as globally), the gap between seed and Series A is one of the hardest to cross. Many companies raise seed rounds but can't raise a Series A because they haven't found PMF.

---

## 5. Series B — Scaling What Works

**What it is:** You've proven the model. Now put significant fuel on the fire.

**Typical size:** ₹30-150 crore ($10-30M)

**What the startup has:**
- Proven business model (positive unit economics or a clear path)
- Revenue at ₹20-100 crore ARR
- Team of 50-200 people
- Multiple working acquisition channels
- Early market leadership in the target segment

**What investors are buying:** Execution capability at scale. The bet now is on the team's ability to grow 5-10x from here.

**Common investors:**
- Top-tier VCs (Peak XV, Accel, Elevation)
- Crossover funds (who invest in both private and public companies)

**What the money is used for:**
- Aggressive hiring (product, sales, engineering, marketing)
- Geographic expansion
- Marketing to accelerate growth
- Infrastructure to handle scale

---

## 6. Series C, D, and Beyond — Growth at Scale

**What it is:** At this point, the company is no longer a startup in the traditional sense. It's a high-growth company executing a proven model.

**Typical size:** ₹150-1,000+ crore ($30M-$200M+)

**Who invests:** Growth equity funds (Tiger Global, Softbank, General Atlantic), late-stage VCs, sovereign wealth funds

**What it's used for:**
- International expansion
- M&A (acquiring smaller companies)
- Building new product lines
- IPO preparation
- Secondary sales (existing shareholders selling some stakes)

**The late-stage metrics that matter:**
- Revenue growth rate (>50% YoY is considered strong at scale)
- Path to profitability or actual profitability
- Market share in target segments
- NRR > 100% (for SaaS)

## 7. The India Funding Landscape — 2010-2024

### The First Wave (2010-2015)
Flipkart, Snapdeal, and Ola raised hundreds of millions, drawing global attention to Indian internet. Investors poured money into e-commerce and ride-sharing.

### The Scaling Wave (2016-2019)
Paytm, Byju's, OYO, Zomato, Swiggy, CRED, Razorpay, Zepto — a wave of consumer tech and fintech unicorns emerged. SoftBank's Vision Fund made massive bets. Indian startup valuations rose dramatically.

### The Euphoria Peak (2020-2021)
COVID accelerated digital adoption. Combined with global zero-interest rates, VC and growth funds had massive capital to deploy. Valuations became disconnected from fundamentals. Tiger Global invested in hundreds of Indian startups. The number of Indian unicorns doubled in 18 months.

### The Funding Winter (2022-2023)
Global interest rates rose sharply. Investors became cautious. VCs shifted from "grow at all costs" to "prove you can be profitable." Indian startups saw:
- Layoffs at previously high-flying companies (Byju's, OYO, Unacademy, Meesho)
- Down rounds (raising at lower valuations than previous rounds)
- Extended runways without raising (companies cut costs to survive)
- Several high-profile failures (GoMechanic, Trell, ZestMoney)

### The Recovery (2024-)
The market stabilized. Companies that survived the winter emerged leaner and more focused. Investors returned to backing companies with strong unit economics and clear paths to profitability. The bar is higher — "show me the money" replaced "show me the growth."

## 8. Bridge Rounds and Down Rounds

### Bridge Round

When a company doesn't have the metrics to raise a full round, but has enough traction that existing investors want to give it more time, they may do a "bridge round" — a smaller raise, usually from existing investors, to give the company 6-12 more months.

Instrument: typically a convertible note, which converts at the next priced round.

A bridge round is neither good nor bad — it's a tool. Companies that needed bridges include many that went on to be very successful.

### Down Round

A down round is when a company raises at a valuation *lower* than its previous round.

This happens when:
- The company grew more slowly than expected
- The market moved against the company
- Global market conditions deteriorated (as in 2022)
- The company burned too much cash

**Why down rounds are painful:**
- Existing investors are diluted (their stake is worth less than before)
- Founder morale suffers
- Employees with ESOPs see their strike prices become unfavorable
- Press coverage is negative, which affects recruiting

**Anti-dilution provisions** in investor term sheets protect investors in down rounds by giving them more shares to compensate. This further dilutes founders.

Notable Indian down rounds: Several companies including Byju's, Ola, OYO took valuation cuts in 2022-2023.

---

## 9. Secondary Sales — When Founders and Early Employees Cash Out

In the VC model, founders and early employees hold illiquid equity — it's worth something on paper but can't be converted to cash until an exit (IPO or acquisition).

Secondary sales allow some liquidity before the full exit:
- A founder sells 5% of their shares to new investors
- Early employees sell vested shares to new investors entering the cap table
- An angel investor exits partially in a later round

Common in rounds of Series B and later when the company has demonstrated value. The Flipkart founders took secondary sales long before the Walmart acquisition.

**Why it matters:** Founders who have some liquidity are often *better* long-term operators — the financial pressure of needing an exit is reduced. They can make better long-term decisions without personal financial anxiety driving every choice.

## 10. Case Study: Meesho's Funding Journey

**2015:** Vidit Aatrey and Sanjeev Barnwal founded Meesho, focused on helping small business owners sell on WhatsApp.

**2017:** Raised seed funding of ~$2M from SAIF Partners (now Elevation).

**2018:** Raised $11.5M Series A from Sequoia India. Growing fast, social commerce model proving out.

**2019:** $50M Series B from Naspers/Prosus. Pivoted to focus on resellers, particularly women in tier-2 cities.

**2020:** $125M Series C from Softbank. COVID accelerated the model.

**2021:** $570M Series F at $4.9B valuation — unicorn. SoftBank, ProSus, Facebook (Meta) among investors.

**2022-2023:** The funding winter. Meesho cut 15% of staff, improved unit economics dramatically.

**2024:** Heading toward profitability, reported positive EBITDA months.

The journey: ₹12 crore seed to $4.9B valuation in 6 years. Multiple rounds, strategic pivots at each stage, and a painful recalibration in the funding winter. A realistic picture of how funding actually plays out.

---

## Summary

- Startups raise in rounds because each round de-risks for investors while preserving valuation for founders
- **Pre-Seed:** Personal/friends capital, concept stage
- **Seed:** First institutional money, building MVP and early traction
- **Series A:** Proving PMF and the path to scale (the hardest raise)
- **Series B+:** Scaling a proven model with fuel
- The Indian funding landscape went from growth at all costs (2019-2021) to profitability and sustainability (2022+)
- Bridge rounds buy time; down rounds signal trouble; secondary sales provide founder liquidity
