## 1. Why Competition Analysis Matters Before Starting Any Business

Imagine you have a great idea. You want to start a restaurant. You have a fantastic recipe, you have some savings, and you are excited. You find a location, sign a lease, hire staff, and open your doors.

Six months later, you are struggling. Three other restaurants opened nearby. The food supplier raised prices. A new food delivery app is taking 30% of your revenue as commission. A customer who used to spend ₹500 is now ordering a ₹150 biryani from Zomato instead of coming in.

None of this happened because your food was bad. Your food was great. It happened because you did not understand the competitive forces already operating in your industry before you entered it.

This is the difference between a naive entrepreneur and a thoughtful one. The naive one asks: "Is my idea good?" The thoughtful one asks: "Is this industry a good place to compete in?"

Some industries are structurally wonderful. If you enter them, even a mediocre business can survive and grow. Other industries are structurally terrible — even brilliant companies with great products struggle to make money. Understanding which kind of industry you are entering before you invest your time and money is one of the most important skills in business.

Porter's Five Forces is the tool that teaches you how to answer that question.

---

## 2. Michael Porter and the Five Forces Framework

Michael Porter is a professor at Harvard Business School. In 1979, he published a paper that changed how people think about business strategy. The core insight was simple but powerful:

**The profitability of an industry is not random. It is determined by five structural forces that shape every industry.**

Before Porter, most business people thought about competition the way most people still do — as a fight between existing rivals. "Who is our biggest competitor? How do we beat them?" Porter said: that is too narrow. Competition comes from multiple directions, not just from your direct rivals.

Here is the full framework:

```
                        THREAT OF NEW ENTRANTS
                                 ↓
BARGAINING POWER      COMPETITIVE       BARGAINING POWER
OF SUPPLIERS    →      RIVALRY      ←   OF BUYERS
                                 ↑
                    THREAT OF SUBSTITUTES
```

Think of your business as sitting in the center. Each of the five forces is pushing against you, trying to squeeze your profits. The stronger these forces, the harder it is to make money. The weaker they are, the easier.

The goal of a great business strategy is not just to survive these forces — it is to position yourself where the forces are weak, or to build defenses so strong that the forces cannot harm you.

Let us now go through each force in detail.

## 3. Force 1: Threat of New Entrants

### What It Means

Every time your business makes money, someone notices. And if that someone thinks they can make money too, they might try to enter your market. The "threat of new entrants" asks: **how easy is it for a new competitor to enter your industry and take your customers?**

If it is easy, you have a problem. New entrants keep arriving, prices get pushed down, and everyone's profits shrink. If it is hard, you are protected. You can charge more, grow steadily, and sleep well at night.

The things that make it hard for new competitors to enter are called **barriers to entry**. Understanding barriers to entry is one of the most important ideas in all of business strategy.

### The Main Barriers to Entry

**1. Capital requirements** — How much money do you need to start?

Starting a WhatsApp rival requires billions of dollars in server infrastructure, security, and engineering talent. Starting a food stall requires maybe ₹20,000. The food stall has almost no capital barrier. The messaging app has an enormous one.

**2. Regulatory and legal barriers** — Does the government make entry hard?

Starting a bank in India requires a banking licence from the Reserve Bank of India. The RBI gives out very few. As of 2024, only a handful of full-service banks have been licenced in decades. This is an enormous barrier. Compare this to starting a food delivery company — there is no licence required, just a GST registration. That is why there were dozens of food delivery apps in India in 2015-2016, and why banking is still dominated by a small number of players.

**3. Brand and customer loyalty** — Do customers trust newcomers?

Apple has spent 40 years building a brand that people trust deeply. If a new company called "PearPhone" launched tomorrow with technically better hardware, most Apple users would not switch. They trust Apple. This brand loyalty is a massive barrier to entry.

**4. Network effects** — Does the product get better as more people use it?

WhatsApp is useful because everyone you know is already on it. If a new messaging app launched today — even a better one — you would not switch, because none of your contacts are there. This is a network effect. The existing player benefits from it. The new entrant faces a huge disadvantage. Instagram, LinkedIn, and UPI all benefit from network effects.

**5. Economies of scale** — Do big players have a cost advantage?

Amazon can negotiate deeply discounted rates with suppliers, logistics companies, and cloud providers because of their scale. A new e-commerce startup cannot get those rates. This means Amazon can profitably sell at prices that would bankrupt a newcomer. Scale creates a cost moat that protects big players.

**6. Switching costs** — How painful is it for customers to leave?

If you use Tally for accounting in your business, switching to a new accounting software means re-entering years of data, retraining your staff, and risking errors during the transition. This pain is a switching cost. It protects Tally from new entrants, even if a competitor builds a better product.

### Real Examples: Banking vs. Food Delivery

| Industry | Capital Needed | Regulations | Brand | Network Effects | Barriers Overall |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Banking | Very high | Extremely strict | Strong | Moderate | Very high |
| Food delivery | Moderate | Low | Moderate | High | Moderate |
| Restaurant | Low | Moderate | Low | None | Low |
| Cloud infrastructure | Extremely high | Moderate | Strong | Moderate | Very high |
| Tutoring | Near zero | None | Low | None | Very low |

Notice that tutoring has almost no barriers. That is why there are thousands of tutors competing for students and most of them earn modestly. Banking has enormous barriers — which is why the top three banks in India (SBI, HDFC, ICICI) have been dominant for decades and remain extremely profitable.

### What This Means in Practice

When you are analyzing an industry you want to enter, ask yourself: "What would stop 1000 other people from entering this market tomorrow if they saw me making money?" If the answer is "nothing much," you are in a low-barrier industry and will likely face intense competition. If the answer involves expensive licences, massive capital, or years of trust-building, you have some natural protection.

## 4. Force 2: Bargaining Power of Suppliers

### What It Means

Every business buys inputs from someone. A restaurant buys vegetables, oil, and spices. A smartphone company buys chips, screens, and memory. A movie streaming service licenses content from studios. The people who sell you these inputs are your **suppliers**.

The question is: **who has more power in that relationship — you or them?**

If your suppliers have high bargaining power, they can raise prices, reduce quality, or refuse to sell to you — and you have no choice but to accept it. This squeezes your profits. If your bargaining power is high (and their power is low), you can demand lower prices, better terms, and higher quality. This expands your profits.

### What Gives Suppliers Power?

- **There are few suppliers** — If only one company makes the component you need, they have all the power.
- **Switching suppliers is expensive or difficult** — If changing your supplier means redesigning your product, you are stuck.
- **The supplier's product is critical** — If their ingredient makes or breaks your product, they have leverage.
- **The supplier could cut you out and sell directly** — If your supplier could easily become your competitor, they have power over you.

### Apple vs. Samsung

This is one of the most fascinating supplier relationships in business history.

For years, Samsung manufactured the screens and chips that Apple used in iPhones. Samsung was both Apple's biggest supplier AND Apple's biggest competitor (Samsung makes Galaxy phones).

Apple was terrified of this dependency. Samsung could — in theory — delay delivering components, charge Apple more, or learn from the technology. So Apple spent billions of dollars over a decade to reduce this dependency. They developed their own chips (the A-series, now M-series), diversified their screen suppliers to include LG and BOE, and moved some manufacturing to Taiwan and India.

The lesson: Apple had enormous bargaining power over most of its suppliers because of its scale (it buys hundreds of millions of units), but it was deliberately vulnerable with Samsung. The solution was to reduce dependency — not just negotiate harder.

### Small Farmers vs. Cargill

Now look at the other extreme.

A small wheat farmer in Madhya Pradesh has almost no bargaining power against a company like Cargill or ITC (which buys their grain). Why?

- There are thousands of farmers selling wheat — each individual farmer is replaceable.
- The buyer (Cargill, ITC) can easily find other suppliers.
- The farmer has no alternative buyers in many regions — they cannot easily ship their grain to a different buyer.
- The farmer's product (undifferentiated wheat) is a commodity — it is the same as everyone else's wheat.

This power imbalance is why farmer incomes in India have been chronically low for decades, despite enormous effort. It is a structural problem, not just a policy problem. Porter's framework explains it clearly.

### What This Means in Practice

When entering a business, map out your key suppliers. For each one, ask: "What happens if they raise prices by 30%? Can I find an alternative? How long would it take?" If the answer is "we would be in serious trouble," you have a supplier power problem that needs a strategy — either building alternative supply chains, negotiating long-term contracts, or vertically integrating (buying the supplier).

## 5. Force 3: Bargaining Power of Buyers

### What It Means

This force is the mirror image of supplier power. Instead of asking about the people who sell to you, it asks about the people who buy from you — your customers.

**How much power do your customers have to demand lower prices, better quality, or more features?**

If your customers have high bargaining power, they will push your prices down and increase your costs. If they have low bargaining power, you can charge more and set your own terms.

### What Gives Buyers Power?

- **Buyers are large and buy in huge volumes** — A customer who represents 30% of your revenue can walk into a negotiation and demand massive discounts. You need them more than they need you.
- **Products are standardized (commodities)** — If what you sell is the same as what everyone else sells, buyers can easily switch, giving them enormous leverage.
- **Switching costs are low** — If a buyer can move to your competitor in a day, they will threaten to do so every time they want a lower price.
- **Buyers have full information** — The internet has dramatically increased buyer power. You can compare prices across 10 suppliers in 30 seconds on IndiaMART. This transparency squeezes supplier margins.
- **The product is a small part of the buyer's costs** — If a buyer is spending ₹1 crore on machinery and ₹500 on bolts, they will negotiate hard on the machinery and barely care about the bolt price.

### B2B vs. B2C: A Critical Distinction

**B2B (Business to Business):** You sell to other businesses. Buyer power is often very high. A few large buyers represent most of your revenue. They negotiate hard and have procurement teams whose entire job is to extract discounts from you.

**B2C (Business to Consumer):** You sell to millions of individual consumers. Each individual customer has almost no power. They cannot meaningfully negotiate your prices down. But collectively, if you charge too much, they will switch — so the *market* has power even if *individual* buyers do not.

This is why B2C businesses with brand loyalty (like Apple, Zara, or Amul) can charge premium prices. No individual customer has the power to call Tim Cook and demand a discount.

### How Walmart Crushed Its Suppliers

Walmart is possibly the most famous example of extreme buyer power in history.

Walmart's business model is built entirely on being the lowest-cost retailer. To do that, they extract every possible rupee (or dollar) from their suppliers. Here is how it worked:

1. Walmart would approach a supplier — say, a detergent company — and ask them to supply product.
2. Once the supplier was dependent on Walmart for 30-40% of their revenue, Walmart would start demanding lower prices.
3. The supplier faced a choice: accept the lower price, or lose 30-40% of your business overnight.
4. Over time, suppliers had to move factories to cheaper locations, cut staff, and reduce quality just to meet Walmart's price demands.
5. Some suppliers were eventually driven out of business or forced to manufacture in China and Bangladesh to cut costs.

A book called *The Walmart Effect* by Charles Fishman documented dozens of American manufacturers who were destroyed by this dynamic. It was not malice — it was pure buyer power, systematically applied.

The lesson for suppliers: **never let a single buyer represent more than 15-20% of your revenue.** Diversify your customer base before they have leverage over you.

### What This Means in Practice

When building a business, think carefully about who your customers are and whether they have structural power over you. If you are selling to the Indian government (which buys millions of units of software or equipment), know that they will negotiate hard and payment will be slow. If you are selling to individual consumers on a subscription, each customer's departure costs you little — but you need retention strategies to keep them collectively.

## 6. Force 4: Threat of Substitute Products

### What It Means

A substitute is not a direct competitor. It is a different product that satisfies the same underlying need.

**The question is: can your customers meet the same need in a completely different way, without buying from you or your competitors?**

If the answer is yes, your pricing power is limited. If customers can satisfy their need in five different ways, you will struggle to raise prices. If your product is unique and there is no substitute, you can charge far more.

### The Key Insight: You Are Selling a Job to Be Done

Customers do not buy products. They hire products to do a job. When you understand what job your product is actually doing, you can identify the real substitutes — which are often surprising.

Netflix does not compete just with Disney+ and Amazon Prime. It competes with:
- YouTube (free video entertainment)
- Books (spending an evening reading instead)
- Gaming (PlayStation, mobile games)
- Going out (a movie theater, a restaurant, a concert)
- Social media (spending the evening scrolling instead)

The "job" Netflix is doing is: "keep me entertained in the evenings." All of those other things can do that job too. This is the real competitive landscape.

### Uber vs. Cars vs. Trains vs. Bikes

Let us map the substitutes for Uber's core service: getting from point A to point B in a city.

| Option | Cost | Speed | Convenience | Flexibility |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Uber / Ola | Medium | Fast | Very high | Very high |
| Own car | High upfront, low per trip | Fast | High | Complete |
| Metro / local train | Very low | Fast (on route) | Moderate | Low (fixed routes) |
| Bus | Very low | Slow | Low | Low |
| Auto-rickshaw | Low | Moderate | Moderate | High |
| Bicycle | Near zero | Slow | Low | High |
| Walking | Zero | Very slow | Very low | Complete |

For a 2 km trip in Bengaluru at 9 PM, someone might take an auto, walk, or call an Uber. For a 20 km trip to the airport, the substitutes narrow considerably. This is why Uber charges more for airport trips — the alternatives are much worse.

The threat of substitutes for Uber is high in short distances (walking, cycling, autos) and low for medium-to-long distances. Uber's pricing strategy reflects this.

### The Sugar-Jaggery Substitution in India

Here is a more grounded example. Sugar and jaggery (gur) are substitutes. They do different "jobs" in different contexts — jaggery for traditional sweets and tea in rural areas, refined sugar for industrial food production — but they partially overlap.

When sugar prices spike, some households switch to jaggery. When jaggery prices spike, some industries switch to sugar or corn syrup. This substitution puts a ceiling on how high either product can be priced. Farmers and manufacturers know this well.

### What This Means in Practice

When assessing your business, ask: "What would my customer do if I doubled my price tomorrow?" If the answer is "they would use X instead," then X is your substitute and the threat is real. Your pricing power is constrained by how good the substitute is.

The best way to reduce the threat of substitutes is to be so good at the specific job that no substitute can match you. A great cardiologist faces little substitution threat because alternative medicine cannot do heart surgery. A generic web design agency faces enormous substitution threat from Wix, Squarespace, and AI tools.

## 7. Force 5: Competitive Rivalry

### What It Means

This is the force most people focus on — the direct competition between existing players in your market. **How intense is the fight between the companies already in your industry?**

High rivalry means constant price wars, heavy advertising spend, racing to add features, and thin margins for everyone. Low rivalry means companies can compete on value rather than price, margins are healthy, and the industry grows in a more orderly way.

### What Drives High Rivalry?

**1. Many competitors of similar size**
When there are 10 roughly equal-sized players in a market, every one of them is fighting to be number one. No single player is dominant enough to set prices that others follow. This is the situation in Indian cloud kitchens — Rebel Foods, Biryani By Kilo, Freshmenu, and dozens of regional players compete ferociously.

**2. Slow industry growth**
When an industry is growing fast, every company can grow just by riding the wave. But when growth slows, the only way to grow is to steal customers from competitors. This turns rivals into enemies. Think about Indian telecom after the initial mobile boom — Jio's entry in 2016 started a brutal price war because the market was no longer growing fast enough to give everyone comfortable space.

**3. High fixed costs**
Airlines are a classic example. Airplanes are enormously expensive. Once a plane is in the air, it costs almost the same whether it is 30% full or 100% full. So airlines are desperate to fill every seat, even at deep discounts. This structural pressure creates constant fare wars. IndiGo, Air India, and SpiceJet are always fighting over price-sensitive passengers.

**4. Low differentiation (commodity products)**
If your product is basically the same as your competitor's, customers will choose on price. That triggers price wars. Steel manufacturers, coal suppliers, and generic drug companies face this relentlessly. But Apple, Zara, and Rolex have differentiated products — their customers are not choosing on price alone, so rivalry is less intense.

**5. High exit barriers**
When it is expensive or emotionally difficult to leave an industry, companies stay and fight even when they are losing money. A steel plant worth ₹5,000 crore cannot simply close overnight — the social, economic, and financial costs of closure are enormous. So unprofitable steel companies keep running, oversupplying the market, and driving prices down for everyone.

### A Vivid Example: The Indian Ed-Tech Wars of 2020-2022

During COVID-19, online education exploded. Byju's, Unacademy, Vedantu, WhiteHat Jr., Physics Wallah, and dozens of others raised billions of dollars and went to war.

The rivalry was intense for every reason Porter identified:
- Many well-funded competitors of similar scale
- All fighting for the same pool of students
- Heavy discounting to acquire customers
- Massive marketing spend (every IPL match was covered in ed-tech ads)
- Low differentiation (all offering video lectures for JEE/NEET)

The result? Most of these companies burned through their capital. Byju's went from a $22 billion valuation to bankruptcy proceedings. Vedantu laid off thousands of employees. Unacademy scaled back dramatically.

The industry had too much money chasing too few customers, with too little differentiation. Porter would have predicted this outcome.

The exception: Physics Wallah. Alakh Pandey built it with almost no external capital, kept prices extremely low (sometimes free), and differentiated through genuine teaching quality and a loyal community. He won not by out-spending rivals but by being genuinely different.

## 8. Putting It All Together — Analyzing an Industry You Might Enter

Here is a practical framework for analyzing any industry using all five forces. Score each force from 1 (very weak, favorable to you) to 5 (very strong, unfavorable to you).

```
INDUSTRY ANALYSIS SCORECARD
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Force                          Score (1-5)   Notes
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Threat of New Entrants         ___           Barriers: capital, regulation, brand?
Supplier Bargaining Power      ___           How many suppliers? Switching costs?
Buyer Bargaining Power         ___           Concentrated buyers? Commoditized product?
Threat of Substitutes          ___           Can customers meet the same need another way?
Competitive Rivalry            ___           Number of players, differentiation, growth rate?

TOTAL SCORE: ___/25

10-15: Attractive industry — low structural competition
16-20: Moderate — need a clear competitive advantage
21-25: Difficult industry — requires exceptional execution
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════
```

No industry is a 5/5 on all dimensions (that would be a disaster). Most attractive industries score low on two or three forces because they have structural advantages that protect incumbents.

The goal is not to find a perfect 5/5 opportunity — it does not exist. The goal is to (a) understand the forces you will face, and (b) build a strategy that either avoids the strongest forces or builds defenses against them.

## 9. Full Case Study: India's Quick Commerce Industry

Quick commerce is the business of delivering groceries and daily essentials in 10-20 minutes. The major players as of 2024 are Zepto, Blinkit (owned by Zomato), and Swiggy Instamart. Let us run all five forces on this industry.

### Setting the Scene

Quick commerce in India exploded between 2021 and 2023. Zepto raised over $1 billion. Blinkit was acquired by Zomato for ~₹4,500 crore. Swiggy invested heavily in Instamart. By 2023, the combined market was worth several billion dollars and growing 60-80% annually in major cities.

But is this a good industry to be in? Let us check.

---

### Force 1: Threat of New Entrants — MODERATE TO HIGH (Score: 3/5)

**Barriers that exist:**
- Dark store network: You need to set up hundreds of "dark stores" (small warehouses in neighborhoods) to deliver in 10-20 minutes. Zepto had 350+ dark stores by 2024. This takes significant capital — ₹50-100 crore per city to set up properly.
- Logistics tech: The routing algorithms, inventory management software, and delivery tracking systems take years to build well.
- Brand and trust: Customers have to trust that their groceries will arrive fresh and on time.

**Barriers that are weak:**
- A well-funded new entrant (a Reliance, or a foreign player like GoPuff) could replicate the model. The concept is not patented.
- BigBasket (Tata-owned) launched BB Now in quick commerce. Amazon launched Amazon Fresh. Flipkart launched Minutes. The barriers were not high enough to keep big players out.

**Verdict:** Some barriers, but not strong enough to prevent well-funded entry. Big platforms with existing logistics are natural new entrants.

---

### Force 2: Bargaining Power of Suppliers — LOW (Score: 2/5)

Quick commerce companies buy from FMCG brands (Hindustan Unilever, Dabur, P&G, ITC), regional producers, and farmers. The supplier landscape is:

- Highly fragmented — thousands of FMCG brands want shelf space
- Suppliers need quick commerce platforms to reach customers
- Platforms have buying power (purchasing at scale)
- Platforms can switch suppliers easily (if HUL's Surf Excel is too expensive, they can stock Ghari detergent)

The exception: premium or niche products that have their own brand pull (like Amul, which customers specifically ask for) have more power. But most suppliers are price-takers.

**Verdict:** Supplier power is relatively low. This is favorable for the industry's profitability.

---

### Force 3: Bargaining Power of Buyers — MODERATE (Score: 3/5)

**Factors that reduce buyer power:**
- Individual customers are small — no single customer represents meaningful leverage
- Convenience and speed are addictive — once customers experience 10-minute delivery, they do not want to go back
- The customer base (urban, working, time-poor) values time over price

**Factors that increase buyer power:**
- All three platforms are available on the same phone — switching is one tap away
- Customers are price-sensitive for commodities (onions, milk, bread)
- Promo-hopping: customers follow the best offer across platforms, reducing loyalty
- Price comparison is instant on smartphones

**Verdict:** Individual customers have moderate power collectively through easy switching. This forces platforms to run constant discounts, which hurts profitability.

---

### Force 4: Threat of Substitutes — HIGH (Score: 4/5)

This is the biggest structural challenge for quick commerce.

| Substitute | Cost | Convenience | For whom? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Traditional kirana store | Low | High (trust, credit) | Everyone, esp. non-urban |
| Supermarket (D-Mart, More) | Low | Moderate | Weekly shoppers |
| Standard grocery delivery (BigBasket 2-hour) | Low | Good | Planners |
| Self-pickup at retail | Low | Low | Already out anyway |
| Weekly household purchase | Lowest | Planned | Disciplined households |

Most Indian households still primarily shop at kirana stores. Quick commerce is largely an urban, upper-middle-class habit. The moment a customer plans ahead, the substitute (kirana store, D-Mart, or slow delivery) is usually cheaper. Quick commerce's premium is entirely based on the value of not planning — convenience for forgetful or last-minute needs.

This limits how much quick commerce can charge. If Zepto raises prices 20%, many customers will just plan their shopping better and visit D-Mart.

**Verdict:** High threat of substitutes. This is a structural weakness. Quick commerce can grow share by changing habits, but it will always face this ceiling.

---

### Force 5: Competitive Rivalry — VERY HIGH (Score: 5/5)

This is where quick commerce truly struggles.

- **Three major players** (Zepto, Blinkit, Swiggy Instamart) of comparable size, all well-funded.
- **Undifferentiated product:** Groceries are commodities. The onions from Zepto and the onions from Blinkit are the same onions.
- **Price wars and promotion wars:** Every platform runs constant discounts, cashback offers, and free delivery promotions. This destroys margins.
- **Massive fixed costs:** Dark stores, delivery fleet, and inventory must be maintained regardless of order volume.
- **Heavy marketing spend:** Every IPL, every festival, every cricket match — all three platforms are advertising aggressively.

As of 2023, none of the three platforms was profitable at a unit economics level before accounting for overhead. They were all burning cash to acquire customers and fight each other.

**Verdict:** Maximum rivalry. This is the industry's defining challenge.

---

### Overall Quick Commerce Score

| Force | Score | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Threat of New Entrants | 3/5 | Moderate barriers, but big players can enter |
| Supplier Power | 2/5 | Favorable — suppliers need the platforms |
| Buyer Power | 3/5 | Moderate — easy switching, price sensitivity |
| Threat of Substitutes | 4/5 | Serious — kirana stores, D-Mart, planning ahead |
| Competitive Rivalry | 5/5 | Brutal — three funded players, commodity product |
| **TOTAL** | **17/25** | Structurally difficult |

**The honest verdict:** Quick commerce is a structurally challenging industry. The only way to win is to become the dominant player (ideally, one of the three becomes the clear number one and the others shrink or exit), achieve massive scale to reduce costs per order, and lock in customer habits before the window closes.

This is exactly why Zomato (Blinkit) and Swiggy have been willing to burn enormous cash — they are betting that if they survive the war, the winner will eventually be profitable at scale. It is a rational bet, but it requires tolerating years of losses.

Zepto's bet is slightly different: stay leaner, grow in Tier 2 cities where competition is less intense, and win on execution rather than advertising.

## 10. Limitations of Porter's Framework

Porter's Five Forces is one of the most useful tools in business, but it is not perfect. Here are the important limitations you should know.

**It is static, not dynamic.** The framework takes a snapshot of competitive forces at one moment. But industries evolve. Jio's entry into Indian telecom in 2016 completely transformed the competitive forces in that industry within 18 months. A 2015 Porter analysis of Indian telecom would be useless by 2017.

**It focuses on existing industries, not new ones.** When Steve Jobs launched the iPhone in 2007, there was no "smartphone industry" as we know it today. The framework cannot easily analyze industries that do not yet exist. It is better at helping you understand existing markets than imagining future ones.

**It ignores complementors.** Some companies make your product more valuable without competing with you. Intel and Microsoft made each other more valuable for 30 years — they were "complementors." Porter's framework does not capture this well.

**It is best for industry-level analysis, not company-level.** Two companies in the same industry (e.g., IndiGo and Air India) face the same five forces but have very different strategies and profitability. The framework explains the industry context but not why specific companies outperform others within it. For that, you need additional tools like the Value Chain Analysis or the Resource-Based View of the firm.

**It can be gamed.** Companies that know Porter's framework sometimes use it to build artificial barriers rather than create genuine value. A large incumbent might lobby the government for regulations that make it harder for new entrants — not because the regulation benefits consumers, but because it protects the incumbent's profits.

Despite these limitations, Porter's Five Forces remains the best single tool for understanding why some industries are profitable and others are not. Use it alongside other frameworks, not as the only lens.

## 11. Summary

Here is what you learned in this chapter:

**The core idea:** Industry structure determines how profitable a business can be. Before entering any market, understand the five forces that will determine your fate.

**The five forces:**

1. **Threat of New Entrants** — How easily can new competitors enter your market? High barriers (capital, regulation, brand, network effects) protect you. Low barriers expose you to constant new competition.

2. **Bargaining Power of Suppliers** — If your suppliers have power over you, they can raise your costs. Diversify supply, build alternatives, and never let one supplier become irreplaceable.

3. **Bargaining Power of Buyers** — If your customers have power over you, they will push your prices down. Build loyalty, differentiate your product, and never let one customer represent too much of your revenue.

4. **Threat of Substitutes** — Are there completely different ways your customers could meet the same need? If yes, your pricing power is limited. Reduce the threat by being deeply excellent at the specific job you do.

5. **Competitive Rivalry** — How intense is the fight between existing players? Commodity products with many equal-sized competitors in slow-growth markets face the most intense rivalry.

**The quick commerce lesson:** Even a fast-growing, exciting industry like quick commerce can be structurally weak. High rivalry, easy substitution, and moderate buyer power mean profitability requires becoming the dominant player — which requires burning a lot of cash first.

**The framework's limitation:** It is a snapshot, not a movie. Use it to understand your starting position, but keep re-running the analysis as industries evolve.

3. **Substitute identification exercise:** For each of the following products, identify three non-obvious substitutes (not direct competitors, but different ways to meet the same need):
   - A gym membership
   - A business class airline ticket
   - A coaching class for CA exams
   - A hotel room on a work trip

4. **Supplier power scenario:** You run a restaurant that uses only one specific supplier for your signature ingredient — a particular variety of hill-grown cheese from a small farm in Himachal Pradesh. Your supplier knows they are your only source. Using Porter's framework, analyze this relationship. What should you do to reduce your risk?

5. **The Jio disruption:** In 2015, before Reliance Jio launched, run a Porter's analysis on the Indian telecom industry. Then re-run the same analysis in 2018, after Jio had been operating for two years. What changed in each force? What does this tell you about the framework's limitations?

6. **Design your moat:** You are starting a new B2B software company that helps small restaurants manage inventory and orders. List five specific things you would do — one for each Porter force — to make the competitive environment more favorable for your company over the next three years.

7. **Debate:** Some people argue that Porter's framework is outdated in the era of platform businesses (Google, Amazon, Uber, Airbnb) because platforms can simultaneously be suppliers, buyers, and competitors. Do you agree? Which parts of the framework still apply to platform businesses and which do not?
