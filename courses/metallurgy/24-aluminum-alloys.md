# Chapter 24: Aluminum Alloys — The Aerospace Metal

> **"Every modern commercial aircraft consists of approximately 70–80% aluminum by weight. The Boeing 777 uses 70,000 kg of aluminum alloys in its structure. Without aluminum, commercial aviation as we know it would not exist — the weight penalty of steel would make most aircraft economically non-viable. Aluminum's combination of low density, high specific strength, and corrosion resistance changed the world."**

## 1. Why Aluminum?

**Key properties of aluminum:**

| Property | Value | Comparison |
|---------|-------|-----------|
| Density | 2.70 g/cm³ | 35% of steel (7.87) |
| Young's modulus E | 69 GPa | 33% of steel (207 GPa) |
| Yield strength (alloy) | 70–570 MPa | Up to 75% of steel |
| Specific strength σ_y/ρ | 200 kN·m/kg | 2× structural steel |
| Melting point | 660°C | Low → easy processing |
| Electrical conductivity | 61% IACS | Good (used in power lines) |
| Corrosion resistance | Excellent | Al₂O₃ passive film |
| Recyclability | Excellent | 95% less energy than primary |

**Critical limitation:** Low melting point means Al alloys cannot be used above ~200°C sustained → maximum use temperature ~180°C for 7xxx, ~200°C for 2219.

## 2. Aluminum Alloy Designation System

**Wrought alloys** (worked/processed):
```
Series  Principal Alloying Element    Heat Treatable?
1xxx    Pure Al (≥99%)               No
2xxx    Cu (major)                   Yes
3xxx    Mn                           No
4xxx    Si                           No (some filler alloys yes)
5xxx    Mg                           No
6xxx    Mg + Si                      Yes
7xxx    Zn (+ Mg, Cu)                Yes
8xxx    Other (Li, Fe, etc.)         Some
```

**Cast alloys:** 3-digit number + decimal:
```
1xx.x   Pure aluminum
2xx.x   Al-Cu
3xx.x   Al-Si-Mg or Al-Si-Cu
4xx.x   Al-Si
5xx.x   Al-Mg
7xx.x   Al-Zn
```

## 3. Temper Designations

| Temper | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| -F | As fabricated (no special thermal/strain control) |
| -O | Annealed (softest) |
| -H1x | Strain hardened (x = amount of work) |
| -H2x | Strain hardened + partially annealed |
| -T3 | Solution treated + cold worked + naturally aged |
| -T4 | Solution treated + naturally aged |
| -T5 | Artificially aged only (from elevated temperature fabrication) |
| -T6 | Solution treated + peak artificially aged |
| -T7 | Solution treated + overaged (better SCC resistance) |
| -T8 | Solution treated + cold worked + peak artificially aged |

## 4. 1xxx — Pure Aluminum

**Composition:** ≥ 99.0% Al
**Strengthening:** Cold work only (no precipitation hardening)
**σ_y:** 10–150 MPa depending on temper

**Properties:** Maximum corrosion resistance, electrical conductivity, reflectivity, formability.

**Applications:**
- 1350: electrical conductors (overhead power lines, bus bars)
- 1100: food processing equipment, chemical tanks, decorative trim
- 1050: foil, automotive trim

---

## 5. 2xxx — Al-Cu Alloys

**Principal alloying element:** Copper (2–6% Cu + Mg for 2024)

**Precipitation sequence:** GP zones → θ″ → θ′ → θ (CuAl₂) — see Ch 21

**Key alloys:**

**2024 (Al-4.4Cu-1.5Mg-0.6Mn):**
- The classic aerospace alloy — "Duralumin"
- T3 temper: σ_y = 345 MPa, σ_UTS = 483 MPa, K_Ic = 26 MPa√m
- T4 temper: slightly lower strength, more formable
- Used in: fuselage skins, wing lower skins (tensile loading)
- Weakness: susceptible to corrosion → always clad with pure Al (Alclad 2024)

**2219 (Al-6.3Cu + Mn, Zr, V, Ti):**
- Better weld ability + high temperature (to 175°C)
- T87 temper: σ_y = 393 MPa
- Used in: fuel tanks for Saturn V, Space Shuttle external tank (now replaced by 2195 Al-Li)

**2025 (Al-4.5Cu + Mn, Si):**
- Forgeable: aircraft propeller hubs

---

## 6. 5xxx — Al-Mg Alloys

**Principal alloying element:** Magnesium (0.5–5.5% Mg)

**Strengthening:** Solid solution hardening (Mg is a large atom in Al → strong solid solution effect) + work hardening. NOT heat-treatable.

**Key alloys:**

**5083 (Al-4.4Mg-0.7Mn):**
- Best Al alloy for marine environments
- Good strength in as-welded condition
- Used in: LNG tanker hulls, naval vessels, offshore structures
- Warning: susceptible to sensitization (β-phase Al₃Mg₂ at grain boundaries) at T > 65°C sustained → SCC risk

**5052 (Al-2.5Mg-0.25Cr):**
- Excellent formability and corrosion resistance
- Used in: marine hardware, fuel tanks, appliances

**5182 (Al-4.5Mg-0.35Mn):**
- Can lid stock: billions of beverage cans/year

---

## 7. 6xxx — Al-Mg-Si Alloys

**Precipitation:** Mg₂Si (β phase) via GP zones → β″ → β′ → β

**Key feature:** Excellent extrudability → used for complex structural profiles.

**Key alloys:**

**6061 (Al-1Mg-0.6Si-0.3Cu-0.2Cr):**
- "The standard" — most widely used structural aluminum
- T6: σ_y = 276 MPa, σ_UTS = 310 MPa, excellent corrosion resistance
- Excellent weldability (alloy 4043 filler)
- Used in: bicycle frames, truck frames, structural profiles, boat hulls

**6082 (Al-1Mg-1Si-0.7Mn-0.15Cr):**
- European equivalent of 6061 but slightly higher strength
- T6: σ_y = 255 MPa

**6063 (Al-0.7Mg-0.4Si):**
- Architectural extrusions: window frames, door sills, anodized profiles
- T5/T6: σ_y = 145–214 MPa; not a structural alloy

**Caution:** 6061-T6 loses ~30% strength in the HAZ when welded due to dissolution of β″ precipitates → design with weld joints in low-stress zones or post-weld age.

---

## 8. 7xxx — Al-Zn-Mg-Cu Alloys

**The strongest Al alloys.** Precipitation: MgZn₂ (η phase) → GP zones → η″ → η′ → η

**Key alloys:**

**7075 (Al-5.6Zn-2.5Mg-1.6Cu-0.23Cr):**
- Highest-strength "standard" aluminum alloy
- T6: σ_y = 503 MPa, σ_UTS = 572 MPa — exceeds structural steel yield strength!
- T73: σ_y = 435 MPa, excellent SCC resistance (overaged, see Ch 21)
- K_Ic(T6): ~29 MPa√m; K_Ic(T73): ~32 MPa√m
- Used in: aircraft upper wing skins (compression), fuselage frames, military vehicles

**7068 (Al-7.2Zn-2.5Mg-2.0Cu):**
- Higher strength than 7075
- T6511: σ_y = 593 MPa

**7050 (Al-6.2Zn-2.3Mg-2.3Cu + Zr):**
- Zr addition → Al₃Zr dispersoids → controls recrystallization + improves fatigue
- Better K_Ic and corrosion than 7075 in thick sections
- T7452: σ_y = 448 MPa; excellent for thick plates, forgings

**7055 (Al-8.0Zn-2.0Mg-2.3Cu):**
- Used in Boeing 777 upper wing stringers
- T77: σ_y = 607 MPa (highest-strength commercial Al alloy)

**SCC risk:** 7xxx in T6 temper is highly susceptible to stress corrosion cracking in moist environments with tensile stress. T73 or T76 temper (overaged) required for SCC-sensitive applications.

## 9. Cast Aluminum Alloys

Most cast aluminum alloys are Al-Si based:
- Si improves castability (lower melting point, better fluidity)
- Si content: 5–12% for most cast alloys

**A356 (Al-7Si-0.4Mg):**
- Most widely used aerospace casting alloy
- T6 heat treatment: σ_y = 207 MPa
- Used in: aircraft wheels, structural castings, automotive wheels

**A319 (Al-6Si-3Cu):**
- Common for automotive engine components: cylinder heads, engine blocks

**Hypereutectic Al-Si (A390: 17%Si):**
- Primary Si crystals for wear resistance
- Automotive cylinder bores (Al block with Si liners)

## 10. Aluminum Processing

**Primary production:**
1. Bauxite ore (Al₂O₃·nH₂O) mined → Bayer process → alumina Al₂O₃
2. Hall-Héroult electrolysis: Al₂O₃ + 6e⁻ → 2Al + 3O²⁻ (energy-intensive: 15 kWh/kg)
3. Alloying → direct-chill (DC) casting → large ingots

**Wrought processing:**
- Hot rolling: ingot → slab → plate → sheet at 400–500°C
- Cold rolling: for precise thickness + work hardening
- Extrusion: heated billet pushed through die → complex profiles
- Forging: closed-die at 400–450°C → near-net-shape complex parts

**Heat treatment critical parameters (7075-T6 example):**
```
1. Solution treat: 470°C ± 3°C for 2 hours (too high → grain boundary melting!)
2. Quench: cold water within 15 seconds of removal from furnace (quench sensitivity!)
3. Age: 120°C for 24 hours (T6)
```

**Joining:**
- Welding: 5xxx and 6xxx weld well (MIG/TIG); 2xxx and 7xxx difficult (hot cracking, HAZ softening) → use friction stir welding (FSW) for 2xxx/7xxx
- Fastening: Ti or Al rivets (avoid galvanic corrosion with steel/carbon fiber)

## Exercises

1. A structural bracket must support a load requiring σ_y > 300 MPa with minimum weight. Compare: (a) 6061-T6, (b) 2024-T3, (c) 7075-T6. For each: state σ_y, density (assume 2.70 g/cm³ for all), specific strength σ_y/ρ, and the minimum area cross-section required for a 100 kN tensile load. Which has the lightest design?

2. A Boeing 737 fuselage skin is made of 2024-T3 Alclad sheet. (a) Why is Alclad (pure Al cladding) used instead of bare 2024? (b) The aircraft operates between -50°C and +70°C. What temperature-related degradation mechanism is relevant? (c) After 20 years of service, the aircraft will be inspected for fatigue cracks. What NDE method would be used for the fuselage skin, and what crack size can it reliably detect?

3. 7075-T6 wing panels are being specified for a new aircraft. The design stress is 350 MPa tensile, but the panels will be exposed to tropical marine atmosphere. A senior engineer recommends T73 temper instead of T6. (a) Calculate the weight penalty of switching from T6 (σ_y = 503 MPa) to T73 (σ_y = 435 MPa) for the same load (panels must be thicker). (b) What microstructural change explains the SCC resistance improvement in T73? (c) Is there any way to get near-T6 strength AND good SCC resistance? (Hint: investigate 7050-T74 and retrogression-re-aging).

4. 6061-T6 extrusions are being butt-welded with TIG welding using 4043 filler wire. After welding, the joint fails at only 60% of the base metal strength (σ_y = 165 MPa instead of 276 MPa). (a) Explain the microstructural cause of HAZ softening. (b) Would post-weld T6 aging (solution treat + age) restore full strength? (c) Why is Friction Stir Welding (FSW) better for 6061 than TIG? (d) What industries use FSW for Al aircraft assemblies?

5. Modern commercial aircraft are increasingly using carbon fiber reinforced polymers (CFRP) instead of aluminum for primary structure. (a) Compare CFRP (ρ = 1.55 g/cm³, σ_y = 600 MPa) vs. 7075-T6 (ρ = 2.80 g/cm³, σ_y = 503 MPa) on specific strength. (b) What advantage does aluminum have over CFRP for impact resistance? (c) For a wing spar exposed to fatigue loading, which material has an advantage and why (consider Paris Law exponent m ≈ 4 for Al vs. ≈ 10 for CFRP)? (d) Why are fastener holes a critical concern at Al-CFRP interfaces?
