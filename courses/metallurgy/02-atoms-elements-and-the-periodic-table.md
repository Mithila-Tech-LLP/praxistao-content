## 1. The Atom — Structure and Scale

An atom consists of three types of particles:

| Particle | Location | Charge | Mass |
|----------|----------|--------|------|
| Proton | Nucleus | +1 | 1.673 × 10⁻²⁷ kg |
| Neutron | Nucleus | 0 | 1.675 × 10⁻²⁷ kg |
| Electron | Surrounding the nucleus in orbitals | −1 | 9.109 × 10⁻³¹ kg (1/1836 of proton) |

**Scale — this is hard to intuit:**
- A typical atom is ~0.1–0.5 nm in diameter (1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m)
- An iron atom is 0.252 nm across
- A copper wire 1 mm in diameter is about 4 million copper atoms across
- The nucleus is 100,000 times smaller than the atom (if an atom were a football stadium, the nucleus would be a pea at the center)

The **atomic number (Z)** is the number of protons. It uniquely defines the element. Iron always has Z = 26; nickel always has Z = 28. Change the number of protons and you change the element.

The **atomic mass** is the total of protons + neutrons. Iron's most common form has 30 neutrons, giving atomic mass ≈ 56.

A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons. The electrons are what matter for chemical behavior and bonding.

## 2. Electrons, Shells, and Orbitals

Electrons don't orbit the nucleus like planets — they exist in **probability clouds** called orbitals. But for metallurgy, the simplified shell model is very useful.

### Electron Shells

Electrons fill into shells numbered n = 1, 2, 3, 4...  
Each shell holds a maximum of 2n² electrons:
- Shell 1: max 2 electrons
- Shell 2: max 8 electrons
- Shell 3: max 18 electrons
- Shell 4: max 32 electrons

### Subshells (Orbitals)

Within each shell, electrons fill subshells labeled s, p, d, f:

| Subshell | Orbitals | Max electrons | Shape |
|----------|----------|---------------|-------|
| s | 1 | 2 | Sphere |
| p | 3 | 6 | Dumbbells (3 directions) |
| d | 5 | 10 | Complex (5 shapes) |
| f | 7 | 14 | Very complex |

The **filling order** (Aufbau principle): 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, **4s, 3d**, 4p, **5s, 4d**, 5p, **6s, 4f, 5d**, 6p...

Note: the 4s fills before 3d, and 5s before 4d — this is why transition metals (like Fe, Ni, Cu) have partially filled d shells.

### Why the d Shell Matters for Metallurgy

The **d electrons** (in the d subshell) are responsible for:
- Magnetism (unpaired d electrons → ferromagnetism in Fe, Co, Ni)
- High melting points of transition metals (d-d bonding)
- Catalytic activity
- The unique bonding in superalloys (Ni-base alloys exploit d-electron chemistry)

**Iron (Fe, Z=26) electron configuration:**
`1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d⁶ 4s²`

4 unpaired d electrons → iron is ferromagnetic below 770°C (the Curie temperature).

**Nickel (Ni, Z=28) electron configuration:**
`1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d⁸ 4s²`

Nickel has 8 d electrons. Two are paired, meaning 2 unpaired → nickel is also ferromagnetic.

### Valence Electrons

The **outermost shell electrons** are the valence electrons. They determine:
- How atoms bond to each other
- The element's chemical reactivity
- Whether something is a metal, non-metal, or metalloid

Most metals have 1–3 valence electrons. This is key: with few tightly-held valence electrons, metals form the metallic bond (see Chapter 3).

## 3. The Periodic Table — A Metallurgist's Map

The periodic table organizes elements by atomic number, with rows (periods) and columns (groups) that reveal patterns.

### The Periodic Table Layout for Metals

```
  IA  IIA                              IIIB IVB  VB  VIB VIIB VIIIB IB  IIB  IIIA IVA  VA  VIA VIIA  0
  H                                                                                                    He
  Li  Be                                                                       B    C    N    O    F   Ne
  Na  Mg   ←—————————— Transition Metals ———————————————→                     Al   Si   P    S    Cl  Ar
  K   Ca  [Sc  Ti   V  Cr  Mn  Fe  Co  Ni  Cu  Zn]        Ga   Ge  As   Se   Br   Kr
  Rb  Sr  [Y   Zr  Nb  Mo  Tc  Ru  Rh  Pd  Ag  Cd]        In   Sn  Sb   Te   I    Xe
  Cs  Ba  [La  Hf  Ta   W  Re  Os  Ir  Pt  Au  Hg]        Tl   Pb  Bi   Po   At   Rn
```

Metals occupy the **left and center** of the periodic table. The boundary between metals and non-metals runs diagonally from B-Si-Ge-As-Sb-Te.

### Periodic Trends Relevant to Metallurgy

| Property | Trend Across a Period (→) | Trend Down a Group (↓) | Metallurgical Significance |
|----------|--------------------------|----------------------|---------------------------|
| Atomic radius | Decreases | Increases | Packing; solid solution effects |
| Melting point | Peaks at Group VI (W, Cr, Mo) | Varies | High-temp alloy design |
| Electronegativity | Increases | Decreases | Tendency to form intermetallics |
| Density | Generally increases | Increases | Weight-critical applications |
| Ionization energy | Increases | Decreases | Corrosion tendency |

### Groups of Special Interest to Metallurgists

**Group IA (Alkali Metals):** Li, Na, K — too reactive for structural use but Li used in Al alloys for density reduction.

**Group IIA (Alkaline Earth):** Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba — Mg is the lightest structural metal; Ca/Sr used as de-oxidizers and de-sulfurizers in steel.

**Group IVB:** Ti, Zr, Hf — lightweight, high melting point, excellent corrosion resistance. Ti is vital for aerospace; Zr and Hf are added to superalloys for grain boundary strengthening.

**Group VB:** V, Nb, Ta — V and Nb used in microalloyed steels; Nb added to Ni superalloys for γ″ strengthening; Ta for high-temperature corrosion resistance.

**Group VIB:** Cr, Mo, W — Cr for corrosion resistance (stainless steel); Mo for hardenability; W for high-temperature strength (highest melting metal at 3422°C).

**Group VIII:** Fe, Co, Ni (plus Ru, Rh, Pd, Os, Ir, Pt) — the **iron-group transition metals** are the foundation of all structural metallurgy. Nickel is the base of superalloys.

**Group IB:** Cu, Ag, Au — excellent conductors; Cu is the benchmark conductor; Au for contacts; Ag for some high-performance uses.

## 4. Why Most Elements Are Metals

75 of 118 elements are metals. That's because the metallic bond (low electronegativity, few loosely-held outer electrons) is the most common electronic configuration.

An element is metallic when:
1. It has a **low ionization energy** — easy to lose valence electrons
2. It has a **low electronegativity** — doesn't want to grab electrons from neighbors
3. It has **few valence electrons** relative to its electron shells

This combination makes it energetically favorable to let the valence electrons roam freely through the crystal — the **electron sea** — rather than being shared directionally (covalent) or transferred (ionic).

The **right side of the periodic table** (high electronegativity, high ionization energy) → non-metals. The **left and center** → metals. The diagonal zone → metalloids (Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te) with intermediate behavior.

> **Rhenium** (Re) is singled out here because it's the single most important alloying addition in modern superalloys. A 3% Re addition to a nickel superalloy improves creep life by 2–3× at 1000°C. ## 6. Atomic Radius, Density, and Packing

### Atomic Radius

The **atomic radius** (half the distance between two atoms of the same element bonded together) ranges from 0.053 nm (H) to 0.260 nm (Cs).

For metals, typical radii are:
- Small: Al 0.143 nm, Fe 0.126 nm, Ni 0.124 nm
- Medium: Cu 0.128 nm, Ti 0.145 nm
- Large: W 0.141 nm, Mo 0.139 nm

**Why this matters for alloying:** When you put a second element into a host metal, the size difference determines whether you get a solid solution or an intermetallic compound. The **Hume-Rothery rules** (more in Chapter 10) say: if the radius difference is < 15%, solid solution is possible; if it's > 15%, limited solubility → intermetallics.

Example: Al (0.143 nm) and Cu (0.128 nm): size difference = 10.5% → extensive solid solubility → Cu strengthens Al alloys well.

### Density

Density depends on atomic mass and how tightly atoms pack. Metals range enormously:

| Metal | Density (g/cm³) |
|-------|----------------|
| Lithium | 0.53 |
| Magnesium | 1.74 |
| Aluminum | 2.70 |
| Titanium | 4.51 |
| Iron | 7.87 |
| Nickel | 8.91 |
| Copper | 8.96 |
| Molybdenum | 10.28 |
| Rhenium | 21.02 |
| Tungsten | 19.25 |

In superalloy design, **density is a critical constraint**: adding heavy elements like Re and W improves strength but increases blade density. A heavier blade requires stronger disk, bigger engine, heavier airframe. The density-normalized creep strength is the true figure of merit.

## 7. Electronegativity and Mixing Tendency

**Electronegativity** (Pauling scale) measures how strongly an atom pulls electrons toward itself when bonded.

Low electronegativity metals (like Al, Mg, Na) lose their electrons easily → strong metallic bonding.  
High electronegativity elements (like O, N, F) pull electrons from metals → oxidation, nitridation.

The difference in electronegativity between two elements predicts what kind of bond forms:
- ΔEN < 0.4 → metallic bond (metallic alloy)
- 0.4 < ΔEN < 1.7 → polar covalent or intermetallic
- ΔEN > 1.7 → ionic bond (oxide, nitride)

This is why:
- Cr (EN = 1.66) + O (EN = 3.44): ΔEN = 1.78 → ionic Cr₂O₃ forms → corrosion protection layer
- Al (EN = 1.61) + O (EN = 3.44): ΔEN = 1.83 → Al₂O₃ forms → anodize aluminum
- Ni (EN = 1.91) + Fe (EN = 1.83): ΔEN = 0.08 → metallic → form solid solution easily (Ni-Fe alloys)

**Electronegativity and hot corrosion:** When sulfur (EN = 2.58) attacks nickel (EN = 1.91) at 900°C, the ΔEN = 0.67 is large enough for ionic-like NiS to form — hot corrosion. Adding aluminum (forms stable Al₂O₃) or chromium (forms Cr₂O₃) protects by providing a more thermodynamically stable oxide.

## 8. Isotopes and Nuclear Concerns

**Isotopes** are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons. Most engineering metals are used as naturally occurring mixtures of their stable isotopes.

However, two situations matter to metallurgists:

**Cobalt-60:** Natural cobalt is almost entirely Co-59 (one stable isotope). When structural cobalt alloys are irradiated with neutrons in nuclear environments, Co-59 absorbs a neutron → radioactive Co-60. This severely limits cobalt alloy use in nuclear power plants.

**Nickel-63:** Similarly, Ni-63 (radioactive, half-life 100 years) can form from Ni-62 in reactor environments. Special low-activation alloys are designed by minimizing elements with problematic isotopic responses.

For most engineering applications — aerospace, power generation, automotive — isotope effects are irrelevant. But for nuclear metallurgists, isotope selection is a primary design driver.

## Summary

- An atom = nucleus (protons + neutrons) + electrons in orbitals. Atomic number (Z) defines the element.
- **Electrons fill shells and subshells** in order. The d-shell electrons are particularly important for transition metals (Fe, Ni, Co, W, Mo, Re) — they drive magnetic behavior, high melting points, and superalloy properties.
- The **periodic table** is a metallurgist's map: group determines valence electrons and bonding character; period determines size and density. Metals occupy the left and center.
- **75 of 118 elements are metals** because metallic bonding (electron sea from low-EN, few valence electrons) is the most common configuration.
- **Atomic radius** predicts alloying behavior (Hume-Rothery rules): < 15% size difference → solid solution possible.
- **Electronegativity difference** predicts bond type: metals mix well (low ΔEN); metal + oxygen → ionic oxide (high ΔEN).
- Key superalloy elements: Ni (base), Cr (oxidation), Al (γ′ strengthening + oxidation), Re (creep resistance), W/Mo (solid solution strengthening), Ta/Nb (γ′ and γ″), Hf/Zr/Y (grain boundary strengthening).
