# Chapter 28: Refractory Metals — Masters of Extreme Temperature

> **"Tungsten melts at 3,422°C — the highest melting point of any element. A tungsten light bulb filament operates at 2,500°C, radiating light for thousands of hours. Molybdenum carries electrical current in furnaces at 1,700°C. Niobium alloys strengthen rocket nozzles that reach 1,800°C. These are the refractory metals — the metals that laugh at temperatures that would vaporize aluminum or melt nickel superalloys. But they all share a common flaw: oxygen kills them at elevated temperature."**

## 1. What Makes a Metal 'Refractory'?

**Definition:** Refractory metals have melting points above approximately 2,200°C:

| Element | Symbol | Melting Point (°C) | Crystal Structure | Density (g/cm³) |
|---------|--------|------------------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Tungsten | W | 3,422 | BCC | 19.3 |
| Rhenium | Re | 3,186 | HCP | 21.0 |
| Osmium | Os | 3,045 | HCP | 22.6 |
| Tantalum | Ta | 3,017 | BCC | 16.6 |
| Molybdenum | Mo | 2,623 | BCC | 10.2 |
| Niobium | Nb | 2,477 | BCC | 8.6 |
| Hafnium | Hf | 2,233 | HCP | 13.3 |
| Iridium | Ir | 2,446 | FCC | 22.6 |
| Chromium | Cr | 1,907 | BCC | 7.1 |

Most refractory metals are BCC → potential for ductile-to-brittle transition (DBTT). Their high melting points are accompanied by high creep resistance (diffusion is proportional to T/T_m — creep doesn't begin until ~1,400–1,600°C for W/Mo).

## 2. Tungsten (W)

**Melting point: 3,422°C** — highest of any element.

**Properties:**
- σ_y (25°C, pure): 450–550 MPa (alloyed W up to 1,000 MPa)
- At 1,200°C: still ~200 MPa (nickel superalloys essentially soft at this T)
- Very high density: 19.3 g/cm³
- Very high thermal conductivity: 174 W/m·K
- Very low CTE: 4.5 μm/m·K (good for thermal stability, dimensional precision)
- DBTT: ~200–400°C (pure W) → BRITTLE at room temperature!

**Critical weakness: oxidation above 500°C:**
```
W + O₂ → WO₃ (gas above 850°C!) 
"Tungsten catastrophic oxidation" — above ~1,000°C in air, WO₃ evaporates → 
no protective scale → rapid metal loss → "pest" behavior
```

Must be used in:
- Vacuum (incandescent bulbs)
- Inert gas atmosphere (Ar, N₂) in furnaces
- Refractory ceramic environment (in nuclear/aerospace where necessary)

**Applications:**
- Incandescent lamp filaments (2,500°C, in Ar atmosphere)
- Electron emitters (cathode ray tubes, X-ray tubes)
- Electrical contacts (tungsten-copper composites for circuit breakers)
- Cutting tools and wear parts as WC-Co cemented carbide (hardest class of materials)
- Kinetic energy penetrators (armor-piercing ammunition) — highest density
- Furnace heating elements (up to 2,800°C in vacuum/H₂ atmosphere)
- Rocket nozzle throats and plasma torch electrodes

**W-Re alloys:** Adding 3–26% Re raises DBTT from 300°C to near RT → improves machinability and ductility. W-Re thermocouple wire (Type C: W-5Re vs W-26Re) used in nuclear reactors and rocket plumes to 2,300°C.

## 3. Molybdenum (Mo)

**Melting point: 2,623°C.** 

**More useful than W for many applications because:**
- DBTT: ~20°C (pure Mo) → workable at room temperature (just barely!)
- Lower density: 10.2 vs. 19.3 g/cm³ for W
- Better machinability than W
- Good resistance to hydrogen, liquid metals

**Critical weakness: oxidation above 600°C:**
```
Mo + O₂ → MoO₃ (liquid above 795°C — and very volatile!)
At 1,000°C in air: rapid oxidation → "molybdenum pest"
```
Like W, must use in controlled atmosphere or vacuum.

**TZM alloy (Mo-0.5Ti-0.08Zr-0.02C):**
- Ti, Zr form TiC and ZrC → pin dislocations → improve strength and DBTT
- σ_y = 700 MPa at 25°C → 350 MPa at 700°C
- DBTT ≈ 0°C (better than pure Mo)
- Used for: rocket engine nozzles (in inert atmosphere), isotope production targets, die casting dies for molten aluminum (Mo resists Al liquid metal attack)

**Mo in superalloys:** Up to 3% Mo dissolves in nickel → solid solution strengthening (large atom, ~12% size mismatch) → CMSX-4 contains 0.6% Mo. Partitions to γ matrix.

**Applications:**
- High-temperature heating elements (up to 1,700°C in H₂ or N₂ atmosphere)
- X-ray tube targets (Mo characteristic radiation used in X-ray spectroscopy)
- Thin-film transistors (Mo in OLED displays)
- Nuclear fuel rod cladding (in some designs — resists neutron damage)

## 4. Niobium (Nb) / Columbium (Cb)

**Melting point: 2,477°C.** Also called "Columbium" (Cb) in older American usage.

**Unique feature among refractories:** Niobium oxidizes in air but the Nb₂O₅ scale is somewhat protective up to ~600°C. Not as catastrophically non-protective as W or Mo.

**Lowest DBTT of BCC refractories:** ~−70°C (well below RT) → niobium is DUCTILE at room temperature, easy to process.

**Niobium-base alloys:**
- C-103 (Nb-10Hf-1Ti): σ_y = 300 MPa at 1,400°C; used in rocket nozzles (Space Shuttle OMS engine nozzle), thermal shields
- C-129Y (Nb-10W-10Hf-0.1Y): highest strength Nb alloy; up to 1,400°C

**Nb in microalloyed steels:** 0.01–0.1% Nb in HSLA steels → NbC precipitates pin austenite grain boundaries during rolling → ASTM grain size 10+ retained → Hall-Petch strengthening (Ch 13). The biggest use of Nb by tonnage.

**Nb in superalloys:** In IN718 → Nb₃Ni γ″ phase. In γ′-forming alloys: Nb partitions to γ′ → strengthens precipitate.

**Superconductors:** NbTi and Nb₃Sn are the commercial superconductors used in MRI machines, particle accelerators (LHC), and experimental fusion reactors. Not a high-temperature use, but accounts for significant Nb demand.

## 5. Tantalum (Ta)

**Melting point: 3,017°C.** Also DUCTILE at RT (DBTT < -200°C) — exceptional for a refractory.

**Key property:** Almost complete corrosion immunity — resists ALL acids except HF and oleum (fuming H₂SO₄). Even HNO₃ at 200°C barely attacks Ta. This is unique among metals.

**Why so corrosion-resistant:** Ta₂O₅ passive film is extremely stable and repairs itself instantly.

**Applications:**
- Chemical plant liners, heat exchangers for highly corrosive acids (where Hastelloy C-276 would fail)
- High-purity sulfuric acid production vessels
- Capacitors: Ta₂O₅ dielectric layer on Ta powder → smallest volume capacitors for electronics (100+ billion Ta capacitors/year in smartphones, etc.)
- Bone and dental implants (biocompatible, no rejection)
- High-temperature X-ray/electron beam targets
- As a "getter" in vacuum systems (absorbs residual gas)

**In superalloys:** CMSX-4 contains 6.5% Ta. The highest-performance γ′ former — Ta partitions preferentially to γ′ and raises its stability and strength:
- Ta is the best refractory γ′ former (k_Ta > 1 → partitions to γ′)
- Slows diffusion → retards γ′ coarsening
- Solid solution strengthening of γ′

**Cost and supply:** Ta is expensive ($200–500/kg) and concentrated in specific geographic regions (Congo, Rwanda, Australia) → supply chain risk → electronics industry tracks "conflict minerals" reporting.

## 6. Rhenium (Re)

**Melting point: 3,186°C.** Second highest after W. HCP structure → no DBTT.

**The most valuable refractory metal for superalloy engineers:**
Re has been the key addition to advance each generation of SX superalloys:
- 2nd generation SX (CMSX-4): 3% Re → +30°C temperature capability
- 3rd generation SX (CMSX-10): 6% Re → +25°C more
- Higher Re → TCP phase risk (see Ch 43)

**Three mechanisms by which Re improves creep resistance (from Ch 16):**
1. Solid solution strengthening (large atom, 9% mismatch) → slows dislocation glide
2. Dislocation climb retardation → Re clusters impede vacancy diffusion that enables climb
3. γ′ coarsening suppression → Re partitions to γ matrix → reduces γ/γ′ interdiffusion

**Other Re applications:**
- W-Re thermocouple alloys (highest T measurement)
- Re-containing catalysts (petroleum reforming — produces high-octane gasoline)
- Rocket propulsion (Re-coated W-Re nozzles)

**Rarity:** Re is one of the rarest stable elements on Earth — crustal abundance ~5 ppb (about 10× less common than gold). Primarily recovered as a byproduct of Mo mining (Mo-Cu-Re porphyry deposits). ~50% of global Re supply goes to superalloy production.

## 7. Other Refractory Metals

**Chromium (Cr): Melting 1,907°C:**
- Excellent oxidation resistance (Cr₂O₃)
- Added to steel (stainless), Ni alloys (oxidation/corrosion resistance)
- Pure Cr is VERY brittle (DBTT > 200°C) → rarely used in pure form structurally
- Hard chromium plating (electroplating) for wear resistance on hydraulic cylinders, aircraft landing gear

**Vanadium (V): Melting 1,910°C:**
- Added to tool steels (V₄C₃ carbides for wear resistance)
- HSLA steels (VC precipitation strengthening)
- Vanadium redox flow batteries (energy storage)
- Oxidizes readily in air above 500°C

**Hafnium (Hf):**
- Excellent resistance to liquid metal attack and high-T creep
- Added to Ni superalloys (CMSX-4 has 0.1% Hf) → γ′ strengthening + oxide scale adhesion
- Nuclear: Hf has very high neutron absorption cross-section → control rods (opposite of Zr which has very LOW absorption — separated in ore processing)

**Zirconium (Zr):**
- Zr metal: very low thermal neutron cross-section → cladding for nuclear fuel rods
- Zircaloy-4 (Zr-1.5Sn-0.2Fe-0.1Cr) → fuel rod cladding in light water reactors
- ZrO₂ (zirconia): TBC material (7%Y₂O₃-ZrO₂ = YSZ) — see Ch 57

## 8. The Critical Problem — DBTT and Oxidation

**DBTT (Ductile-to-Brittle Transition Temperature):**
Most BCC refractory metals are brittle at low temperatures:
```
Metal   DBTT (approx)    Workable at RT?
W       200–400°C        NO (brittle, must process hot)
Mo      ~25°C            BARELY (very sensitive to purity)
Cr      200–400°C        NO
Nb      −70°C            YES (very workable)
Ta      −200°C           YES (excellent ductility)
V       ~−50°C           YES (reasonable)
Re      HCP → no DBTT    YES (ductile through all T)
```

**Catastrophic oxidation:** The other critical limit. At elevated temperature in air:
```
W: WO₃ evaporates above 850°C → no protective scale
Mo: MoO₃ liquid + volatile above 795°C
Cr: Cr₂O₃ protective to 1,000°C, then volatile CrO₃
```

This is why refractory metals require:
- Protective coatings (silicide, aluminide)
- Inert atmosphere or vacuum
- Careful design to prevent air exposure at operating temperature

**Silicide coatings for oxidation protection:**
MoSi₂ or WSi₂ coatings form SiO₂ glass layer at high T → extremely protective.
C-103 Nb alloy nozzles are coated with silicide (R512E: Cr-Si-Fe-B) for use in air at 1,400°C.

## 9. Refractory Metals in Turbine Blades

Refractory metals are NOT used as the structural material of turbine blades (too brittle and too heavy for W, Mo) but they are critical ALLOYING ELEMENTS:

| Element | Amount in CMSX-4 | Role |
|---------|-----------------|------|
| W | 6.4 wt% | Solid solution strengthening of γ matrix |
| Re | 3 wt% | Retards creep (3 mechanisms), see Ch 43 |
| Ta | 6.5 wt% | Best γ′ former, slows γ′ coarsening |
| Mo | 0.6 wt% | Solid solution strengthening |
| Hf | 0.1 wt% | Oxide scale adhesion, γ′ strengthening |

Total refractory additions in CMSX-4: ~17% by weight → each gram of blade contains significant refractory element investment.

**Future RHEAs (Refractory High Entropy Alloys):**
New class of alloys based on W, Mo, Nb, Ta, Cr, V mixtures → potential as structural materials to 1,600°C+ (Ch 61). Currently limited by:
- DBTT (unless dominated by Nb, Ta)
- Catastrophic oxidation
- Very high density (W-heavy compositions)
- Difficult processing

## Exercises

This represents the fraction of melting point the filament operates at — compare to a Ni superalloy blade at 1,050°C (T_m,Ni = 1,455°C). (b) Despite the high homologous temperature, tungsten creep is acceptable because of its BCC crystal structure and low diffusivity. At T/T_m = 0.73 for W vs 0.72 for Ni superalloy, which metal has higher creep rate and why? (c) The filament "sags" over time — what type of deformation mechanism is occurring?

2. Molybdenum must be hot-pressed and hot-worked above 1,000°C because it is brittle at room temperature. (a) Why does BCC structure lead to DBTT while FCC does not? (b) Adding 0.5%Ti + 0.08%Zr (TZM alloy) lowers DBTT to near 0°C. What mechanism is responsible? (c) TZM is used as a die casting mold for liquid aluminum. Why doesn't liquid Al attack Mo (hint: examine Mo-Al phase diagram and compare to Al-Fe system)?

3. CMSX-4 contains 6.5% Ta, 6.4% W, 3% Re, 0.6% Mo. Calculate: (a) the total weight fraction of refractory elements, (b) if Re costs $3,000/kg, Ta $400/kg, W $35/kg, Mo $30/kg, and the alloy costs are proportional to element cost (ignoring Ni cost): what is the refractory element cost per kg of CMSX-4? (c) If a turbine blade weighs 300g and contains CMSX-4, what is the refractory element cost in the blade?

4. Niobium C-103 (Nb-10Hf-1Ti) rocket nozzle is used in a thruster where gas temperature reaches 1,400°C. The nozzle is coated with R512E silicide for oxidation protection. (a) Describe how MoSi₂-type silicide coatings protect Nb in air at 1,400°C (mention SiO₂ formation). (b) What happens if the coating is breached (small crack)? (c) Compare C-103 to a carbon-carbon composite nozzle: both can survive 1,400°C, but C/C needs ceramic coating for oxidation. Which is heavier (ρ_Nb = 8.6 g/cm³ vs ρ_C/C ≈ 1.9 g/cm³)? For a 2-kg nozzle, what volume would each material occupy?

5. Rhenium's global supply is approximately 50 tonnes/year, with ~50% going to superalloy production. (c) This is why Ru (ruthenium) was explored as a partial Re substitute in 4th-generation alloys (TMS-138 has 2%Ru). What is the supply situation for Ru? (d) What research directions address the Re supply chain risk?
