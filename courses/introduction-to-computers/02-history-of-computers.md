## 1. Before Computers — Calculating by Hand

For most of human history, numbers were calculated by people.

```
Ancient counting tools:
  Abacus (2700 BC)     → beads on rods for arithmetic
  Slide rule (1620)    → mechanical multiplication/division
  Logarithm tables     → books of pre-calculated numbers
  Human "computers"    → people paid to do calculations all day
```

In the 1800s, huge teams of mathematicians ("human computers") calculated artillery tables, census data, and astronomical charts by hand. It was slow, expensive, and full of errors.

## 2. The First "Computers" — Mechanical Machines

**Charles Babbage** (1791–1871) had a vision: a machine that could calculate without human error.

```
1822 — Difference Engine
  A mechanical calculator made of gears and levers.
  Could calculate polynomial functions automatically.
  Babbage never finished building it (he ran out of funding).
  
1837 — Analytical Engine
  Babbage's next design — much more ambitious.
  Had an "input" (punch cards), a "store" (memory), a "mill" (CPU), 
  and an "output" (printer).
  This was the conceptual design of the modern computer — 100 years early.
  It was never built in his lifetime.
```

**Ada Lovelace** (1815–1852), Babbage's collaborator, wrote what is considered the **first computer program** — instructions for the Analytical Engine to calculate a sequence of numbers. She is often called the world's first programmer.

## 3. The First Electronic Computers (1940s)

World War II pushed governments to fund computing machines urgently — for breaking codes and calculating artillery trajectories.

```
1936 — Alan Turing
  British mathematician describes a theoretical "Universal Computing Machine"
  — a machine that could simulate any other machine given the right instructions.
  This is the theoretical foundation of all computers.
  
1943 — Colossus (Britain)
  First electronic computer. Used to break German Enigma codes in WWII.
  10 feet tall. 1,500 vacuum tubes.
  Could not be programmed — it was built for one specific task.
  
1945 — ENIAC (USA)
  Electronic Numerical Integrator and Computer.
  First general-purpose electronic computer.
  Size: 1,800 square feet (roughly a tennis court).
  Weight: 30 tons.
  Power: 150,000 watts (your laptop uses about 45 watts).
  Components: 18,000 vacuum tubes, 70,000 resistors, 10,000 capacitors.
  Speed: 5,000 additions per second.
  Your phone does 3,000,000,000 additions per second.
```

```
What are vacuum tubes?
  
  ┌────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  A glass tube, like a light bulb,          │
  │  that could switch electrical signals      │
  │  ON or OFF.                                │
  │                                            │
  │  ON = 1    OFF = 0                         │
  │                                            │
  │  This is where binary computing starts.    │
  │  Every calculation was made by combining   │
  │  millions of ON/OFF switches.              │
  └────────────────────────────────────────────┘
  
  Problem with vacuum tubes:
  - Huge (size of a light bulb)
  - Hot (generated enormous heat)
  - Failed constantly (needed replacement every few days)
  - Expensive
```

## 4. Transistors Change Everything (1950s)

**1947: The transistor is invented at Bell Labs.**

A transistor does the same job as a vacuum tube (switch electrical signals ON/OFF) but:
- Size of a fingernail (not a light bulb)
- Used almost no power
- Barely ever failed
- Cheap to mass produce

```
Vacuum tube vs. Transistor:
  
  Vacuum tube:   🔮 Size of a light bulb   | Hot | Fails weekly
  Transistor:    ▫️  Size of a fingernail   | Cool | Lasts forever
```

Suddenly computers could be smaller, faster, and cheaper. Mainframe computers appeared — still huge by today's standards, but small enough to fit in one room and used by businesses and universities.

**1957: First programming language — FORTRAN**
IBM created a language that let scientists write programs using English-like words instead of raw numbers. The age of software had begun.

## 5. The Microchip — Shrinking the Computer (1960s–70s)

The next revolution: putting many transistors on one tiny chip of silicon.

```
1958–1959: The Integrated Circuit (IC)
  Jack Kilby (Texas Instruments) and Robert Noyce (Fairchild) 
  independently invent the microchip.
  
  Instead of wiring individual transistors together by hand,
  you print them all onto one tiny piece of silicon.
  
  1960: A chip held 4 transistors.
  1970: A chip held 1,000 transistors.
  1980: A chip held 100,000 transistors.
  1990: A chip held 1,000,000 transistors.
  2000: A chip held 40,000,000 transistors.
  2020: Apple M1 chip holds 16,000,000,000 transistors.
```

**Moore's Law (1965):**
Gordon Moore (co-founder of Intel) observed that the number of transistors on a chip doubles roughly every two years. This has held true for 60 years — and explains why computers get twice as powerful every couple of years.

## 6. Personal Computers — Computers for Everyone (1970s–80s)

Before the 1970s, computers were owned only by governments, universities, and large corporations. Then everything changed.

```
1971: Intel 4004 — first microprocessor
  A complete CPU on one chip. A computer's brain the size of a thumbnail.
  
1975: Altair 8800
  First personal computer kit. Hobbyists bought it to assemble themselves.
  A young Bill Gates and Paul Allen wrote software for it — founding Microsoft.
  
1976–1977: Apple I and Apple II
  Steve Wozniak built it. Steve Jobs sold it.
  The Apple II was the first widely successful personal computer with color graphics.
  
1981: IBM PC
  IBM releases the Personal Computer.
  Microsoft's MS-DOS becomes its operating system.
  The "PC" standard is born.
  
1984: Apple Macintosh
  First popular computer with a Graphical User Interface (GUI):
  Instead of typing commands, you use a mouse to click on icons.
  "The computer for the rest of us."
  
1985: Windows 1.0
  Microsoft releases Windows — a GUI on top of MS-DOS.
  Eventually becomes the world's dominant operating system.
```

## 7. The Internet and the Web (1990s)

Computers became truly powerful when they were connected to each other.

```
1969: ARPANET
  US military network connecting 4 universities.
  The ancestor of today's internet.
  
1983: TCP/IP
  The "rules" (protocols) that let different computers talk to each other.
  Every device on the internet still uses TCP/IP today.
  
1991: World Wide Web (Tim Berners-Lee)
  A British scientist at CERN invents the Web.
  Web pages, hyperlinks, HTTP, HTML — the Web is not the internet,
  it's a way of using the internet to share documents.
  
1993: Mosaic browser
  First graphical web browser. You could see images on websites.
  
1994–1995: Amazon, Yahoo, eBay launched
1998: Google launched — changed how we find information forever
1999: Wi-Fi — connect without cables
```

The internet transformed computers from tools for calculation into tools for communication.

## 8. The Mobile Revolution (2000s–2010s)

```
2001: iPod — 1,000 songs in your pocket
  Showed that small computers could be powerful media devices.
  
2003: Skype — free video calls over the internet
2004: Facebook — social networks emerge
2005: YouTube — video sharing
  
2007: iPhone — the most important product launch in modern computing
  Combined phone + music player + internet browser in one device.
  Had a touchscreen (no physical keyboard).
  Opened App Store in 2008 → anyone could write software for it.
  
2008: Android (Google)
  Free, open-source mobile OS.
  Now runs on 75% of smartphones worldwide.
  
2010: iPad — tablet computers
2010s: Billions of people get their first computer via smartphone
```

By 2015, more people were accessing the internet on phones than on computers.

## 9. Today and Tomorrow (2020s)

```
2020: Apple Silicon (M1 chip)
  Apple designs its own CPU for MacBooks.
  Faster than Intel chips. Uses a fraction of the power.
  
2022: ChatGPT — AI for everyone
  For the first time, anyone could have a natural conversation with a computer.
  Artificial intelligence went from research lab to 100 million users in 2 months.
  
2023–2026: AI everywhere
  AI writing code, generating images, answering questions, driving cars.
  
Coming soon:
  Quantum computers — computing using quantum physics
  DNA computers — storing data in biological molecules
  Brain-computer interfaces — connecting your brain to a computer
```
