## 1. Two Fundamental Types: Transverse vs. Longitudinal

Every wave can be classified by asking: **how does the medium's particles move relative to the direction the wave travels?**

There are two answers:

1. **Transverse**: particles move perpendicular (at 90°) to the wave's direction of travel
2. **Longitudinal**: particles move parallel (along the same line) as the wave's direction of travel

These are not just minor technical distinctions — they describe fundamentally different kinds of physical disturbance, with different behaviors, different properties (polarization, for instance, only applies to transverse waves), and different mechanisms for how the energy moves.

---

## 2. Transverse Waves — Definition and Diagram

A **transverse wave** is one where the particles of the medium vibrate **perpendicular** to the direction the wave travels.

```
    Direction of wave travel: →  →  →  →  →  (horizontal)
    
    Direction of particle vibration: ↑ ↓ ↑ ↓  (vertical — perpendicular)
    
    
    Snapshot of transverse wave at one instant:
    
    Displacement
    ^
    |          *         *
    |         * *       * *
    |        *   *     *   *
    |       *     *   *     *
    +------*-------*-*-------*----> Direction of travel
    |     *              *   *
    |    *                  *
    |   *                    *
    
    Each dot (*) represents the position of one particle.
    The wave moves to the RIGHT.
    Each particle moves UP and DOWN.
```

### Key Feature

The particle motion and the wave travel direction are **at right angles** to each other. Draw the wave traveling into the page — particles move up-down or left-right, not into the page.

### Examples of Transverse Waves

- **Light** (and all electromagnetic waves): the electric and magnetic fields oscillate perpendicular to the direction of travel.
- **Water surface waves**: water molecules move up and down while ripples travel horizontally across the surface.
- **Waves on a rope or string**: shake a rope sideways, the wave travels along the rope's length.
- **Seismic S-waves** (secondary waves): rock particles shear up-down or side-to-side as the wave travels through Earth.

## 3. Longitudinal Waves — Definition and Diagram

A **longitudinal wave** is one where the particles of the medium vibrate **parallel** to the direction the wave travels.

```
    Direction of wave travel: →  →  →  →  →  (horizontal)
    
    Direction of particle vibration: ←  →  ←  → (horizontal — parallel)
    
    
    Snapshot of longitudinal wave at one instant:
    
    ||  |  ||  |  ||  |  ||  |  ||
    ||  |  ||  |  ||  |  ||  |  ||    →  (wave travels right)
    ||  |  ||  |  ||  |  ||  |  ||
    
    ^^^    ^^^    ^^^    ^^^    ^^^
    compressed regions (compressions)
    
        ^      ^      ^      ^
        spread-out regions (rarefactions)
    
    
    Showing individual particles:
    
    . .  .   .    .   .  . .  .   .    .   .  .
    [compression][rarefaction][compression][rarefaction]
    
    In a compression, particles are closer together (high pressure).
    In a rarefaction, particles are farther apart (low pressure).
```

### Compressions and Rarefactions

In a longitudinal wave, the medium alternates between:

- **Compression**: region where particles are pushed together → higher density, higher pressure
- **Rarefaction** (also spelled rarefaction): region where particles are pulled apart → lower density, lower pressure

The wave travels as a series of compressions and rarefactions moving through the medium. The particles themselves just oscillate back and forth around their rest positions.

### Visualizing a Longitudinal Wave

A slinky spring is excellent for visualizing longitudinal waves. Push one end in and out:

```
    Slinky at rest:
    
    \/\/\/\/\/\/\/\/\/\/\/\/\/
    
    Slinky with longitudinal wave:
    
    \/\//\/\/\  \/\//\/\/\  \/\//\/\/\
         ^^^                ^^^
    compression           compression
         
           ^^^                ^^^
        rarefaction          rarefaction
    
    The compressed/stretched pattern moves along the slinky.
    Each coil just moves left and right.
```

### Examples of Longitudinal Waves

- **Sound**: air molecules compress and expand as sound travels. You will hear this in detail in Chapter 34.
- **Seismic P-waves** (primary waves): rocks compress and expand as the wave passes through Earth.
- **Ultrasound** used in medical imaging.
- **Compression waves** in any elastic material (metal rods, liquids).

    Particle motion: ↑↓                Particle motion: ← →
    
      /\/\/\                            ||||  |  ||||  |  ||||
     /      \   →                       → (compressions travel right)

    Crests and troughs visible          Compressions and rarefactions

## 5. Mechanical Waves — Need a Medium

A **mechanical wave** requires a physical medium (matter) to travel through. The wave is a disturbance in that matter — the particles must push or pull on each other for the energy to propagate.

Without matter, there is nothing to push, nothing to compress, nothing to oscillate.

### Examples of Mechanical Waves

- **Sound**: needs air, water, wood, or any matter. No air → no sound. This is why space is silent.
- **Ocean waves**: need water.
- **Seismic waves**: need rock and earth.
- **Waves on a string**: need the string.

### The Classic Demonstration

```
    Bell ringing in a vacuum jar:
    
    [Bell]  ← inside glass jar
    ~ring~
    
    When jar is full of air:    You HEAR the bell clearly.
    
    When air is pumped out:     Bell keeps vibrating, but
                                you hear NOTHING — no medium!
    
    Sound cannot travel without matter.
```

### Wave Speed in Mechanical Waves

For mechanical waves, speed depends on:
1. **Stiffness of the medium** (resistance to compression/shearing): stiffer → faster
2. **Density of the medium**: denser → slower

We will see the consequences of this in Section 9.

## 6. Electromagnetic Waves — No Medium Needed

**Electromagnetic (EM) waves** are fundamentally different from mechanical waves. They are disturbances in the **electromagnetic field** — not in any material medium.

EM waves can travel through the vacuum of empty space. They do not need matter to propagate. This is how sunlight reaches Earth through ~150 million kilometers of empty space.

### The Electromagnetic Spectrum

All EM waves travel at the same speed in a vacuum:

```
    c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s  (the speed of light)
```

They differ only in frequency (and therefore wavelength):

```
    Low frequency                              High frequency
    Long wavelength                            Short wavelength
    Low energy per photon                      High energy per photon
    
    Radio → Microwave → Infrared → Visible → UV → X-rays → Gamma rays
    
    |              |             |         |       |         |
    km scale      cm scale      mm–μm     400-700  10 nm    <0.01 nm
                                           nm
    
    FM radio (100 MHz): λ ≈ 3 m
    Microwave oven (2.45 GHz): λ ≈ 12 cm
    Red light (430 THz): λ ≈ 700 nm
    X-ray (30 PHz): λ ≈ 0.01 nm
```

All EM waves are **transverse**: the electric field and magnetic field both oscillate perpendicular to the direction of travel, and perpendicular to each other:

```
    Electric field E: oscillates up-down
    Magnetic field B: oscillates left-right
    Wave travels:     into the page  →
    
         E
         ↑
         |
    -----+-----> B
         |
         
    Wave travels perpendicular to both E and B.
```

## 7. Water Waves — A Hybrid

Water waves seen at the surface are neither purely transverse nor purely longitudinal. They are a **combination** of both.

Individual water molecules move in **circular or elliptical paths** as a wave passes:

```
    Water surface wave cross-section:
    
              crest
           **  *  **
          *  **   ** *
    ------*---------*------ surface
    
    Particle paths below the surface:
    
    O   O   O   O   O   O
     ↗↘ ↗↘ ↗↘ ↗↘ ↗↘ ↗↘
    
    Each water molecule moves in a small circle.
    The circular motion decreases with depth.
```

- At the crest: the water molecule moves in the same direction as the wave (forward component = longitudinal)
- At the trough: the water molecule moves backward (against wave direction)
- At the sides: the molecule moves up or down (transverse component)

This is why water waves are called **surface waves** or **elliptical waves** — they are a combination that only exists at the interface between water and air.

## 8. Seismic Waves and Earth's Interior

Earthquakes generate waves that travel through the Earth. By studying these seismic waves, geologists have mapped Earth's interior structure without ever drilling more than a few kilometers.

### Types of Seismic Waves

**P-waves (Primary waves)**:
- Longitudinal (compression) waves
- Travel through solid and liquid rock
- Travel faster (~6–8 km/s in crust)
- Arrive at seismometers first ("primary")

**S-waves (Secondary waves)**:
- Transverse (shear) waves
- Travel through solid rock ONLY — cannot travel through liquids
- Travel slower (~3–4 km/s in crust)
- Arrive at seismometers second ("secondary")

### How We Know Earth Has a Liquid Outer Core

This is one of the great detective stories of science:

```
    Surface
    ||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||
         \          |           /      Earthquake epicenter
          \         |          /
           \        |         /       P-waves reach everywhere
            \       |        /
             \      |       /         S-waves disappear in a
              \     |      /          "shadow zone" on the
               \    |     /           opposite side of Earth
                ====|====             ← outer core boundary
                LIQUID OUTER CORE
                ====|====
    
    S-waves cannot travel through liquid. The shadow zone on the
    far side of Earth from an earthquake proves the outer core is liquid.
```

P-waves can travel through the liquid outer core but are bent (refracted) when entering and leaving it. S-waves are completely stopped by the liquid outer core — seismometers on the far side of Earth from an earthquake record P-waves but no S-waves. This "S-wave shadow zone" proves the outer core is liquid.

The solid inner core was detected because P-waves are bent at its boundary, leaving a small shadow region even for P-waves. Scientists calculated the inner core must be solid iron-nickel at extreme pressure.

```
    Earth cross-section:
    
            Crust (0-35 km)
           /
    ------/-------------------------------------------
         /   Upper Mantle (solid rock)
        /
    ---/---------------------------------------------
      /     Lower Mantle (denser solid rock)
     /
    /-----S-wave boundary (no S-waves below)------
    |      Outer Core (LIQUID iron-nickel, ~2,300 km thick)
    |-----P-wave inner boundary----------------------
    |      Inner Core (SOLID iron-nickel, ~1,220 km radius)
    |      hottest part of Earth: ~5,000-6,000°C
```

## 9. Wave Speed in Different Media

### Sound in Different States of Matter

Sound travels faster in denser, more rigid materials, because molecules are closer together and can pass the compression along faster:

```
    Sound speed comparison:
    
    Gas (air at 20°C):      ~343 m/s
    Liquid (water):         ~1,480 m/s
    Solid (steel):          ~5,960 m/s
    
    Ratio: steel/air ≈ 17×
    
    Ordering:   gas  <  liquid  <  solid
    
    Why faster in solids?
    - Molecules are closer together
    - Bonds between molecules are much stiffer
    - Compression passes along the chain much more quickly
```

Think of it this way: in a gas, molecules are far apart and collide infrequently. In a solid, atoms are directly bonded to neighbors and a push is transmitted almost instantly.

### Practical Consequence: You Can Hear a Train Coming

Put your ear to a steel rail track. You hear the train through the steel long before you hear it through the air, because sound in steel is ~17 times faster.

Indigenous peoples and experienced travelers historically used this technique. The first to codalize it was probably railroad workers.

### Light in Different Media

Light slows down when passing through transparent materials:

```
    Speed of light comparison:
    
    Vacuum:         3.000 × 10⁸ m/s  (exact, by definition)
    Air:            2.999 × 10⁸ m/s  (almost same as vacuum)
    Water:          2.255 × 10⁸ m/s  (about 75% of vacuum speed)
    Glass:          ~2.000 × 10⁸ m/s (about 67% of vacuum speed)
    Diamond:        ~1.240 × 10⁸ m/s (about 41% of vacuum speed)
    
    Refractive index n = c_vacuum / c_medium
    Diamond: n = 3.00/1.24 ≈ 2.42  (high n → big bending → sparkle!)
```

Light slowing down in a medium is why it refracts (bends). The higher the refractive index, the more it slows and the more it bends.

## 10. Polarization — Transverse Waves Only

**Polarization** is a property unique to **transverse waves**. It refers to the direction in which the medium vibrates.

For a transverse wave traveling horizontally (say, along the x-axis), the particles can vibrate:
- Up-down (vertical plane): **vertically polarized**
- Left-right (horizontal plane): **horizontally polarized**
- At any angle in between: at that angle

```
    Transverse wave on a rope — vertical polarization:
    
           ↑ ↓ ↑ ↓
    rope: ---/\/\/\---  →  (wave travels right)
    
    Transverse wave on a rope — horizontal polarization:
    
    rope: ---/\/\/\---  →  (wave travels right)
           ← → ← →
    
    Both travel in the same direction, but particle vibration
    is in different planes.
```

### Polarizing Filters

A **polarizing filter** only allows vibrations in one specific plane to pass through. Light from the sun is **unpolarized** — it vibrates in all planes simultaneously (random orientations). After passing through a polarizing filter, it vibrates in only one plane.

```
    Unpolarized light:     Polarizing filter:     Polarized light:
    
       |  /  -  \  |           | | |               |  |  |
       | / \ / \ |        →    | | |          →    |  |  |
       |/   X   \|             | | |               |  |  |
       |\   X   /|             (only vertical
       | \ / \ / |              passes through)
       |  \  -  /  |
    
    Vertical polarizing filter blocks horizontal oscillations.
```

### Applications of Polarization

- **Polaroid sunglasses**: sunlight reflected off water is horizontally polarized. Polaroid glasses have vertical filters that block this reflected glare.
- **LCD screens**: liquid crystal displays use two perpendicular polarizing filters with liquid crystals between them. Applying a voltage rotates the polarization to let light through (pixel on) or block it (pixel off).
- **3D cinema**: left and right eye glasses have different polarization orientations. Two projectors show slightly different images with different polarizations. Each eye sees only its image → 3D effect.
- **Stress analysis in engineering**: transparent plastic under stress rotates polarized light. When viewed through a polarizing filter, stressed regions show colorful patterns, revealing where a structure is under load.

### Why Longitudinal Waves Cannot Be Polarized

In a longitudinal wave, particles vibrate parallel to the direction of travel. There is only one possible direction — along the wave's path. There is no "vertical" or "horizontal" option. You cannot polarize a sound wave because the vibration is already locked to one axis (the propagation direction).

## 11. Energy and Amplitude

The energy carried by a wave is related to its amplitude:

```
    E ∝ A²
    
    (Energy is proportional to the square of the amplitude)
```

This applies to all waves — mechanical and electromagnetic.

### What This Means Practically

- Double the amplitude → 4× the energy (2² = 4)
- Triple the amplitude → 9× the energy (3² = 9)
- Half the amplitude → 1/4 the energy (0.5² = 0.25)

```
    Small amplitude wave:           Large amplitude wave:
    
       /\/\                            /\      /\
      /    \                          /  \    /  \
    --      --                      --    \  /    --
                                           \/
    
    Low energy                      4× higher amplitude = 16× more energy
```

For sound: a sound wave with twice the pressure amplitude has four times the intensity (energy per second per area).

For light: a light wave with double the electric field amplitude carries four times the power per unit area (intensity).

## 12. Intensity and the Inverse-Square Law

**Intensity** (I) is the power (energy per second) carried by a wave per unit area perpendicular to the wave's direction of travel:

```
    I = P / A
    
    (I in W/m², P in watts, A in m² — area, not amplitude!)
```

### The Inverse-Square Law

When a wave spreads out from a point source in three dimensions, it spreads its energy over an ever-larger sphere. The surface area of a sphere is 4πr². So:

```
    I = P / (4π r²)
    
    I ∝ 1 / r²
    
    (Intensity decreases as the inverse square of the distance)
```

```
    Point source at center:
    
                .
               ...
              .   .
             .     .
            .   *   .       ← all the energy passes through here
           .         .      (sphere of radius r)
          . . . . . . .
         .           .
        .      *      .     ← same energy, but sphere now 2r
       .               .    → area is 4× bigger
      .                 .   → intensity is 4× smaller
      . . . . . . . . . .
```

**If you double the distance from a source, the intensity drops to 1/4.**
**If you triple the distance, intensity drops to 1/9.**

### Examples

- A light bulb: stand 1 m away vs. 2 m away — the brightness (intensity) is 4× less at 2 m.
- Sound: standing 10 m from a speaker vs. 20 m — the sound intensity (not loudness in dB, but the physical intensity) is 4× less.
- Radiation from a radioactive source: shield yourself by increasing distance, and the intensity falls fast.

The inverse-square law applies to any wave spreading out in 3D from a point source: light, sound, gravity, electrostatic force, and more.

```
    Distance: r      2r       3r       4r
    Intensity: I     I/4      I/9      I/16
    
    Falling fast! By 10× the distance, intensity is 100× weaker.
```

## 13. Summary

- **Transverse waves**: particle motion perpendicular to wave direction. Examples: light, water surface waves, seismic S-waves, string waves.

- **Longitudinal waves**: particle motion parallel to wave direction. Features compressions and rarefactions. Examples: sound, seismic P-waves.

- **Mechanical waves** need a medium (matter) to travel. Sound cannot travel through a vacuum.

- **Electromagnetic waves** need no medium. All EM waves travel at c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s in a vacuum. The EM spectrum ranges from radio waves to gamma rays.

- **Water waves** are a hybrid — particles move in circles, combining transverse and longitudinal motion.

- **Seismic waves**: P-waves (longitudinal) travel through solid and liquid. S-waves (transverse) travel through solid only. The S-wave shadow zone proves Earth has a liquid outer core.

- **Wave speed** is faster in stiffer, denser materials for mechanical waves. Sound travels ~343 m/s in air, ~1,480 m/s in water, ~5,960 m/s in steel.

- **Polarization** is a property of transverse waves only. A polarizing filter allows vibrations in only one plane to pass. Longitudinal waves cannot be polarized.

- **Energy ∝ A²**: doubling the amplitude quadruples the energy.

- **Inverse-square law**: intensity ∝ 1/r². Doubling distance from a point source reduces intensity by a factor of 4.

## 14. Key Equations

```
Wave speed (general):
    v = f × λ
    (v in m/s, f in Hz, λ in m)

Speed of light in vacuum:
    c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s

Intensity:
    I = P / A_area
    (I in W/m², P in W, A_area in m²)

Inverse-square law:
    I ∝ 1 / r²
    
    I₁ / I₂ = r₂² / r₁²

Energy and amplitude:
    E ∝ A²
    I ∝ A²

Refractive index (preview):
    n = c / v_medium
    (speed of light in medium = c/n)

Seismic wave speeds (approximate):
    P-waves in crust: ~6-8 km/s
    S-waves in crust: ~3-4 km/s
    
Sound in various media:
    Air (20°C): 343 m/s
    Water: 1,480 m/s
    Steel: 5,960 m/s
```
