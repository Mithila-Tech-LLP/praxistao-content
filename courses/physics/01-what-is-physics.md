## 1. What Is Physics?

Look around you right now. Your chair is holding you up. Light is entering your eyes. Your phone is sitting on a table, attracted downward by the pull of the Earth. The air in the room is warm. Somewhere outside, the Sun is shining — a ball of gas that has been burning for 4.6 billion years. Every single one of these things — the chair, the light, gravity, warmth, the Sun — is explained by one subject: **physics**.

**Physics** is the study of the most fundamental rules of the universe. More specifically, it is the science that studies **matter**, **energy**, **space**, and **time** — and the ways they interact with each other.

That sentence might sound abstract, so let us break it down word by word.

### The Four Pillars of Physics

**Matter** is anything that has mass and takes up space. Your body is matter. The air you breathe is matter. The water you drink is matter. Even the tiny electrons zipping through a wire are matter. If it exists as a physical "thing," it is matter. The study of matter asks questions like: What is it made of? How heavy is it? How does it move when you push it?

**Energy** is the ability to cause change — to make something happen. When you kick a ball, you transfer energy from your foot to the ball and it moves. When you feel warm sunlight on your skin, the Sun has sent energy to you in the form of light. When a battery powers a flashlight, stored chemical energy converts into light and heat energy. Energy cannot be created from nothing, and it never disappears — it only transforms from one form to another. This is one of the most profound facts in all of science.

**Space** is the three-dimensional "stage" on which everything exists. It is the room you move through. Ordinary space has three directions: left/right, forward/backward, and up/down. Physics asks: how does space itself behave? Does it warp or bend? (It does — more on that when we meet Einstein.)

**Time** is the dimension that moves forward, from the past through the present toward the future. Physics asks deep questions about time: Is it constant everywhere, or does it flow differently in different places? (It flows differently — this is Einstein's great discovery.) Can time have a beginning?

### The Word "Physics" — Where It Comes From

The word **physics** comes from the ancient Greek word *physis* (φύσις), which means "nature." Ancient Greek thinkers — people like Aristotle, Archimedes, and Democritus — were the first people we know of who tried to explain nature using reason and observation rather than mythology. They asked: Why do things fall? What are objects made of? Why does the Moon rise and set? Physics, in this sense, is humanity's oldest scientific project.

For most of history, physics was simply called "natural philosophy" — the philosophy of the natural world. It was only in the 1800s that the word "physicist" came into common use to describe someone who studies physics professionally.

### Why Is Physics Called the "Fundamental" Science?

You will sometimes hear people call physics the most fundamental of all the sciences. This is not arrogance — it is a real observation about how the sciences relate to each other.

Chemistry is the science of how atoms combine to make molecules and materials. But why do atoms behave the way they do? The answer is physics — specifically quantum physics, which governs atoms.

Biology is the science of living things. But life depends on chemistry — on the way molecules interact inside cells. And chemistry depends on physics. So at the deepest level, physics underlies biology too.

Engineering is the application of science to building things. Civil engineers design bridges using physics (forces, materials). Electrical engineers design circuits using physics (electromagnetism). Mechanical engineers design engines using physics (thermodynamics).

This is what "fundamental" means: not that physics is more important than other sciences in everyday life, but that its laws are the foundation upon which everything else is built.

> **Note on Scale:** Physics operates at every scale imaginable — from the interior of a single proton (a size of about 0.000 000 000 000 001 meters) all the way up to the observable universe (a size of about 880 000 000 000 000 000 000 000 000 meters across). The same basic rules — conservation of energy, the laws of motion, gravity — apply at both extremes.

Physics is a vast subject, so over centuries it has been divided into branches — each branch focusing on a particular aspect of nature. Think of it like a university: the university teaches many subjects, but they are all connected under one roof. ### Classical Mechanics

**Classical mechanics** is the oldest and most intuitive branch of physics. It studies how objects move when forces act on them. A football arcing through the air, a car braking on a highway, a pendulum swinging — all of these are problems in classical mechanics. The rules of classical mechanics were mostly worked out by Isaac Newton in the 1600s, which is why they are also called **Newtonian mechanics**. These rules work perfectly for everyday objects — things you can see and touch. (They break down at very high speeds and very small scales, where Einstein and quantum physics take over.)

### Thermodynamics

**Thermodynamics** (from Greek: *therme* = heat, *dynamis* = power) studies heat, temperature, and how energy flows between objects. It explains why a hot cup of tea cools down, how a car engine converts burning fuel into motion, and why you cannot build a perpetual motion machine. One of its central ideas — that disorder in the universe always increases — is among the most profound and slightly unsettling facts in all of science.

### Optics

**Optics** is the study of light and how it behaves. It explains why lenses in your glasses or camera can focus an image, why rainbows form, why things look different colors, and how a laser works. Optics has deep connections to electromagnetism (because light is an electromagnetic wave) and to quantum physics (because light also behaves like a stream of particles called photons).

### Electromagnetism

**Electromagnetism** studies electric charges, magnetic fields, and the relationship between them. This branch explains how electric power works, how your phone's screen emits light, how radio waves carry music to your car, and why a compass always points north. James Clerk Maxwell, a Scottish physicist, unified electricity and magnetism into one theory in 1865 — one of the greatest achievements in the history of science.

### Quantum Physics

**Quantum physics** (also called quantum mechanics) describes how matter and energy behave at the very smallest scales — the scale of atoms and the particles inside them. At this scale, the rules are deeply strange. Particles can behave like waves. An electron can be in two places at once until you measure it. Energy comes in tiny, discrete "packets" called **quanta** (singular: **quantum**) rather than flowing continuously. Quantum physics is the most precisely tested theory in all of science, and it underlies all of chemistry, all of electronics, and all of modern technology.

### Nuclear Physics

**Nuclear physics** studies the nucleus — the tiny, dense core at the center of every atom. It explains radioactivity (why some atoms are unstable and break apart), nuclear fission (splitting a heavy nucleus to release enormous energy, as in a nuclear power plant), and nuclear fusion (joining two light nuclei to release even more energy, as in the Sun and in hydrogen bombs). Nuclear medicine uses radioactive isotopes to image and treat cancer.

### Astrophysics

**Astrophysics** applies all branches of physics to understand objects beyond Earth — the Sun, planets, stars, black holes, galaxies, and the universe itself. It uses spectroscopy (optics) to identify what stars are made of, nuclear physics to explain how stars shine, and general relativity (Einstein's theory of gravity) to understand black holes and the Big Bang.

## 3. Why Physics Matters

You might be wondering: why should I learn physics? I just want to use my phone. Fair enough. But here is the remarkable thing: your phone only exists because of physics. So does GPS. So does the internet, the MRI machine that scans for tumors, the nuclear reactor generating electricity in some countries, and the fiber-optic cable carrying data at the speed of light. Let us look at each one.

### Smartphones — Quantum Physics in Your Pocket

A modern smartphone contains a processor with billions of **transistors** — tiny switches that control the flow of electricity. The transistor was invented in 1947, and it only works because of quantum physics. Specifically, engineers use the quantum behavior of electrons in semiconductor materials (usually silicon) to build circuits that are impossibly small. The screen emits light through LEDs (light-emitting diodes), which also rely on quantum physics. The touchscreen detects your finger by measuring tiny changes in an electric field. Your phone's camera uses a sensor that converts photons (particles of light) into electrical signals using the **photoelectric effect** — the very phenomenon that won Einstein his Nobel Prize in 1921. So: quantum physics, electromagnetism, optics, and classical mechanics all live in your pocket.

### GPS — When Einstein's Relativity Becomes Practical

The GPS (Global Positioning System) in your phone works by receiving signals from satellites orbiting about 20,000 km above Earth. Your phone computes its position by measuring the tiny time delay in signals arriving from different satellites.

But here is where physics gets interesting: those satellites are moving very fast (about 14,000 km/h), and they are far from Earth's gravity. Einstein's theory of **special relativity** says that fast-moving clocks tick slightly slower. His theory of **general relativity** says that clocks in weaker gravity tick slightly faster. These two effects partially cancel, but the net result is that satellite clocks tick about 38 microseconds per day faster than clocks on Earth's surface.

That sounds tiny. But GPS depends on timing precise to about 20–30 nanoseconds. If engineers did not correct for Einstein's effects, GPS systems would drift by about 10 km per day. Every navigation app would be useless without Einstein's relativity.

> **Worked Example 1.1 — GPS Time Correction**
>
> **Problem:** A GPS satellite clock gains 38 microseconds per day compared to a clock on Earth. How far off would your GPS position be after one day if this were not corrected?
>
> **Given:**
> - Time error per day: 38 microseconds = 38 × 10⁻⁶ seconds
> - Speed of light (speed of GPS signal): c = 300,000 km/s = 3 × 10⁸ m/s
>
> **Solution:**
> Distance error = speed × time error
> Distance error = (3 × 10⁸ m/s) × (38 × 10⁻⁶ s)
> Distance error = 3 × 10⁸ × 3.8 × 10⁻⁵
> **Distance error = 11,400 meters = about 11.4 km**
>
> **Conclusion:** Without Einstein's relativistic corrections, your GPS would be off by over 11 km every single day. Your map app would place you in a completely wrong part of the city.

### MRI Machines — Nuclear Physics Saves Lives

MRI stands for **Magnetic Resonance Imaging**. It uses a phenomenon called **nuclear magnetic resonance**: the nuclei of hydrogen atoms (which are just single protons) behave like tiny bar magnets. When you place a patient inside a powerful magnetic field, these proton-magnets line up with the field. The machine then pulses radio waves at precisely the right frequency to knock these protons out of alignment. When they snap back into place, they emit a detectable radio signal. A computer reconstructs these signals into an image of the body's soft tissue — brain, muscles, organs — without using any radiation. Without a deep understanding of nuclear physics and electromagnetism, MRI would be impossible.

### Nuclear Power — Einstein's Most Famous Equation at Work

Nuclear power plants generate electricity by splitting uranium atoms in a process called **fission**. When a uranium nucleus splits, it releases an enormous amount of energy. How much energy? It is described by Einstein's famous equation: **E = m × c²**. In this equation, E is energy, m is mass, and c is the speed of light (about 300,000 km per second). Because c is so large, even a tiny amount of mass converts into a tremendous amount of energy. One kilogram of uranium fuel contains roughly the same energy as 3,000 tonnes of coal — all from a small piece of metal you could hold in your hand.

### The Internet — Light Traveling Through Glass

Much of the internet's backbone consists of **fiber-optic cables** — thin strands of ultra-pure glass that carry information as pulses of light. The light stays inside the glass fiber through a phenomenon called **total internal reflection**: when light traveling through glass hits the edge of the glass at a shallow enough angle, it bounces back inside rather than escaping. This is a consequence of optics — specifically, Snell's law, which describes how light bends when passing between materials. A single fiber-optic cable the thickness of a human hair can carry hundreds of thousands of simultaneous phone calls. The entire global internet depends on this physics.

## 4. The Scientific Method

Physics is not just a collection of facts and formulas. It is a way of asking questions about nature and finding trustworthy answers. This method of investigation is called the **scientific method**. It is the most powerful tool humanity has ever developed for understanding the world.

The scientific method is a cycle — not a one-time process. Scientists go through it over and over, refining their understanding each time:

### Step 1: Observe

Science begins with careful observation. You notice something in the world that needs explaining. Galileo noticed that a chandelier in a cathedral seemed to swing back and forth in equal time intervals regardless of how wide the swing was. Newton (according to legend) noticed an apple fall from a tree. These small observations led to enormous discoveries.

A good observation is precise. It answers: *What exactly did I see? When? Under what conditions? How could I measure it?*

### Step 2: Form a Hypothesis

A **hypothesis** is a testable explanation for your observation. The key word is *testable*. "There are invisible magic forces at work" is not a hypothesis — it cannot be tested. "Heavier objects fall faster than lighter ones" is a hypothesis — it can be tested by dropping objects and timing them.

A hypothesis is not a wild guess. It is an educated guess, based on what you already know, that makes a specific, falsifiable prediction about what you would observe under certain conditions.

### Step 3: Experiment

A good experiment changes *only one variable at a time* while keeping everything else the same. This allows you to be sure that any change you observe was caused by the thing you changed, not by something else accidentally. The variable you deliberately change is called the **independent variable**. The variable you measure to see what changes is called the **dependent variable**. Everything else you keep constant is a **controlled variable**.

> **Worked Example 1.2 — Galileo's Falling Objects**
>
> **The Old Belief:** Aristotle (ancient Greece) said heavier objects fall faster than light ones. This seemed to make sense — a rock falls faster than a feather, right?
>
> **Observation:** Galileo noticed that objects seemed to fall at similar rates when he compared a cannonball and a musket ball from a height.
>
> **Hypothesis:** All objects (ignoring air resistance) fall at the same rate, regardless of their mass.
>
> **Experiment:** According to legend, Galileo dropped two balls of different masses from the Tower of Pisa simultaneously and observed that they hit the ground at the same time.
>
> - **Independent variable:** Mass of the ball (heavy vs. light)
> - **Dependent variable:** Time to hit the ground
> - **Controlled variables:** Drop height, starting conditions, timing method
>
> **Result:** Both balls hit the ground at the same moment.
>
> **Conclusion:** Galileo's hypothesis was supported. Aristotle's 2,000-year-old belief was wrong. (The feather falls slowly only because air resistance acts more strongly on it relative to its weight — not because it is lighter.) In a vacuum, a feather and a hammer fall at exactly the same rate. This was dramatically demonstrated by astronaut David Scott on the Moon in 1971, where there is no air. He dropped a hammer and a falcon feather simultaneously — they hit the Moon's surface at the exact same moment.

### Step 4: Analyze

After the experiment, you look carefully at the data you collected. You look for patterns. You calculate. You ask: Is this what I expected? How large is the effect? Could random variation explain my result, or is the effect real? This is where mathematics becomes essential — it lets you describe patterns precisely and check whether results are significant.

### Step 5: Conclude

You compare your results to your hypothesis. If the experiment strongly supports the hypothesis — and other scientists can repeat it and get the same result — the hypothesis gains credibility. If the experiment contradicts the hypothesis, you must revise or discard it and form a new one.

A very well-tested hypothesis, supported by many experiments, can become a **scientific theory**. In everyday English, "theory" means a guess. In science, it means the opposite: a robust, well-evidenced explanation that has survived repeated attempts to disprove it. Newton's theory of gravity, Darwin's theory of evolution, Einstein's theory of relativity — these are theories not because they are uncertain, but because they are the most thoroughly tested explanations we have.

> **Key Idea:** In science, you can never "prove" a theory completely true — you can only fail to prove it false. Every theory in physics remains open to revision if new evidence contradicts it. This is what makes science self-correcting, and more trustworthy over time than any authority or tradition.

## 5. Physics in Everyday Life

Physics is not something that only happens in laboratories. It is happening right now, in your kitchen, your bedroom, outside your window, inside your body. Here are five everyday phenomena you have personally experienced, each explained by physics.

### Why Ice Floats on Water

If you have ever put ice cubes in a drink, you have noticed something that is actually quite remarkable: ice floats. Most solids are denser than their liquid form and sink. If ice sank to the bottom of lakes and oceans, the water above it would freeze, then that new ice would sink, and eventually entire bodies of water would freeze solid from the bottom up — killing all aquatic life. The fact that ice floats is one of the reasons life exists on Earth.

Why does ice float? It comes down to **density**. Density is how much mass is packed into a given volume. Objects that are less dense than the liquid they are in will float.

Liquid water molecules (H₂O) are packed closely together and move around freely. When water freezes, the molecules lock into a crystal structure — and in doing so, they actually push slightly apart, forming a lattice with more space between them. This means ice has a slightly lower density than liquid water, so it floats.

### Why the Sky Is Blue

Look up on a clear day. The sky is blue. But why? There is no blue paint up there. The Sun emits white light — a mixture of all colors.

The answer lies in how light interacts with the atmosphere. Sunlight contains light of all colors (wavelengths). When sunlight enters the atmosphere, it collides with tiny nitrogen and oxygen molecules. These collisions scatter light in all directions. The key is that shorter-wavelength light (blue and violet) scatters far more easily than longer-wavelength light (red, orange, yellow). This is called **Rayleigh scattering**, named after British physicist Lord Rayleigh.

Because blue light is scattered in every direction all across the sky, when you look at any part of the sky (except directly at the Sun), you see scattered blue light. The sky appears blue from every direction.

(You might wonder: violet light has an even shorter wavelength than blue — why isn't the sky violet? Partly because there is less violet light in sunlight to begin with, and partly because our eyes are much more sensitive to blue than violet.)

At sunset, the Sun is near the horizon, so its light travels through much more atmosphere to reach your eyes. All the blue has been scattered away long before it reaches you. Only the longer-wavelength reds and oranges survive the journey — so the sky turns red and gold at sunset.

### How a Microwave Oven Heats Food

A microwave oven does not heat food the way a conventional oven does (by heating the air around the food). Instead, it uses **electromagnetic waves** at a specific frequency — about 2.45 gigahertz, which is in the microwave part of the electromagnetic spectrum.

Water molecules are **polar** — they have a slightly positive end and a slightly negative end. When a rapidly oscillating electric field (the microwaves) passes through the food, it causes the water molecules to rapidly flip back and forth to align with the alternating field — about 4.9 billion times per second. All this jostling is kinetic energy (the energy of movement), and kinetic energy of molecules is what we call heat. So the microwaves cause the water in your food to vibrate intensely, and that vibration heats the food from inside outward.

This is why dry food (like crackers or dry bread) does not heat well in a microwave — there is little water for the microwaves to act on. And why cold food with ice crystals heats unevenly — the water molecules in ice are locked in a rigid lattice and cannot rotate as freely.

### Why You Feel Pushed Back When a Car Accelerates

When a car accelerates rapidly from a stoplight, you feel pushed back into your seat. But nothing is actually pushing you backward — that sensation is a consequence of **inertia**, which is a property of all matter.

**Inertia** is the tendency of an object to resist changes in its motion. An object at rest wants to stay at rest. An object moving at a steady speed wants to keep moving at that speed. This is Newton's First Law, which we will study in detail in Chapter 03.

When the car accelerates forward, your body (due to inertia) wants to remain where it was — in place, not moving. But the car seat pushes you forward. From your perspective inside the car, this feels like being pushed backward. It is not a real backward force — it is just your body resisting the forward acceleration.

### Why Mirrors Reflect Your Face

A mirror works because of **specular reflection** — when light hits a very smooth surface, it bounces off at the same angle it arrived. If light from your face arrives at 45° to the mirror's surface, it bounces away at 45° on the other side. Because the reflected light preserves the pattern of light and dark from your face, your brain interprets it as an image of your face on the other side of the mirror.

Rough surfaces (like a wall) also reflect light, but they scatter it in many random directions. This is called **diffuse reflection**. No coherent image forms. That is why you can see yourself in a mirror but not in a painted wall, even though both reflect light.

```
     SPECULAR REFLECTION (mirror)      DIFFUSE REFLECTION (wall)

         Incoming light                   Incoming light
            \  |  /                          \  |  /
             \ | /                            \ | /
    ----------+----------             --------/\/\/\--------
    smooth mirror surface             rough/bumpy surface
         /  |  \                       / | \   |  / \
        /   |   \                    /  |  \  |  /   \
       Light bounces away           Light scatters in
       in ordered angles.           random directions.
       You see an image.            You see no image.
```

## 6. Famous Physicists and Their Discoveries

Physics is built on the shoulders of giants — people who looked at the world with intense curiosity and changed humanity's understanding of it forever. Here are five physicists whose work you will encounter throughout this course.

### Isaac Newton (1643–1727) — English

**Key discoveries:** Laws of motion, universal gravitation, calculus (co-invented), optics.

Newton is widely considered the most influential scientist who ever lived. His three laws of motion — which describe how objects behave when forces act on them — form the foundation of classical mechanics and remain accurate for everything from bicycle wheels to planetary orbits.

**The "aha" moment:** The famous apple story (probably exaggerated, but based on a real event). Newton wondered: the same force that pulls an apple down from a tree — could it be the same force that holds the Moon in orbit around Earth? He worked out the mathematics and showed that yes, gravity is universal. Every object in the universe attracts every other object. The force between the apple and the Earth is the same kind of force as the force between the Earth and the Moon. This was a staggering leap — connecting the ordinary world to the cosmos.

**Famous quote:** "If I have seen further, it is by standing on the shoulders of giants."

---

### Michael Faraday (1791–1867) — English

**Key discoveries:** Electromagnetic induction, the electric motor principle, the concept of electric and magnetic fields.

Faraday grew up poor, the son of a blacksmith, and had almost no formal education. He started his scientific career washing bottles in a laboratory. By the end of his life, he had discovered the fundamental principle that makes electric power possible: when a magnetic field changes near a wire, it creates an electric current in the wire. This is called **electromagnetic induction**. Every electric generator — from the tiny dynamo in a bicycle light to the enormous turbines in a power station — works on this principle discovered by a self-taught bookbinder's apprentice.

**The "aha" moment:** In 1831, Faraday wrapped two coils of wire around an iron ring. When he connected one coil to a battery, a current briefly appeared in the other coil — even though there was no direct connection. He realized that the changing magnetic field created by the first coil was inducing a current in the second. He spent the next decade mapping this invisible force.

---

### James Clerk Maxwell (1831–1879) — Scottish

**Key discoveries:** Unified theory of electromagnetism, prediction that light is an electromagnetic wave, kinetic theory of gases.

Maxwell is often rated second only to Newton in the history of physics. He took Faraday's experimental discoveries about electricity and magnetism and compressed them into four elegant equations — now called **Maxwell's Equations** — that completely describe all of classical electromagnetism. From these equations, he derived that electromagnetic disturbances travel through space at the speed of light, and concluded that light itself is an electromagnetic wave.

**The "aha" moment:** Solving his own equations, Maxwell calculated the speed at which electromagnetic waves should travel. The answer came out to be approximately 310,000 km/s — almost exactly the measured speed of light. He wrote: "We can scarcely avoid the conclusion that light consists in the transverse undulations of the same medium which is the cause of electric and magnetic phenomena." Light and electromagnetism are the same thing. This moment of recognition — that two apparently separate phenomena are one — is one of the greatest unifications in science.

---

### Albert Einstein (1879–1955) — German-Swiss-American

**Key discoveries:** Special relativity, general relativity (new theory of gravity), explanation of the photoelectric effect, E = mc².

Einstein is the most famous scientist in history. In 1905 — his "miracle year" — while working as a patent clerk, he published four papers that each would have made a lesser scientist's career. One explained the photoelectric effect using the idea that light comes in discrete packets called quanta. One introduced special relativity, showing that the speed of light is always the same to every observer and that time and space are intertwined. And one derived E = mc².

**The "aha" moment:** Einstein imagined himself riding alongside a beam of light at the speed of light. He asked: what would the light wave look like to me? Classical physics gave a contradictory, impossible answer. Wrestling with this contradiction for years ultimately led him to special relativity: the realization that there is no absolute time, no absolute space — only relationships between observers. Time itself slows for fast-moving objects.

---

### Niels Bohr (1885–1962) — Danish

**Key discoveries:** Quantum model of the atom, complementarity principle, contributions to quantum mechanics and nuclear fission.

By 1910, physicists knew atoms existed, but nobody understood their structure. Ernest Rutherford had just discovered that atoms have a tiny dense nucleus at their center, with electrons somehow orbiting around it. But classical physics predicted those orbiting electrons should spiral inward and crash into the nucleus within a fraction of a second. Bohr solved this problem by applying quantum ideas: electrons can only occupy specific allowed orbits, and they do not radiate energy while in those orbits. When an electron jumps from a higher orbit to a lower one, it emits a specific amount of energy as light.

**The "aha" moment:** Bohr noticed that his model of discrete electron orbits perfectly predicted the exact colors of light emitted by hydrogen atoms — a set of spectral lines that had been measured precisely but never explained. The match was perfect. The quantum structure of the atom was real.

---

### A Note on Marie Curie (1867–1934) — Polish-French

No list of famous physicists is complete without **Marie Curie**. She discovered two new elements — polonium and radium — and coined the term "radioactivity." She remains the only person in history to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences: Physics (1903) and Chemistry (1911). She did this while overcoming enormous obstacles as a woman in a male-dominated scientific world. Her work on radioactivity formed the foundation of nuclear physics and nuclear medicine.

## 7. How to Study Physics

Physics has a reputation for being hard. And it is true: you will encounter new ideas that require real effort to understand. But there is an important distinction between physics being hard and physics being impossible. Physics is learnable by anyone who is willing to think carefully and patiently. Here is how to approach it.

### Understand, Do Not Just Memorize

The most common mistake beginners make in physics is treating it like a history or geography class — lists of facts to memorize. This strategy will fail you. Physics is not about memorizing that F = ma. It is about understanding *what that equation means*: that force, mass, and acceleration are connected in a specific way, and that if you double the force on an object with the same mass, its acceleration doubles too. When you understand the idea, the formula becomes obvious and unforgettable.

Ask "why?" and "what does this mean?" constantly. If a formula or fact does not make intuitive sense to you yet, do not move on — dig in until it clicks, or ask for help.

### Draw Diagrams for Everything

Physics deals with objects moving through space, forces acting in directions, waves propagating — all inherently visual things. Always draw a diagram before trying to solve a problem. Label the forces, the velocities, the objects. Seeing the physical situation drawn out often makes the mathematics obvious. Physicists draw diagrams constantly — it is not a sign of being a beginner. It is what good physicists do.

### Master the Units

Every physical quantity has **units** — the measuring system that gives the number meaning. Speed without units is meaningless. 60 km/h means something specific. 60 m/s means something very different (much faster). When you solve a problem, always write the units of every quantity, and check that the units of your answer make sense. If you calculate a time in kilograms, something went wrong.

The standard system of units in science is the **SI system** (Système International d'Unités — the International System of Units). The most important SI units for introductory physics are:

| Quantity | SI Unit | Abbreviation | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Length | Meter | m | A door is about 2 m tall |
| Mass | Kilogram | kg | A bag of rice is 1 kg |
| Time | Second | s | A heartbeat is about 1 s |
| Temperature | Kelvin | K | Room temp is about 293 K |
| Electric current | Ampere | A | A phone charger uses 1–2 A |
| Force | Newton | N | Weight of a small apple ≈ 1 N |
| Energy | Joule | J | A bite of food ≈ 100,000 J |
| Power | Watt | W | A light bulb uses 10–60 W |
| Speed | Meters per second | m/s | Walking pace ≈ 1.4 m/s |

### Work Through Examples Step by Step

When you encounter a worked example, do not just read it passively and think "yes, that makes sense." Cover up the solution and try to solve it yourself first. If you get stuck, uncover just the next step, try again, and so on. Active struggle is how learning actually happens. The feeling of understanding a problem you previously could not solve is the best feeling in physics.

### A Systematic Problem-Solving Approach

For any physics problem, follow this structure:

1. **Read carefully.** What is given? What are you asked to find?
2. **Draw a diagram.** Sketch the situation. Label everything.
3. **Identify the relevant physics.** What branch is this? What laws or formulas apply?
4. **Set up the equation.** Write the relevant formula, substituting in what you know.
5. **Solve mathematically.** Do the algebra. Keep track of units throughout.
6. **Check your answer.** Does it have the right units? Does the size make sense? (If a car is going 5,000 m/s, something went wrong.)

### Learn From Mistakes

Getting a problem wrong is not failure — it is information. When you get something wrong, do not just look at the right answer and move on. Figure out *exactly* where your reasoning diverged from correct. Was it a conceptual misunderstanding? A unit error? An algebra mistake? Each type of error teaches you something different. The students who improve fastest in physics are the ones who are most honest with themselves about their mistakes.

> **On Mathematics:** Physics uses mathematics as its language. You do not need advanced mathematics to begin. This course requires only basic algebra — rearranging equations, substituting numbers, working with fractions and powers of 10. As we progress, we will introduce new mathematical tools as needed, always explaining the meaning behind them. The math is a tool for expressing physical ideas precisely; the physical ideas always come first.

## 8. Physics and Other Sciences

No science exists in isolation. Physics, chemistry, biology, and engineering are not separated by high walls — they are deeply connected, and some of the most exciting science happens exactly at the borders between them.

### Physics and Chemistry

Chemistry studies how atoms combine into molecules, how molecules react with each other, and what properties different materials have. But *why* do atoms bond? Why does sodium and chlorine combine to make table salt? Why does water freeze at 0°C? The answers lie in the quantum physics of electrons. Chemistry is, at its deepest level, applied quantum physics.

Thermodynamics connects physics and chemistry through the concept of **chemical energy**. When wood burns, chemical bonds in the wood break and new bonds form in CO₂ and H₂O. The difference in energy between the old bonds and the new bonds is released as heat and light. Thermodynamics predicts exactly how much energy each reaction releases.

### Physics and Biology

Living things are made of matter and run on energy — so physics is everywhere in biology.

**Biophysics** is the field at their intersection. Your nervous system works because electrical signals (governed by electromagnetism) propagate along nerve fibers. Your heart pumps blood using fluid dynamics — the study of how fluids flow under pressure. Your eyes focus light using the optical properties of the lens. The structure of DNA was discovered partly by using X-ray diffraction — a physics technique in which X-rays bounce off the atoms in a crystal to reveal its structure. Even evolution has physical constraints: an animal's size affects how much heat it can dissipate (thermodynamics) and how strong its bones need to be (mechanics).

### Physics and Engineering

If physics asks "how does the universe work?" then engineering asks "how can we use that knowledge to build useful things?" Engineering is applied physics.

- **Mechanical engineering** is built on classical mechanics: forces, stress, strain, fluid dynamics, thermodynamics of engines.
- **Electrical engineering** is built on electromagnetism: circuits, transformers, antennas, semiconductors.
- **Civil engineering** uses mechanics and materials science: how structures bear loads, how bridges distribute weight, how concrete and steel behave under stress.
- **Aerospace engineering** combines fluid dynamics (how air flows around a wing), thermodynamics (how jet engines work), and orbital mechanics (how satellites and spacecraft move).
- **Biomedical engineering** applies optics (fiber-optic endoscopes), nuclear physics (MRI, PET scanners), and fluid dynamics (artificial heart valves) to medicine.

> **Worked Example 1.3 — Physics in a Hospital**
>
> Consider the range of physics used in a modern hospital:
> - **X-rays:** high-energy electromagnetic waves (optics + quantum physics) that pass through soft tissue but are absorbed by dense bone, creating shadow images.
> - **Ultrasound:** high-frequency sound waves (classical mechanics + wave physics) that bounce off tissue boundaries; the echoes create images of organs and fetuses.
> - **MRI:** nuclear magnetic resonance (nuclear physics + electromagnetism) to image soft tissue without radiation.
> - **ECG (electrocardiogram):** measures tiny electrical signals (electromagnetism) produced by the heart's muscle cells.
> - **Radiation therapy:** uses gamma rays or X-rays (nuclear + quantum physics) to destroy cancer cells.
> - **Laser surgery:** uses intense, focused light (optics + quantum physics) to cut or reshape tissue precisely.
>
> Every single diagnostic and treatment tool in that list is a direct application of physics. This is why physics is sometimes described as the foundation of modern medicine.

## 9. Summary

- **Physics** is the study of matter, energy, space, and time — the most fundamental science, because its laws underlie all other natural sciences.
- The word "physics" comes from the Greek *physis* meaning "nature." Humans have been asking physics questions for thousands of years.
- Physics is divided into major branches: **classical mechanics** (motion & forces), **thermodynamics** (heat & energy), **optics** (light), **electromagnetism** (electric & magnetic fields), **quantum physics** (atoms & subatomic particles), **nuclear physics** (atomic nuclei), and **astrophysics** (stars & the universe).
- Physics is not abstract — it is in your smartphone (quantum physics + electromagnetism), your GPS (special and general relativity), your hospital's MRI machine (nuclear physics), the power grid (electromagnetism), and the internet's fiber-optic cables (optics).
- The **scientific method** is the process of: observe → hypothesize → experiment → analyze → conclude. It is self-correcting and remains open to revision when new evidence appears.
- A **hypothesis** is a testable explanation. A **theory** in science means a well-evidenced, thoroughly-tested explanation — not a mere guess.
- Physics explains everyday phenomena: ice floats because it is less dense than liquid water; the sky is blue because of Rayleigh scattering; microwaves heat food by rotating water molecules; you feel pushed back in an accelerating car because of inertia; mirrors work through specular reflection.
- **Newton** gave us the laws of motion and universal gravitation. **Faraday** discovered electromagnetic induction. **Maxwell** unified electricity and magnetism and showed light is an electromagnetic wave. **Einstein** showed that space, time, mass, and energy are all interrelated. **Bohr** gave us the quantum model of the atom. **Curie** discovered radioactivity and two new elements.
- To study physics well: **understand, don't memorize**. Draw diagrams. Write units on every quantity. Work through problems actively. Analyze your mistakes honestly.
- Physics connects deeply to chemistry (quantum physics explains chemical bonds), biology (biophysics, nerve signals, medical imaging), and all branches of engineering.
- The SI system is the standard language of measurement in science. The key base units are the meter (m), kilogram (kg), and second (s).
