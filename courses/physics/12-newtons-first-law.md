## A 2000-Year Mistake

For roughly two thousand years — from the time of the ancient Greeks until the 1600s — humanity had the wrong idea about motion. Not slightly wrong. Fundamentally, completely, upside-down wrong.

And the frustrating part? The wrong idea felt completely logical. It matched everyday experience. It seemed obviously true. Which is exactly why it took so long to correct.

Here is the wrong idea: **objects naturally come to rest, and they need a continuous force to keep moving.**

Push a box across the floor. The moment you stop pushing, it stops. Throw a ball. It slows down and falls. Sail a ship. When the wind stops, the ship slows.

All of this seems to confirm the idea. Objects slow down and stop. That seems like their natural tendency. They seem to "want" to be at rest.

But this intuition — this apparently obvious truth — was wrong. The man who finally broke through and saw the reality was Galileo Galilei, and the ideas were formalized by Isaac Newton.

Understanding WHY the ancient idea was wrong is the first step to really grasping Newton's First Law. So let us start there.

---

## Aristotle's View — The Wrong Idea That Felt Right

**Aristotle** (384–322 BC) was one of the greatest thinkers of the ancient world. He thought carefully about motion and came to these conclusions:

1. Everything has a **natural place** — earth and water tend downward, fire and air tend upward.
2. Objects in their natural place are at rest. This is their default state.
3. Objects AWAY from their natural place are pulled back — which explains why things fall.
4. To keep an object moving (especially sideways, which is "unnatural"), you need a continuous force.
5. The moment the force stops, the motion stops.

```
Aristotle's model:

Push box →   Force applied →   Box moves
Stop pushing →  Force gone  →   Box stops

"The force causes the motion.
 No force = no motion.
 Motion requires a continuous cause."
```

This framework was accepted for about two thousand years. Smart people defended it, built on it, and used it to explain the world. It fit everyday observation — because in everyday life, friction IS present, and friction DOES stop things.

Aristotle's mistake was not noticing that friction was doing the stopping, not the absence of force. The actual experiment he needed was one without friction — and that was very hard to do in ancient Greece.

---

## Galileo's Revolution — Thinking About Ideal Surfaces

**Galileo Galilei** (1564–1642) was the person who cracked the problem. He could not eliminate friction in practice, but he could do something clever: he could *reduce* it systematically and think about what would happen if it went to zero.

### The Inclined Plane Experiment

Galileo rolled balls down a smooth inclined ramp and noticed that a ball rolling DOWN a slope sped up. A ball rolling UP a slope slowed down. What would happen if the slope were perfectly flat (horizontal)?

His reasoning:
- On a downward slope: the ball speeds up (some downward pull)
- On an upward slope: the ball slows down (some upward pull)
- On a flat surface: the net pull is zero... so it should neither speed up NOR slow down

But a real ball on a real flat surface does slow down and stop. Why? **Friction**. The rougher the surface, the faster it stops. A smoother surface: it goes further. A very smooth surface: it goes much further.

He extrapolated: on a perfectly smooth, frictionless surface, the ball would **never stop**. It would roll forever.

```
Rough surface:  Ball stops quickly
-------+*>
       (stops here)

Smoother surface: Ball goes further
-----------+*>
           (stops here)

Very smooth surface: Ball goes much further
-----------------------------+*>
                             (stops here)

Perfectly frictionless (imagined): Ball never stops
--------------------------------------------------->*
                                          (never stops)
```

This was a radical insight. Galileo was saying: **the natural state of motion is not rest. The natural state is constant motion (including rest as a special case of zero speed).** What is UNnatural is a *change* in motion — speeding up or slowing down. A change in motion requires a force. Constant velocity (including zero velocity) requires no force.

This flipped Aristotle completely on its head.

## Newton's First Law — The Law of Inertia

Isaac Newton (1643–1727) built on Galileo's insight and stated it precisely as his First Law of Motion:

> **"An object at rest remains at rest, and an object in motion remains in motion with constant velocity, unless acted upon by a net external force."**

Let us break this down word by word.

### "An object at rest remains at rest..."

A book sitting on a table stays put. You knew this. But now you know WHY — not because "things naturally want to be at rest" (Aristotle), but because there is no net force to change its state.

### "...and an object in motion remains in motion with constant velocity..."

A hockey puck sliding on ice, if the ice were perfectly smooth and there were no air resistance, would slide forever without slowing down. The fact that it eventually stops is because of friction and air resistance — external forces acting on it.

### "...with CONSTANT VELOCITY..."

Constant velocity means constant speed AND constant direction. So Newton's First Law covers both:
- An object moving at constant speed in a straight line requires no net force.
- Any change in speed OR direction requires a net force.

### "...unless acted upon by a NET EXTERNAL FORCE."

"Net" means the vector sum of all forces. "External" means a force applied by something outside the object itself. The internal forces inside an object (atoms pushing on atoms) cannot cause the overall object to accelerate.

```
Newton's First Law Summary:

Zero net force → Constant velocity (no change in motion)
                 (This includes "at rest" as a special case)

Nonzero net force → Change in velocity (acceleration)
                    (Speeding up, slowing down, or turning)
```

## What Is Inertia?

**Inertia** is the property of matter that causes it to resist changes in its state of motion.

Think of it as "stubbornness." An object does not want to start moving (if it is at rest). And it does not want to stop (if it is moving). It "wants" to keep doing what it is already doing.

The amount of inertia an object has is directly related to its **mass**. More mass = more inertia = more resistance to change.

### Everyday Experience of Inertia

Push a bicycle from rest: easy.
Push a car from rest: much harder.
Push a loaded truck from rest: nearly impossible for one person.

All three have zero velocity to start. The difference is their mass — and therefore their inertia.

Now imagine all three are rolling toward you on a flat surface at 5 m/s. Which one is hardest to stop?
- The bicycle stops quickly if you grab it
- The car... you cannot stop with your hands
- The truck... do not even try

Again, mass determines inertia, and inertia determines how hard it is to change velocity (in either direction — starting or stopping).

```
Inertia visualization:

LOW INERTIA (small mass):
Before: [bicycle] → 5 m/s
Apply small force to stop it:
After:  [bicycle]

HIGH INERTIA (large mass):
Before: [truck] → 5 m/s
Apply same small force:
After:  [truck] → 4.99 m/s  (barely changed)

High inertia objects resist changes much more strongly.
```

### Inertia Is Not Momentum (Yet)

We will cover momentum later. For now, just note that inertia is a property of an object (its mass), while momentum also depends on how fast the object is moving. A slow-moving truck has high inertia but moderate momentum. A fast-moving bullet has low inertia but high momentum.

## Understanding Net Force

Newton's First Law mentions the **net external force**. This is critical. It is not about whether ANY force acts on an object — it is about whether the forces CANCEL OUT or not.

### Multiple Forces Can Give Zero Net Force

Imagine a book sitting on a table. Two forces act on it:
1. Gravity pulls it DOWN (let us say with force W = 10 N)
2. The table pushes it UP (the **normal force**, N = 10 N)

```
         [TABLE SURFACE]
              |
              | Normal force N = 10 N (upward)
              |
              v (upward)
           [BOOK]
              ^ (downward)
              |
              | Weight W = 10 N (downward)
              |
```

Net force = N - W = 10 - 10 = 0 N

Net force is zero. By Newton's First Law, the book does not accelerate. It stays at rest. Makes sense.

### Net Force in 2D

Suppose a box on a flat surface is being pushed by three forces:
- Force A: 30 N to the east (+x)
- Force B: 20 N to the west (-x)
- Force C: 10 N upward (+y)
- Weight D: 10 N downward (-y)

Net force in x direction: 30 - 20 = 10 N east
Net force in y direction: 10 - 10 = 0 N

Net force = 10 N east. The box accelerates east. (We calculate HOW MUCH it accelerates using Newton's Second Law, coming in Chapter 13.)

```
         Force C: 10 N up
              ^
              |
Force B: ----[box]----> Force A: 30 N east
20 N west     |
              |
              v
         Weight D: 10 N down
         
Net force = 10 N east (the east-west forces don't cancel)
```

### The Vector Sum

Always add forces as vectors — considering their directions. You cannot just add magnitudes unless all forces point in the same direction.

## Equilibrium — More Than Just Staying Still

An object is in **equilibrium** when the net force on it is zero. This has a precise implication from Newton's First Law:

- If net force = 0 AND the object is at rest → it remains at rest (**static equilibrium**)
- If net force = 0 AND the object is moving → it continues moving at constant velocity (**dynamic equilibrium**)

Both are equilibrium! Newton's First Law makes no distinction between "at rest" and "moving at constant velocity." They are both the same state, physically — neither one requires a net force to maintain.

### The Galilean Perspective

This is why Galileo said the natural state of motion is "constant velocity (including rest)." From Newton's Law, we now see this precisely: both rest and constant motion require zero net force.

A spacecraft in deep space, billions of kilometers from any significant gravity, moves at constant velocity with its engines off. It is in dynamic equilibrium. No force is needed to keep it moving. This was impossible to see on Earth because friction always acts, always bringing things to rest.

```
STATIC EQUILIBRIUM          DYNAMIC EQUILIBRIUM
(at rest)                   (constant velocity)

     Forces balanced:             Forces balanced:
     
     ↑ N = 10 N                   ↑ N = 20 N
     |                            |
  [book]                       [sled moving at 3 m/s]
     |                            |
     ↓ W = 10 N                   ↓ W = 20 N (push force = friction force)
     
     Net force = 0                Net force = 0
     Object stays at rest         Object keeps moving at 3 m/s
     
Both are equilibrium — both governed by Newton's First Law.
```

## Everyday Examples of Inertia

Let us look at real situations where inertia appears, often in surprising ways.

### Example 1: The Tablecloth Trick

A skilled performer grabs a tablecloth and yanks it very quickly off a table. The dishes, glasses, and silverware on the cloth barely move — they stay in place while the cloth slides out from under them.

Why? **Inertia**. The dishes have mass, and therefore inertia. The cloth is pulled away so quickly that the friction force (which is the only force the cloth can apply to the dishes) acts for only a tiny fraction of a second. That very brief force is not enough to significantly change the velocity of the heavy dishes. They stay put.

The key: the force was applied for a very SHORT TIME. The same force applied for a longer time would have moved the dishes.

### Example 2: The Spinning Bucket of Water

Fill a bucket with water and swing it in a vertical circle over your head, fast enough. At the top of the circle, the bucket is upside down — but the water does not fall out.

Why? **Inertia**. The water "wants" to keep moving in a straight line. At the top of the circle, the straight-line path would take it upward (tangent to the circle). It is being forced to move in a circle, but its inertia means it presses against the bottom of the bucket rather than falling away.

### Example 3: The Car Crash (Why You Need a Seatbelt)

You are in a car moving at 60 km/h. The car hits a wall and stops suddenly. Your body, thanks to inertia, "wants" to keep moving at 60 km/h. Nothing is pushing you forward — your inertia just resists the stopping. Without a seatbelt, you fly forward and hit the windshield. The seatbelt applies a force to stop you.

### Example 4: A Spacecraft in Deep Space

The Voyager 1 probe was launched in 1977. Its engines burned for only a short time to leave Earth's gravity. Since then, it has been coasting through space with virtually no fuel use. As of 2024, it is over 24 billion kilometers from Earth and still moving at about 17 km/s — because there is nothing significant in deep space to slow it down. It will continue moving effectively forever (or until it encounters something).

This is Newton's First Law in its purest form.

### Example 5: Starting to Walk

When you walk, you push BACKWARD on the ground with your foot. The ground pushes FORWARD on you (reaction force — Newton's Third Law, coming up). This forward force accelerates you from rest. Once you are at constant walking speed, your muscles only need to supply enough force to overcome friction and air resistance.

## The Seatbelt — A Life-Saving Application of Inertia

This deserves its own section because it is so important and so commonly misunderstood.

### The Misconception

Many people say "the seatbelt keeps you from being thrown forward." This is sloppy language that implies some force throws you forward. But nothing is pushing you forward. Your inertia is not a force — it is a RESISTANCE to changing velocity.

### The Reality

Here is what actually happens:

1. Car and your body are moving at 100 km/h (about 28 m/s)
2. Car hits obstacle and decelerates very rapidly (say, it stops in 0.1 seconds)
3. The car experiences a large backward force (from the impact) that changes its velocity
4. Your body experiences NO such force directly (without a seatbelt)
5. Your body therefore CONTINUES at 28 m/s forward while the car rapidly slows
6. Your body then hits the steering wheel/dashboard/windshield at nearly the original speed

The seatbelt's job: apply a backward force to your body EARLY in the crash, over the 0.1 seconds the car is decelerating, to reduce your body's velocity along with the car. It spreads the stopping force over time and across a wide area of your body.

```
Without seatbelt:

  t = 0:  [car 28 m/s →] [body 28 m/s →]
  t = 0.05s: [car 14 m/s →] [body 28 m/s →]  ← body overtaking car
  t = 0.1s: [car 0 m/s]  [body hits windshield at ~28 m/s]
  
With seatbelt:

  t = 0:  [car 28 m/s →] [body 28 m/s →]
  t = 0.05s: [car 14 m/s →] [body 14 m/s →]  ← seatbelt forces equal deceleration
  t = 0.1s: [car 0 m/s]  [body 0 m/s]
  
  The seatbelt applied a backward force on the body throughout the stop.
  The body decelerates WITH the car, not INTO the car.
```

## Mass Versus Weight — A Crucial Distinction

This is one of the most frequently confused concepts in introductory physics. Please read carefully.

### Mass

**Mass** is the amount of matter in an object. It is measured in **kilograms (kg)**. Mass is an intrinsic property — it does not change with location. You have the same mass on Earth, on the Moon, on Mars, in the vacuum of deep space, everywhere.

Mass is also a measure of inertia — how resistant an object is to changes in velocity.

### Weight

**Weight** is the force that gravity exerts on an object. Since it is a force, it is measured in **Newtons (N)**.

    Weight = mass × gravitational acceleration
    W = m × g

On Earth's surface, g ≈ 9.8 m/s². So a 70 kg person weighs:

    W = 70 × 9.8 = 686 N

On the Moon, g ≈ 1.6 m/s². The same 70 kg person weighs:

    W = 70 × 1.6 = 112 N

The person's mass is 70 kg in both cases. Their weight is different because g is different.

### Why This Matters

In everyday life, we casually say "I weigh 70 kilograms." In physics, this is wrong. You have a MASS of 70 kg. Your WEIGHT is 686 N on Earth.

The confusion exists because on Earth, mass and weight are proportional (W = mg), so they are in a fixed ratio. But anywhere else in the universe, the ratio changes.

```
Same person (mass = 70 kg):

On Earth (g = 9.8 m/s²):
   Weight = 70 × 9.8 = 686 N
   
On Moon (g = 1.6 m/s²):
   Weight = 70 × 1.6 = 112 N
   (about 1/6 of Earth weight)
   
On Jupiter (g = 24.8 m/s²):
   Weight = 70 × 24.8 = 1736 N
   (about 2.5 times Earth weight)
   
In deep space (g ≈ 0):
   Weight ≈ 0 N (weightlessness)
   
Mass is ALWAYS 70 kg — unchanged everywhere.
Weight CHANGES because g changes.
```

### Weightlessness

Astronauts in the International Space Station appear to float — they seem "weightless." But they still have mass (70 kg, 100 kg, whatever). And gravity IS acting on them — the ISS orbits at about 400 km altitude, where g ≈ 8.7 m/s², nearly as strong as on the surface.

They feel weightless because they are in free fall — the station and everyone in it are all falling toward Earth at the same rate, which means there is no contact force between them. This is a different situation from having no weight. True weightlessness (zero weight) only exists infinitely far from all mass.

## Inertial Reference Frames

A **reference frame** is a coordinate system — a perspective from which you observe and measure motion.

An **inertial reference frame** is one that is not accelerating. From such a frame, Newton's First Law holds exactly: objects with no net force on them move at constant velocity.

Examples of (approximately) inertial reference frames:
- A room standing still
- A train moving at constant velocity on straight track
- A car traveling at steady speed on a straight highway
- The surface of Earth (approximately — Earth does rotate and orbit, so it is very slightly non-inertial, but usually we ignore this)

Newton's First Law only works exactly in inertial reference frames. If you are in an accelerating frame, objects seem to accelerate even without a net force on them — which is bizarre and confusing unless you account for the frame's own acceleration.

## Non-Inertial Reference Frames and Phantom Forces

A **non-inertial reference frame** is one that is accelerating. When you observe physics from a non-inertial frame, strange things seem to happen.

### The Accelerating Car

You are in a car that suddenly accelerates forward rapidly. You feel pushed back into your seat. But what is pushing you back?

From outside the car (an inertial frame):
- The car accelerates forward
- The seat pushes your back forward (with the car)
- Your body resists (inertia) — you feel the seat pushing you

From inside the car (a non-inertial frame):
- It seems like a mysterious force is pushing you backward
- But there is no such force! You invented it to explain why you are "moving backward" relative to the car.

These imaginary forces in non-inertial frames are called **fictitious forces** or **pseudo-forces**. They are useful mathematical tools (physicists use them all the time for rotating frames, spacecraft analysis, etc.) but they are not real forces from Newton's perspective.

```
INERTIAL FRAME (outside car):
Ground observer sees:
  Car accelerates → →
  Person's back gets pushed by seat
  Person accelerates with car → →
  No mysterious backward force.
  
NON-INERTIAL FRAME (inside car):
Person in car feels:
  "Something" pushes me back into the seat
  This is the pseudo-force / fictitious force
  It exists only because the frame is accelerating.
```

### Other Everyday Non-Inertial Experiences

- Standing in an elevator that accelerates upward: you feel heavier (the floor pushes up harder)
- Standing in an elevator that accelerates downward: you feel lighter
- Sitting in a car going around a bend: you feel pushed outward (the "centrifugal force" — a fictitious force)
- Riding a rollercoaster through a loop: you feel very heavy at the bottom, light at the top

All of these are your body's experience of inertia in a non-inertial frame.

## Newton's First Law and the Cosmos

Before Newton, people wondered: what keeps the Moon moving? What keeps the planets in their orbits? The common assumption (carrying Aristotelian thinking) was that some force must be pushing them continuously.

Newton's First Law reveals: **nothing needs to push them**. If there were no forces, they would move in straight lines at constant speed forever. The Moon does not move in a straight line — it curves in an ellipse around Earth. That curving requires a force: **gravity**.

So Newton's genius was not just the laws themselves — it was connecting the motion of objects on Earth to the motion of celestial bodies through the same laws.

The Moon is falling toward Earth under gravity. But it has enough sideways velocity (from when the solar system formed) that it keeps "missing" Earth as it falls — it curves around rather than crashing in. This is an orbit: continuous falling in a circle (or ellipse).

```
Without gravity, Moon would fly in a straight line:

     Moon ---------> (into space)
     
With gravity pulling toward Earth:

              Moon path
     Earth   /       \
       *   /           \
          \             /
           \           /
            \---------/
            (closed orbit)
            
The Moon is constantly falling toward Earth.
Gravity curves its path from a straight line into an orbit.
No engine needed — Newton's First Law says constant motion requires no force.
The only force is gravity, which curves the path continuously.
```

This was the great unification: the same physics that governs a rolling ball on Earth governs the Moon in the sky. Newton had connected the terrestrial and celestial realms.

## Worked Example 1 — Is This Object in Equilibrium?

**Problem:** A box has three forces acting on it:
- Force 1: 40 N pointing east
- Force 2: 30 N pointing west
- Force 3: 10 N pointing east

Is the box in equilibrium? If not, what is the net force?

### Solution

Assign east as positive (+x), west as negative (-x).

Force 1: +40 N
Force 2: -30 N
Force 3: +10 N

Net force = 40 + (-30) + 10 = +20 N (east)

The net force is **20 N east**. This is NOT zero, so the box is NOT in equilibrium. According to Newton's First Law, the box will accelerate eastward.

```
         Force 2        Force 1    Force 3
         30 N west      40 N east  10 N east
         ←              →           →
         [  ←  [BOX]  →  →  ]
         
         Net = -30 + 40 + 10 = +20 N  (east)
         The box accelerates east.
```

## Worked Example 2 — Forces on a Stationary Object

**Problem:** A lamp of mass 2 kg hangs from the ceiling by a wire. The lamp is stationary. What is the tension force in the wire?

### Given

- Mass of lamp: m = 2 kg
- g = 9.8 m/s²
- The lamp is stationary (at rest)

### Solution

Since the lamp is stationary, it is in equilibrium (static equilibrium). By Newton's First Law, the net force must be zero.

Forces acting on the lamp (vertical direction only):
- Weight (downward): W = m × g = 2 × 9.8 = 19.6 N downward
- Tension in wire (upward): T (unknown)

For equilibrium:

    Net force = T - W = 0
    T = W
    T = 19.6 N

The wire exerts a tension of **19.6 N upward** on the lamp, exactly equal and opposite to the lamp's weight.

```
         [Ceiling]
              |
              | T = 19.6 N (upward)
              ↑
           [LAMP]  ← mass 2 kg
              ↓
              | W = 19.6 N (downward = weight)
              
         Net force = T - W = 19.6 - 19.6 = 0
         Lamp stays stationary. Newton's First Law confirmed.
```

## Worked Example 3 — Mass and Weight on Different Worlds

**Problem:** An astronaut has a mass of 80 kg. Calculate their weight (in Newtons) on:
(a) Earth (g = 9.8 m/s²)
(b) Mars (g = 3.7 m/s²)
(c) The Moon (g = 1.6 m/s²)
(d) Deep space (g ≈ 0 m/s²)

Also: what is their mass in each location?

### Solution

Weight formula: W = m × g

**(a) Earth:**

    W = 80 × 9.8 = 784 N

**(b) Mars:**

    W = 80 × 3.7 = 296 N

**(c) Moon:**

    W = 80 × 1.6 = 128 N

**(d) Deep space:**

    W = 80 × 0 = 0 N (weightless)

**Mass in all locations:** 80 kg (mass does not change with location)

### Summary Table

| Location | g (m/s²) | Weight (N) | Mass (kg) |
|----------|----------|------------|-----------|
| Earth | 9.8 | 784 | 80 |
| Mars | 3.7 | 296 | 80 |
| Moon | 1.6 | 128 | 80 |
| Deep space | ≈ 0 | ≈ 0 | 80 |

Notice: the mass is always 80 kg. The weight varies enormously.

On Mars, this person would weigh about 37.8% of their Earth weight — they could jump much higher and lift heavier objects. But they would still have the same inertia (mass), which means starting or stopping a heavy object would require the same effort as on Earth.

## Common Misconceptions

### Misconception 1: "An object needs a constant force to keep moving."

This is Aristotle's error. An object moving at constant velocity does NOT need a force to maintain that velocity. It only needs a force if something (like friction) is trying to slow it down. In the absence of friction, constant motion is natural and effortless.

### Misconception 2: "Inertia is a force."

Inertia is NOT a force. It is a property of matter — the tendency to resist changes in motion. When you feel "pushed back" in an accelerating car, no force is pushing you back. Your inertia resists the acceleration, and the seat provides the forward force that overcomes this resistance.

### Misconception 3: "More speed = more inertia."

Inertia is determined by mass alone, not by speed. A large, slow truck has more inertia than a small, fast bullet. (Though technically at very high speeds near the speed of light, mass does increase slightly — but this is relativistic physics far beyond this course.)

### Misconception 4: "The seatbelt 'keeps you in your seat' by pushing you forward."

The seatbelt pulls you backward (decelerating you). Without a seatbelt, you would keep moving forward (inertia) while the car stops around you. The seatbelt provides the backward force needed to decelerate you along with the car.

### Misconception 5: "Weight and mass are the same thing."

They are fundamentally different. Mass (kg) is the amount of matter and a measure of inertia. Weight (N) is the gravitational force on that mass. They are proportional on Earth (W = mg) but represent different physical quantities. Your mass is 70 kg everywhere. Your weight is 686 N on Earth and only 112 N on the Moon.

### Misconception 6: "Astronauts in orbit have no gravity acting on them."

Gravity is VERY much acting on them — about 88% as strong as on Earth's surface at the altitude of the ISS. They feel "weightless" because they and their spacecraft are all in free fall together, so there are no contact forces between them. It is not zero gravity; it is zero apparent weight.

## Summary

- **Aristotle's error**: for 2000 years, people believed objects naturally tend toward rest and need a continuous force to keep moving. This seemed obvious because friction was not recognized as a force.

- **Galileo's breakthrough**: by extrapolating to a frictionless ideal surface, he realized objects would move forever without force. Friction, not the absence of force, causes deceleration.

- **Newton's First Law**: "An object at rest remains at rest, and an object in motion remains in motion at constant velocity, unless acted upon by a net external force."

- **Inertia** is the tendency of matter to resist changes in motion. More mass = more inertia.

- **Net force** is the vector sum of all forces on an object. It is zero when forces cancel out, nonzero when they do not.

- **Equilibrium** means net force = zero. This covers both objects at rest (static equilibrium) and objects moving at constant velocity (dynamic equilibrium).

- Everyday inertia examples: seatbelts, tablecloth trick, spacecraft coasting in space, feeling pushed back when a car accelerates.

- **Mass** (kg) is the amount of matter — a fixed property. **Weight** (N) is the gravitational force on that mass — it changes with the local value of g.

    Weight = mass × g
    W = m × g

- **Inertial reference frames** are non-accelerating frames where Newton's laws hold directly.

- **Non-inertial frames** are accelerating frames where "phantom" fictitious forces seem to appear.

- **The cosmic implication**: the Moon and planets do not need a continuous push — they move by inertia. Gravity curves their otherwise straight paths into orbits.
