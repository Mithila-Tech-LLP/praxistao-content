## The Big Misconception

Most people use the words "temperature" and "heat" as if they mean the same thing. In everyday language, someone might say "the heat is 40 degrees today." In physics, that sentence is actually mixing up two completely different concepts.

This confusion is so common that it is worth spending some time on it right at the start.

Consider two objects: a cup of boiling water at 100°C and a swimming pool filled with water at 30°C. Which one has more heat?

The answer might surprise you. The swimming pool, despite being cooler, contains far more **thermal energy** than the tiny cup. The cup has a higher temperature, but the pool has enormously more energy stored in its billions of trillions of water molecules.

This is the heart of the distinction:

- **Temperature** tells you how hot or cold something is — it measures the average kinetic energy of the particles.
- **Heat** is the transfer of thermal energy from a hotter object to a cooler one.

You cannot say an object "contains heat." Heat only exists during the process of transfer. After the transfer, the object simply has more internal (thermal) energy. This sounds like a subtle distinction but it matters enormously.

## What Is Temperature?

Everything you can see and touch is made of atoms and molecules. These particles are never perfectly still — they are constantly moving, vibrating, rotating, and bouncing off each other. This constant, chaotic motion is called **thermal motion**.

**Temperature** is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a substance.

When you heat something up, you are adding energy to its particles. They start moving faster. When they move faster, the average kinetic energy goes up, and so does the temperature. When you cool something down, the particles slow down, the average kinetic energy drops, and so does the temperature.

The key word here is **average**. In a gas, for example, some molecules are moving very fast and some are moving slowly. Temperature does not tell you the speed of any single molecule — it tells you the average over all of them.

```
Low Temperature: Slow-moving particles

  o    o  o
    o    o
  o   o    o
      o  o

High Temperature: Fast-moving particles

 o  o  o  o
o  o   o  o  o
  o  o  o   o
o   o  o  o
```

Notice that at high temperature the particles are spread out a bit more and moving faster (implied by the wider spacing and disorder). This is also why things tend to expand when heated — a concept we will explore in the next chapter.

Because temperature is about average kinetic energy, it is a **scalar** quantity — it has a magnitude but no direction. You measure temperature with a **thermometer**, which works by allowing a substance (mercury, alcohol, gas, or an electronic sensor) to reach thermal equilibrium with the object being measured and then reading off its change.

## What Is Heat?

**Heat** (symbol Q) is the transfer of thermal energy between objects due to a temperature difference.

Heat always flows spontaneously from the hotter object to the cooler object. It never flows the other way on its own. This is one of the most fundamental rules of nature, and we will explore it more in the chapters on thermodynamics.

Heat is measured in **joules (J)**, the SI unit of energy. Sometimes you will see it measured in **calories** (1 calorie = 4.184 J), which was historically defined as the energy needed to raise 1 gram of water by 1°C.

Heat is not a thing stored inside an object. It is a process of energy transfer. Once the transfer stops, we no longer speak of "heat" — we speak of the object's **internal energy** or **thermal energy**, which is the total kinetic and potential energy of all its particles.

Key points about heat:
- Heat flows from hot to cold, never cold to hot (on its own)
- Heat is a form of energy transfer, not a substance
- Heat is measured in joules
- The symbol Q is used for heat; positive Q means heat added to an object

## Temperature Scales

There are three temperature scales you need to know:

### Celsius Scale (°C)

Invented by Anders Celsius in 1742. This is the most commonly used scale in everyday life outside the United States.

- **0°C** = freezing point of water (at sea level)
- **100°C** = boiling point of water (at sea level)

The degree size is set so that there are 100 equal steps between these two reference points.

### Fahrenheit Scale (°F)

Used primarily in the United States for everyday temperatures.

- **32°F** = freezing point of water
- **212°F** = boiling point of water

### Kelvin Scale (K)

The scientific scale. Used by physicists and chemists worldwide. It has the same degree size as Celsius but starts at a different zero point — **absolute zero**.

- **0 K** = absolute zero (the coldest possible temperature)
- **273.15 K** = freezing point of water
- **373.15 K** = boiling point of water

Note: We say "273 kelvin" or "273 K", NOT "273 degrees kelvin". The degree symbol is not used with Kelvin.

### Conversion Formulas

```
Celsius to Fahrenheit:
    F = (9/5) × C + 32

Fahrenheit to Celsius:
    C = (5/9) × (F - 32)

Celsius to Kelvin:
    K = C + 273.15

Kelvin to Celsius:
    C = K - 273.15
```

### Quick Conversion Table

```
Description          | Celsius | Fahrenheit | Kelvin
---------------------|---------|------------|--------
Absolute zero        | -273.15 | -459.67    |   0
Liquid nitrogen      | -196    | -321       |  77
Dry ice (CO2)        | -78.5   | -109       | 194.65

```

## Absolute Zero and the Kelvin Scale

As you cool something down, the particles slow down. You might ask: how cold can something get? Can it get cold without limit?

The answer is no. There is a coldest possible temperature called **absolute zero**, defined as 0 K or -273.15°C.

At absolute zero, the particles would have the minimum possible energy. In classical physics, this would mean they have stopped moving entirely. Quantum mechanics tells us the particles still have a tiny amount of "zero-point energy" and can never be completely still, but the temperature still has a definite floor.

This is why the Kelvin scale is so important in physics. Because temperature is proportional to kinetic energy, and kinetic energy cannot be negative, temperature in kelvin cannot be negative. The Kelvin scale starts at the lowest possible point.

```
Temperature Scale Comparison:

   Kelvin     Celsius    Fahrenheit
   ------     -------    ----------
   
   373.15 --- 100  ----- 212      ← Water boils
     |          |           |
     |          |           |
   293.15 ---  20  ------  68     ← Room temp
     |          |           |
     |          |           |
   273.15 ---   0  ------  32     ← Water freezes
     |          |           |
     |          |           |
     0    --- -273.15 --- -459.67  ← Absolute zero
```

Absolute zero is considered impossible to actually reach. Scientists have gotten extremely close — within billionths of a kelvin — but the laws of thermodynamics prevent reaching it exactly.

## Thermal Equilibrium and the Zeroth Law

Suppose you put a cold metal spoon into a bowl of hot soup. The spoon gets warmer and the soup gets slightly cooler. After enough time, both are at the same temperature. At this point, no more net heat flows between them. We say they have reached **thermal equilibrium**.

**Thermal equilibrium**: Two objects are in thermal equilibrium when they are at the same temperature and there is no net heat flow between them.

The **Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics** makes a formal statement about this:

> If object A is in thermal equilibrium with object C, and object B is in thermal equilibrium with object C, then object A is in thermal equilibrium with object B.

This sounds obvious, but it is actually the logical foundation for temperature measurement. The thermometer (object C) is placed in contact with a hot cup of tea (object A) until equilibrium is reached. Then, without changing the thermometer, you place it against something else (object B) and see if the reading changes. If it does not, A and B are at the same temperature.

Without the Zeroth Law, the concept of a thermometer would not be logically consistent.

```
Zeroth Law Illustrated:

    [Object A] -- thermal equilibrium -- [Object C: Thermometer]
                                                |
    [Object B] -- thermal equilibrium -- [Object C: Thermometer]
    
    Therefore: Object A and Object B are at the same temperature.
```

The Zeroth Law is called "zeroth" because it was recognized as more fundamental than the First and Second Laws, but was formulated after them. It was given the number zero so the other laws did not have to be renumbered.

## Specific Heat Capacity

Different materials respond differently to the same amount of heat. Metal heats up quickly when you put it on a stove, while water takes much longer. This property is captured by the **specific heat capacity**.

**Specific heat capacity** (symbol c) is the amount of energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kilogram of a substance by 1°C (or 1 K — the step size is the same).

The formula connecting heat Q, mass m, specific heat c, and temperature change ΔT is:

```
Q = m × c × ΔT

Where:
  Q  = heat energy transferred (joules, J)
  m  = mass of the substance (kilograms, kg)
  c  = specific heat capacity (J / kg·°C  or  J / kg·K)
  ΔT = change in temperature (°C or K)
```

### Specific Heat Values for Common Substances

```
Substance         | Specific Heat c (J / kg·°C)
------------------|----------------------------
Water (liquid)    |  4186
Ice               |  2090
Steam             |  2010
Aluminium         |   900
Glass             |   840
Iron / Steel      |   450
Copper            |   385
Lead              |   128
Gold              |   129
```

Notice how water has an exceptionally high specific heat capacity. It takes 4186 joules to raise just 1 kg of water by 1°C, compared to only 450 joules for the same mass of iron. This has profound consequences for climate, cooking, and biology, which we will discuss shortly.

## Worked Example 1

**Problem:** How much heat is needed to raise the temperature of a 2 kg iron block from 20°C to 120°C?

**Given:**
- m = 2 kg
- c = 450 J/kg·°C (iron)
- Initial temperature T₁ = 20°C
- Final temperature T₂ = 120°C
- ΔT = T₂ - T₁ = 120 - 20 = 100°C

**Solution:**

```
Q = m × c × ΔT
Q = 2 kg × 450 J/kg·°C × 100°C
Q = 90,000 J
Q = 90 kJ
```

**Answer:** You need 90,000 joules (90 kJ) to heat the iron block.

---

## Worked Example 2

**Problem:** How much heat is needed to raise the temperature of 500 mL of water from 20°C to 100°C? (500 mL of water has a mass of 0.5 kg)

**Given:**
- m = 0.5 kg
- c = 4186 J/kg·°C (liquid water)
- ΔT = 100 - 20 = 80°C

**Solution:**

```
Q = m × c × ΔT
Q = 0.5 kg × 4186 J/kg·°C × 80°C
Q = 0.5 × 4186 × 80
Q = 167,440 J
Q ≈ 167 kJ
```

**Answer:** About 167 kJ of heat is needed.

This is also why it takes so long to boil a kettle — water's enormous specific heat means it soaks up a lot of energy for every degree gained.

## Calorimetry

**Calorimetry** is the science of measuring heat transfer. The basic principle is conservation of energy: the heat lost by the hot object equals the heat gained by the cold object, assuming no energy escapes to the surroundings.

```
Heat lost by hot object = Heat gained by cold object

m_hot × c_hot × (T_hot - T_final) = m_cold × c_cold × (T_final - T_cold)
```

A **calorimeter** is an insulated container used to minimise heat loss to the environment during experiments.

```
Calorimetry Setup:

    Insulated walls
    ┌──────────────────────┐
    │  ┌────────────────┐  │
    │  │  Thermometer   │  │
    │  │      |         │  │
    │  │   Hot water    │  │ ← Insulation prevents
    │  │       +        │  │   heat loss to room
    │  │   Cold water   │  │
    │  │                │  │
    │  └────────────────┘  │
    └──────────────────────┘
    
    Heat flows from hot → cold until equilibrium
    No heat escapes the system (ideal case)
```

## Worked Example 3

**Problem:** 200 g of water at 80°C is mixed with 300 g of water at 20°C in an insulated container. What is the final temperature?

**Given:**
- Hot water: m₁ = 0.2 kg, T₁ = 80°C, c = 4186 J/kg·°C
- Cold water: m₂ = 0.3 kg, T₂ = 20°C, c = 4186 J/kg·°C

**Setup:**

Both are water so both have the same specific heat c. This simplifies things.

```
Heat lost by hot = Heat gained by cold

m₁ × c × (T₁ - T_f) = m₂ × c × (T_f - T₂)
```

Since c appears on both sides, we can cancel it:

```
m₁ × (T₁ - T_f) = m₂ × (T_f - T₂)

0.2 × (80 - T_f) = 0.3 × (T_f - 20)

16 - 0.2 T_f = 0.3 T_f - 6

16 + 6 = 0.3 T_f + 0.2 T_f

22 = 0.5 T_f

T_f = 44°C
```

**Answer:** The final temperature is 44°C.

**Check:** This should be between 20°C and 80°C, and closer to the 20°C side because there is more cold water. 44°C is slightly below the midpoint of 50°C, which makes sense since the cold mass (0.3 kg) is larger.

## Phase Changes

So far we have talked about heat causing temperature changes. But there is something surprising: you can add heat to an object without changing its temperature at all. This happens during a **phase change**.

**Phases of matter** are the different states matter can exist in:

```
                   Melting                  Boiling/Evaporation
                 (solid → liquid)           (liquid → gas)
                 
    SOLID  ─────────────────────→  LIQUID  ─────────────────────→  GAS
    
           ←─────────────────────         ←─────────────────────
               Freezing                      Condensation
           (liquid → solid)               (gas → liquid)
           
           
   Also:  SOLID ──────────────────────────────────────────────→  GAS
                              Sublimation
                        (dry ice, snow in winter)
```

The most familiar phase changes for water:
- **Melting point of water**: 0°C at normal atmospheric pressure
- **Boiling point of water**: 100°C at normal atmospheric pressure

During a phase change, the temperature stays constant even though heat is being added or removed. All the energy goes into breaking (or forming) the bonds between molecules, not into speeding them up.

---

## Latent Heat

The energy required to cause a phase change is called **latent heat** (from Latin "latens" meaning "hidden", because it does not show up as a temperature change).

```
Q = m × L

Where:
  Q = heat energy needed for the phase change (joules)
  m = mass of the substance (kg)
  L = latent heat (J/kg)
```

There are two types:

**Latent heat of fusion (L_f)**: energy needed to melt (solid → liquid) or released when freezing (liquid → solid)

**Latent heat of vaporisation (L_v)**: energy needed to boil (liquid → gas) or released when condensing (gas → liquid)

### Latent Heat Values for Water

```
Process                    | Latent Heat Value
---------------------------|------------------
Fusion (melting/freezing)  | L_f = 334,000 J/kg = 334 kJ/kg
Vaporisation (boil/cond.)  | L_v = 2,260,000 J/kg = 2,260 kJ/kg
```

Notice that L_v is about **six times larger** than L_f. It takes much more energy to boil water (break it free from all its liquid neighbours) than to melt it (just loosen the rigid solid structure slightly).

## Why Boiling Takes More Energy

When ice melts, the water molecules go from a rigid crystal structure to a liquid where they can move around, but they are still close to each other and still attracted to each other. Not much structural change.

When water boils, the molecules must escape from all their neighbours and spread out as a gas where they barely interact. The molecules must overcome all the intermolecular attractions that hold the liquid together. This requires a huge amount of energy — hence the very large latent heat of vaporisation.

This is why:
- It takes about 80 calories to melt 1 gram of ice (fusion)
- But it takes about 540 calories to boil that same gram of liquid water (vaporisation)

Boiling requires about **7 times more energy** than melting the same mass of ice.

## Real-World Applications of Latent Heat

### Why Sweating Cools You

When sweat (mostly water) evaporates from your skin, it undergoes vaporisation. Each kilogram of sweat that evaporates carries away 2,260,000 joules of energy from your body. This is why even a small amount of evaporation can cool you down significantly. The energy is taken from your skin, lowering its temperature.

This is also why blowing on your skin when it is wet feels cool — you accelerate evaporation.

### Why Coastal Areas Have Mild Climates

Water has an extremely high specific heat (4186 J/kg·°C) AND a very high latent heat of vaporisation. The ocean stores enormous amounts of energy.

In summer, the ocean absorbs vast heat without warming up much, keeping coastal air cool. In winter, the ocean releases that stored heat gradually, keeping coastal air warm. The ocean acts as a thermal buffer, moderating temperature extremes.

Cities far from the ocean, like Denver or Moscow, experience extreme hot summers and cold winters because there is no large body of water nearby to smooth out the temperature swings. Coastal cities like San Francisco, London, or Lisbon have much milder, more consistent temperatures.

### Why Cooking Burns are Worse with Steam

If you put your hand into water at 100°C, it is very painful and dangerous. But if you put your hand into steam at 100°C, the burn is far worse. Why? Because the steam must first condense on your skin, releasing its latent heat of vaporisation (2,260 kJ/kg) directly into your skin before it even cools down.

### Why Burns from Boiling Water Are Worse Than Expected

Latent heat also explains why a burn from boiling water is so severe — the steam condenses and releases its latent heat into your skin even after the water itself has cooled, prolonging the injury.

### Refrigerators and Air Conditioners

These machines work by forcing a refrigerant fluid through a cycle of evaporation and condensation. The fluid evaporates inside the fridge (absorbing heat via latent heat) and condenses outside (releasing that heat). The fluid carries energy from inside the fridge to the outside, cooling the interior.

### Drying Your Hair

Your wet hair has water in it. To dry it, you must supply enough energy for vaporisation. This is why a hair dryer takes time — it is supplying latent heat to evaporate each droplet of water.

## Worked Example 4

**Problem:** How much energy is needed to:
(a) Heat 0.5 kg of ice from -20°C to 0°C
(b) Melt that ice at 0°C
(c) Heat the resulting water from 0°C to 100°C
(d) Boil the water at 100°C

Use: c_ice = 2090 J/kg·°C, c_water = 4186 J/kg·°C, L_f = 334,000 J/kg, L_v = 2,260,000 J/kg

**Part (a): Heating ice from -20°C to 0°C**

```
Q_a = m × c_ice × ΔT
Q_a = 0.5 × 2090 × 20
Q_a = 20,900 J = 20.9 kJ
```

**Part (b): Melting ice at 0°C**

```
Q_b = m × L_f
Q_b = 0.5 × 334,000
Q_b = 167,000 J = 167 kJ
```

**Part (c): Heating water from 0°C to 100°C**

```
Q_c = m × c_water × ΔT
Q_c = 0.5 × 4186 × 100
Q_c = 209,300 J = 209.3 kJ
```

**Part (d): Boiling water at 100°C**

```
Q_d = m × L_v
Q_d = 0.5 × 2,260,000
Q_d = 1,130,000 J = 1130 kJ
```

**Total energy:**

```
Q_total = 20.9 + 167 + 209.3 + 1130
Q_total = 1527.2 kJ
```

**Summary of results:**

```
Process              | Energy (kJ) | Percentage of total
---------------------|-------------|--------------------
Heating ice          |    20.9     |   1.4%
Melting ice          |   167.0     |  10.9%
Heating water        |   209.3     |  13.7%
Boiling water        |  1130.0     |  74.0%
---------------------|-------------|--------------------
Total                |  1527.2     | 100%
```

Boiling the water uses over 74% of all the energy needed for the entire journey from -20°C ice to steam. This is the power of latent heat.

## Summary

In this chapter we established the foundation of thermal physics:

**Temperature** is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a substance. It is not the same as heat.

**Heat** is the transfer of thermal energy from a hotter object to a cooler object. It flows spontaneously from hot to cold and is measured in joules.

**Temperature scales**: Celsius is used in everyday life; Fahrenheit is used in the US; Kelvin is used in science. Kelvin starts at absolute zero (0 K = -273.15°C).

**Absolute zero** (0 K) is the lowest possible temperature, where particles have minimum possible energy.

**Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics**: If two objects are each in thermal equilibrium with a third object, they are in thermal equilibrium with each other. This is the logical basis for temperature measurement.

**Specific heat capacity** c tells you how much energy per kilogram per degree is needed to change an object's temperature. Q = mcΔT.

**Calorimetry** uses conservation of energy: heat lost by hot = heat gained by cold (in an isolated system).

**Phase changes** occur at fixed temperatures (melting point, boiling point). During a phase change, all added energy breaks or forms bonds rather than raising temperature.

**Latent heat** Q = mL is the energy involved in phase changes. Latent heat of vaporisation is much larger than latent heat of fusion for water.
