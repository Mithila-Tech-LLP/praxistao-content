## 1. Why Atomic Models Matter

Everything you see around you is made of atoms. The chair you sit on, the air you breathe, the screen you read from — all atoms, arranged in different ways.

But for most of human history, nobody knew what an atom looked like inside. The word "atom" comes from the Greek *atomos*, meaning indivisible. For over 2,000 years, people assumed atoms were the smallest possible things — tiny, featureless spheres.

Then, in the late 1800s and early 1900s, a series of remarkable experiments tore that picture apart. Scientists discovered that atoms had inner structure — and that structure was far stranger than anyone imagined.

Understanding atomic structure is not just an abstract exercise. It explains:

- Why elements have different chemical properties
- Why heated gases glow in specific colors
- How lasers, LEDs, and neon signs work
- How astronomers know what distant stars are made of
- Why some materials conduct electricity and others do not
- How atomic clocks keep time so precisely that they lose less than a second in 300 million years

The story of atomic models is one of the greatest scientific detective stories ever told. Let us follow it from the beginning.

---

## 2. Dalton's Atomic Theory (1803)

### The First Scientific Atomic Model

John Dalton was an English chemist who studied how elements combine in fixed proportions. His observations led him to propose the first scientific (not just philosophical) theory of atoms.

**Dalton's Five Postulates (1803):**

1. All matter is made of tiny, indestructible particles called **atoms**
2. Atoms of the same element are identical in mass and properties
3. Atoms of different elements have different masses and properties
4. Atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios to form **compounds**
5. In chemical reactions, atoms are rearranged (not created or destroyed)

### The Solid Sphere Model

Dalton imagined atoms as **solid, featureless spheres** — like tiny billiard balls. They had no internal structure; they were simply the smallest unit of each element.

```
Dalton's Atom (1803):

     ( )       ( )       ( )
   Hydrogen   Carbon   Oxygen

Each is a solid, indivisible sphere.
Different elements = different sizes/masses.
No internal structure whatsoever.
```

### What Dalton Got Right

- The idea that elements have characteristic atoms
- Conservation of mass in reactions
- The basis for the mole concept
- Atoms combining in fixed ratios (law of definite proportions)

### What Dalton Got Wrong

- Atoms are NOT indivisible — they have internal parts
- Atoms of the same element are NOT always identical (isotopes exist)

Despite being incomplete, Dalton's model was enormously useful and drove chemistry forward for most of the 1800s. It predicted the behavior of gases, explained chemical reactions, and gave chemists a mental framework that worked well enough.

The first hint that something was inside the atom came in 1897 — and it came from an unlikely source: a glowing tube of gas.

## 3. Thomson and the Discovery of the Electron (1897)

### The Cathode Ray Tube Experiment

In the 1890s, physicists were fascinated by **cathode ray tubes** — sealed glass tubes with most of the air pumped out, with a metal electrode at each end. When a high voltage was applied, a mysterious glowing beam traveled from the negative electrode (cathode) to the positive electrode (anode).

```
Cathode Ray Tube Setup:

High Voltage
(-)        (+)
|           |
[Cathode]---->----[Anode]
           BEAM
           (cathode rays)

The beam travels in straight lines, casts sharp shadows,
and causes the end of the tube to glow.
```

The key question was: what IS this beam? Is it a wave? A stream of particles?

### Thomson's Clever Test

J.J. Thomson (Cambridge, 1897) applied electric and magnetic fields to the beam. He found:

1. The beam **bent toward the positive plate** when an electric field was applied
2. The beam **bent in the expected direction** when a magnetic field was applied
3. By balancing the two fields, he could measure the **ratio of charge to mass** (e/m)

The bending toward the positive plate meant the particles in the beam were **negatively charged**.

Thomson found that e/m was the same regardless of:
- What metal the cathode was made from
- What gas (if any) was left in the tube
- What voltage was used

This was astonishing. The same particles appeared to come from every material. These particles must be a **universal component of all matter**.

Thomson called them **corpuscles**; we now call them **electrons**.

### Key Discovery: The Electron

**Properties of the electron (discovered 1897):**
- Mass: m_e = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg (extremely light)
- Charge: q = -1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C (negative)
- The electron is about 1836 times lighter than a proton

This immediately raised a problem: if atoms contain negative electrons, but atoms are normally electrically neutral, then atoms must also contain **positive charge** somewhere. But where?

### Thomson's Plum Pudding Model (1904)

Thomson proposed that an atom consists of:
- A **diffuse, positive charge** spread throughout the entire volume of the atom (like a ball of pudding)
- **Electrons embedded within** this positive "pudding" (like raisins or plums in a pudding)

```
Thomson's Plum Pudding Model (1904):

    +  -  +  -  +
  -   +   -   +   -
    +  -  +  -  +
  
  Positive charge spread uniformly
  throughout the atom.
  Electrons (-) embedded inside
  like raisins in pudding.
```

This model neatly explained electrical neutrality — the positive and negative charges balanced each other out. It was also consistent with the evidence available at the time.

But it was wrong. And the proof came from one of the most famous experiments in physics.

## 4. Rutherford's Gold Foil Experiment (1911)

### The Setup

Ernest Rutherford (working in Manchester with Geiger and Marsden) designed an experiment to test Thomson's plum pudding model. He used **alpha particles** (α-particles) — positively charged particles emitted by radioactive radium — as tiny bullets to probe the structure of gold atoms.

```
Rutherford Scattering Experiment:

                  [Gold foil]
                  (only a few
                  atoms thick)
                      |
[Radioactive       |  |  |
 Ra source]   ------>  |  |------->  Detector screen
(alpha         ------>  |  |         (zinc sulfide,
 particles)    ------>  |  |         glows when hit)
               ------>  |  |
               
Alpha particles fire at gold foil from one side.
Detectors placed ALL AROUND (including behind the source).
```

### What Was Expected

If Thomson's plum pudding model were correct, the positive charge is spread diffusely throughout each atom. The alpha particles (positively charged) would experience only a weak, spread-out electric repulsion. They should pass almost straight through, perhaps deflecting by tiny angles (< 1 degree) due to the soft positive "pudding."

```
Expected Result (Plum Pudding):

>>>>>>>  >>>>>>>>  >>>>>>>>  >>>>>>>
  
Alpha particles pass through with
tiny deflections. Nothing bounces back.
```

### What Was Actually Found

Rutherford later described his astonishment:

*"It was quite the most incredible event that has ever happened to me in my life. It was almost as incredible as if you fired 15-inch shells at a piece of tissue paper and they came back and hit you."*

The actual results were:

1. **Most alpha particles (~99.99%)** passed straight through with no deflection — the atom is mostly empty space
2. **Some (~1 in 10,000)** deflected by large angles (> 90°)
3. **A very small number** bounced almost straight back (nearly 180°)

```
Actual Results (Rutherford):

>>>>>>>  >>>>>>>>>  >>>>>>>>  >>>>>>>   [most go straight through]

           ↗ some deflect at large angles

   <<  [a few bounce back!]
```

### What This Proved

For a particle to bounce almost straight back, it must have hit something much heavier than itself and with a very concentrated positive charge. If the positive charge were spread out (as Thomson suggested), the repulsion would be too weak to reverse an alpha particle.

**Rutherford's Conclusions:**

1. The atom is **mostly empty space** (that is why most alpha particles pass through)
2. All the positive charge is concentrated in a tiny, dense region at the center — the **nucleus**
3. The nucleus contains almost all the atom's **mass**
4. Electrons orbit the nucleus at relatively large distances, like planets around the Sun

```
Rutherford's Nuclear Model (1911):

         e-   e-
        /        \
       /    (+)   \
      e-  NUCLEUS  e-   <-- tiny, dense, positive nucleus
       \          /
        \        /
         e-   e-

Nucleus: tiny (~10⁻¹⁵ m)
Atom:    large (~10⁻¹⁰ m)
```

This was a revolutionary discovery. The atom had a definite, tiny nucleus. Everything else was essentially empty.

## 5. The Nuclear Atom: Dimensions and Structure

### The Scale of the Atom

Let us put the numbers in perspective. These figures are staggering.

| Feature | Size |
|---------|------|
| Atom (hydrogen) | ~1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m = 0.1 nanometers |
| Nucleus (proton) | ~1 × 10⁻¹⁵ m = 1 femtometer |
| Electron (point-like) | < 10⁻¹⁸ m |

The ratio of atom size to nucleus size is:

```
Ratio = (atom diameter) / (nucleus diameter)
      = (10⁻¹⁰) / (10⁻¹⁵)
      = 100,000
```

**The nucleus is 100,000 times smaller than the atom.**

To put this in human terms: if the nucleus were the size of a marble (1 cm), the atom would be 1 km across. The entire 1 km sphere would be essentially empty, with a marble at the center.

```
Scale Model:

 If nucleus = marble (1 cm)
 
 [marble]                    ← nucleus, 1 cm

 [                           ]
 ←          1 km            →   ← atom boundary (where electrons orbit)
 
 Everything in between is empty space.
 The electrons are like specks of dust
 orbiting somewhere on the 1 km boundary.
```

### Volume Comparison

Volume ∝ r³. So the ratio of volumes is:

```
Volume ratio = (10⁻¹⁰)³ / (10⁻¹⁵)³
             = 10⁻³⁰ / 10⁻⁴⁵
             = 10¹⁵
```

The nucleus occupies only 1 part in 10¹⁵ of the atom's volume.

**The atom is 99.9999999999999% empty space.**

Yet matter feels solid. Why? Because the electromagnetic forces between electron clouds prevent atoms from passing through each other. Solidity is not about physical substance filling space — it is about force fields.

### The Nucleus: Protons and Neutrons

Rutherford's model had protons in the nucleus. In 1932, James Chadwick discovered the **neutron** — a neutral particle in the nucleus with nearly the same mass as a proton.

```
The Nucleus:

  (p)(n)(p)
  (n)(p)(n)
  (p)(n)(p)

p = proton  (charge +e, mass 1.673 × 10⁻²⁷ kg)
n = neutron (charge 0,  mass 1.675 × 10⁻²⁷ kg)

The protons and neutrons are held together by the
STRONG NUCLEAR FORCE — much more powerful than
electrostatic repulsion between the protons.
```

The number of protons defines the **element**. The number of neutrons can vary (producing **isotopes**).

## 6. Bohr's Planetary Model (1913)

### The Problem with Rutherford's Model

Rutherford's nuclear model had a fatal flaw from classical physics. According to Maxwell's equations, an **accelerating charged particle radiates energy**. An electron in circular orbit is constantly accelerating (centripetal acceleration). Therefore, it should continuously emit radiation, lose energy, and spiral into the nucleus — all within about 10⁻¹¹ seconds.

Matter should not exist. Yet here we are.

Something was fundamentally wrong with applying classical physics to the atom.

### Bohr's Bold Postulates (1913)

Niels Bohr, a young Danish physicist, solved this by combining Rutherford's nuclear model with Planck's quantum hypothesis. He made two daring postulates that contradicted classical physics:

**Postulate 1: Electrons can only orbit in certain allowed "shells" at specific distances from the nucleus. While in these allowed orbits, an electron does NOT radiate energy.**

**Postulate 2: An electron can jump from one allowed orbit to another. When it jumps DOWN (to lower energy), it emits a photon. When it jumps UP (to higher energy), it absorbs a photon. The photon energy equals the energy difference between the levels.**

```
Bohr's Model:

                n = 4  ___________
              n = 3  ___________
           n = 2  ___________
        n = 1  ___________    (ground state, lowest energy)
      
      [NUCLEUS]

      Electrons can ONLY be in these specific levels.
      They jump between them — never in between.
```

### The Allowed Orbits

Bohr derived the allowed radii by combining Newton's law (for circular orbit) with the condition that angular momentum is quantized:

```
Allowed angular momentum: m_e × v × r = n × h / (2π)

where n = 1, 2, 3, 4, ... (principal quantum number)
      h = Planck's constant = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s
```

From this, the allowed radii in hydrogen are:

```
r_n = n² × a₀

where a₀ = Bohr radius = 5.29 × 10⁻¹¹ m ≈ 0.053 nm
```

So:
- n = 1: r₁ = a₀ = 0.053 nm (ground state)
- n = 2: r₂ = 4a₀ = 0.212 nm
- n = 3: r₃ = 9a₀ = 0.476 nm

## 7. Energy Levels in the Hydrogen Atom

### The Formula

Bohr derived the energy of each allowed orbit in hydrogen:

```
E_n = -13.6 eV / n²

where n = 1, 2, 3, ...  (principal quantum number)
      eV = electron volt = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
```

**Important points:**

1. The energies are **negative** — this means the electron is **bound** to the nucleus. You must supply energy to free it.
2. As n increases, the energy becomes less negative (closer to zero) — the electron is less tightly bound.
3. At n = infinity, E = 0 — the electron is completely free (ionized atom).

### Energy Level Table for Hydrogen

```
n   |  E_n (eV)  |  Description
----|------------|----------------------------
1   |  -13.6     |  Ground state (most stable)
2   |  -3.40     |  First excited state
3   |  -1.51     |  Second excited state
4   |  -0.850    |  Third excited state
5   |  -0.544    |  Fourth excited state
∞   |   0        |  Ionization limit (free electron)
```

### Energy Level Diagram

```

  E (eV)

  -1.51 ────────────────────────────  n = 3  ─┐ Paschen series (IR)
                                               │
  -3.40 ────────────────────────────  n = 2  ─┼─┐ Balmer series (visible)
                                               │ │
```

## 8. Photon Emission and Absorption

### The Core Quantum Idea

This is where Bohr's model connects to Planck's quantum hypothesis. When an electron transitions between energy levels, it must **exchange exactly the right amount of energy** — no more, no less.

### Emission: Jumping Down

When an electron drops from energy level E₂ to lower level E₁:

```
Energy of emitted photon:

  E_photon = E₂ - E₁

  Since E_photon = hf:

  hf = E₂ - E₁

  f = (E₂ - E₁) / h

  And since c = f × λ:

  λ = hc / (E₂ - E₁)
```

Where:
- h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s (Planck's constant)
- c = 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s (speed of light)
- f = frequency of the emitted photon
- λ = wavelength of the emitted photon

**A specific transition → a specific frequency → a specific color of light.**

```
Emission:

  Before:  Electron at E₂ (higher level)
  
       ───────────  E₂
       |
       |  electron falls
       |
       ↓
       ───────────  E₁

       A photon is emitted with energy E₂ - E₁.
       The photon flies away at the speed of light.
```

### Absorption: Jumping Up

When a photon hits an atom, if and ONLY if its energy exactly matches an energy level difference, the electron absorbs it and jumps up:

```
Absorption:

  Incoming photon ──→  atom
  
  If E_photon = E₂ - E₁ EXACTLY:
  
       ───────────  E₁
       
       ↑  electron jumps up
       |
       ───────────  E₂

       The photon is ABSORBED. It disappears.
       The electron is now in excited state E₂.
```

If the photon energy does NOT match any transition, the photon passes right through. **Atoms are extremely selective absorbers.**

### Why This Explains Spectral Lines

Because only specific transitions are allowed (between specific energy levels), only specific frequencies of light are emitted or absorbed. This produces **sharp, discrete spectral lines** — not a continuous rainbow.

**Each element has its own unique set of energy levels → its own unique set of spectral lines.**

## 9. Emission Spectra: The Atomic Fingerprint

### How Emission Spectra Are Produced

When you heat a gas or pass electricity through it (as in a neon sign), electrons are excited to higher energy levels by collisions. They then fall back down, emitting photons of specific frequencies. If you pass this light through a **prism or diffraction grating**, you see distinct bright lines on a dark background — an **emission spectrum**.

```
Producing an Emission Spectrum:

  [Gas tube] → [Prism/Grating] → [Screen]
  
  Only specific wavelengths appear as bright lines:
  
  ───────────────────────────────────────── (screen)
      |      |  ||   |
      |      |  ||   |
     656    486 434 410   nm
     (red) (cyan)(violet)(violet)
     
  These four visible lines = Balmer series of hydrogen
```

### Hydrogen's Visible Lines (Balmer Series)

The four visible lines of hydrogen:

```
Color     | Wavelength | Transition
----------|------------|----------------
Red       | 656 nm     | n=3 → n=2
Blue-green| 486 nm     | n=4 → n=2
Violet    | 434 nm     | n=5 → n=2
Violet    | 410 nm     | n=6 → n=2
```

### Helium, Mercury, and Sodium

Every element has a different pattern:

```
Sodium emission spectrum (visible range):

     ─────────────────────────────────
              ||
              ||   ← Famous sodium yellow doublet (589 nm)
                   (why sodium streetlamps glow orange-yellow)
     ─────────────────────────────────

Mercury emission spectrum:

     ─────────────────────────────────
       |         |      |    ||
      405nm    436nm   546nm 577/579nm
     (violet) (blue) (green)(yellow)
     ─────────────────────────────────
```

The uniqueness of these patterns is the key to **spectroscopic analysis**: you can identify an unknown element by its spectral lines.

## 10. Absorption Spectra and Fraunhofer Lines

### Continuous vs. Line Spectra

A **hot, dense solid** (like the filament of a light bulb, or the interior of a star) emits a **continuous spectrum** — all wavelengths, like a full rainbow.

A **hot, sparse gas** emits only certain wavelengths — a **line emission spectrum**.

A **cool gas** placed in front of a continuous source **absorbs** the same specific wavelengths it would emit — a **line absorption spectrum** (dark lines on a bright background).

```
Continuous Spectrum:

  ████████████████████████████████  (all wavelengths present)

Emission Spectrum (hot gas):

  ─────│──│───│──│────────────────  (bright lines, dark background)

Absorption Spectrum (cool gas in front of bright source):

  ████████████████████████████████  (continuous background)
  minus:
  ─────│──│───│──│────────────────  (same positions, but now DARK)
  
  =
  ████╍████╍███╍██╍███████████████  (dark lines at those wavelengths)
```

The **dark lines** appear at exactly the same wavelengths as the bright emission lines for the same element. This is because the cool gas absorbs those specific photons from the background source.

### Fraunhofer Lines in the Sun's Spectrum

In 1814, Joseph von Fraunhofer noticed that the Sun's spectrum was not a perfect continuous rainbow — it had hundreds of dark lines crossing it. These are now called **Fraunhofer lines**.

```
Sun's Spectrum (schematic):

  Violet         Blue         Green       Yellow     Red
  400nm                                              700nm
  ────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  |||  | || |   |   |       | |  |  |  |    |   | |
  |||  | || |   |   |       | |  |  |  |    |   | |
  
  Each dark line = a specific element in the Sun's atmosphere
  absorbing that wavelength from the interior's bright light.
```

By comparing these lines with laboratory spectra, we can identify which elements are in the Sun's outer layers:

- **Sodium** (yellow doublet at 589 nm)
- **Hydrogen** (multiple lines)
- **Calcium** (prominent lines in blue/violet)
- **Iron** (hundreds of lines)
- And many others

This is why astronomers can determine the **composition of stars millions of light years away** — just from the light they emit. The spectral fingerprint survives the entire journey across space.

## 11. Spectral Series in Hydrogen

### The Three Main Series

When electrons in hydrogen fall to different final levels, they produce three main spectral series, each covering a different part of the electromagnetic spectrum:

```
                n = ∞ ─────── 0 eV
                n = 5 ───────
                n = 4 ───────
                n = 3 ─────── ─1.51 eV
                              │
                              │  Paschen series
                              │  (transitions to n=3)
                              │  INFRARED
                              
                n = 2 ─────── ─3.40 eV
                              │
                              │  Balmer series
                              │  (transitions to n=2)
                              │  VISIBLE (mostly)
                              
                              
                              
                              
                n = 1 ─────── ─13.6 eV
                              │
                              │  Lyman series
                              │  (transitions to n=1)
                              │  ULTRAVIOLET
```

### Lyman Series (Ultraviolet)

All transitions TO n = 1 (ground state):

```
n = 2 → n = 1:  E_photon = 13.6 - 3.40 = 10.2 eV  →  λ = 122 nm (deep UV)
n = 3 → n = 1:  E_photon = 13.6 - 1.51 = 12.1 eV  →  λ = 103 nm
n = 4 → n = 1:  E_photon = 13.6 - 0.85 = 12.8 eV  →  λ = 97 nm
```

These are in the **ultraviolet** — invisible to the human eye, but detected by UV sensors. Important in astrophysics and plasma physics.

### Balmer Series (Visible)

All transitions TO n = 2:

```
n = 3 → n = 2:  E_photon = 3.40 - 1.51 = 1.89 eV  →  λ = 656 nm  (red)
n = 4 → n = 2:  E_photon = 3.40 - 0.85 = 2.55 eV  →  λ = 486 nm  (blue-green)
n = 5 → n = 2:  E_photon = 3.40 - 0.54 = 2.86 eV  →  λ = 434 nm  (violet)
n = 6 → n = 2:  E_photon = 3.40 - 0.38 = 3.02 eV  →  λ = 410 nm  (violet)
```

These are in the **visible range** and were first observed by Balmer in 1885 — years before anyone understood why they existed. Bohr's theory explained them perfectly.

### Paschen Series (Infrared)

All transitions TO n = 3:

```
n = 4 → n = 3:  E_photon = 1.51 - 0.85 = 0.66 eV  →  λ = 1875 nm (IR)
n = 5 → n = 3:  E_photon = 1.51 - 0.54 = 0.97 eV  →  λ = 1281 nm (IR)
```

These lie in the **infrared** — detected by IR cameras and spectrometers.

## 12. Worked Examples

### Worked Example 1: Photon Emitted from Transition

**Problem:** An electron in hydrogen falls from n = 4 to n = 2. Calculate:
(a) The energy of the emitted photon in eV
(b) The frequency of the light
(c) The wavelength and the region of the EM spectrum

**Solution:**

Step 1 — Find the energy levels:

```
E₄ = -13.6 / 4² = -13.6 / 16 = -0.850 eV
E₂ = -13.6 / 2² = -13.6 / 4  = -3.40  eV
```

Step 2 — Calculate photon energy:

```
E_photon = E₄ - E₂ = (-0.850) - (-3.40) = 2.55 eV
```

Step 3 — Convert to Joules:

```
E_photon = 2.55 × 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 4.08 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
```

Step 4 — Find frequency (E = hf):

```
f = E / h = (4.08 × 10⁻¹⁹) / (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴)
f = 6.16 × 10¹⁴ Hz
```

Step 5 — Find wavelength (c = fλ):

```
λ = c / f = (3.0 × 10⁸) / (6.16 × 10¹⁴)
λ = 4.87 × 10⁻⁷ m = 487 nm
```

**Answer:** The photon has energy 2.55 eV, frequency 6.16 × 10¹⁴ Hz, wavelength 487 nm — a blue-green color in the **visible** spectrum. This is the n=4→2 line of the Balmer series.

---

### Worked Example 2: Identifying a Transition from Wavelength

**Problem:** A line in hydrogen's Lyman series has wavelength 97.2 nm. What transition produced this line?

**Solution:**

Step 1 — Convert wavelength to energy:

```
E_photon = hc / λ = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.0 × 10⁸) / (97.2 × 10⁻⁹)
E_photon = (1.988 × 10⁻²⁵) / (9.72 × 10⁻⁸)
E_photon = 2.045 × 10⁻¹⁸ J
E_photon = 2.045 × 10⁻¹⁸ / 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 12.78 eV
```

Step 2 — Lyman series means final level is n = 1:

```
E_final = -13.6 / 1² = -13.6 eV

E_initial = E_final + E_photon = -13.6 + 12.78 = -0.82 eV
```

Step 3 — Find n_initial:

```
-13.6 / n² = -0.82
n² = 13.6 / 0.82 = 16.6 ≈ 16
n = 4
```

**Answer:** The transition is from n = 4 to n = 1.

### Worked Example 3: Ionization

**Problem:** How much energy is needed to ionize a hydrogen atom that is currently in its first excited state (n = 2)?

**Solution:**

```
Current energy: E₂ = -13.6 / 2² = -3.40 eV

Energy at ionization: E_∞ = 0 eV

Energy needed = E_∞ - E₂ = 0 - (-3.40) = 3.40 eV
```

**Answer:** 3.40 eV is needed to ionize hydrogen from n = 2.

Compare: from the ground state (n = 1), 13.6 eV is needed. The first excited state requires much less energy — the electron is already partially free.

---

### Worked Example 4: Maximum Wavelength for Ionization

**Problem:** What is the maximum wavelength of light that can ionize a ground-state hydrogen atom?

**Solution:**

The minimum photon energy needed = 13.6 eV = 13.6 × 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J

Maximum wavelength (minimum energy):

```
λ_max = hc / E_min = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.0 × 10⁸) / (2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸)
λ_max = 1.988 × 10⁻²⁵ / 2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸
λ_max = 9.12 × 10⁻⁸ m = 91.2 nm
```

**Answer:** 91.2 nm — in the far ultraviolet. Any photon with λ < 91.2 nm has enough energy to ionize ground-state hydrogen. This is called the **Lyman limit**, important in astrophysics.

## 13. The Quantum Mechanical Model

### Why Bohr's Model Was Not Enough

Bohr's model was a triumph for hydrogen. It explained the spectral lines perfectly. But it had serious problems:

1. It could NOT explain spectra of multi-electron atoms (helium, lithium, etc.)
2. It could NOT explain why electrons didn't radiate in "allowed" orbits (it just asserted they don't)
3. It could NOT explain the relative intensities of spectral lines
4. It treated electrons as classical particles following definite orbits — which the Heisenberg uncertainty principle says is impossible

The full solution required **quantum mechanics**, developed by Heisenberg, Schrödinger, Dirac, and others in the 1920s.

### De Broglie's Insight (1924)

Louis de Broglie proposed that electrons (and all matter) have **wave-like properties**:

```
de Broglie wavelength:

  λ = h / (m × v) = h / p

where p = momentum
```

Bohr's allowed orbits are exactly the orbits where the electron wave fits an **integer number of wavelengths** around the circle. This explains WHY only certain orbits are allowed — they are the resonant modes of the electron wave.

```
Allowed orbit condition:

  Circumference = n × λ_electron

  2π × r = n × (h / mv)

  This is exactly Bohr's quantization condition!
```

### Schrödinger's Wave Equation (1926)

Erwin Schrödinger wrote a wave equation for electrons in atoms. Solving it gives **wavefunctions** (ψ) — mathematical functions that describe the quantum state of the electron.

The key insight: **|ψ|²** at any point gives the **probability of finding the electron there**.

```
Electron in ground state (n=1) of hydrogen:

  Top view:

         . . . . .
       . . . . . . .
      . . . . . . . .
      . . . [*] . . .   ← highest probability near nucleus
      . . . . . . . .
       . . . . . . .
         . . . . .

  Each dot represents probability density.
  The electron is NOT at a fixed location.
  It's spread out as a probability cloud.
```

There are NO fixed circular orbits. Instead, there are **orbitals** — regions of space where the electron is likely to be found.

### Quantum Numbers

The quantum mechanical treatment of hydrogen requires **four quantum numbers** to fully specify the state of an electron:

```
1. n  — principal quantum number    (n = 1, 2, 3, ...)
         determines the main energy level and size of orbital

2. l  — angular momentum quantum    (l = 0, 1, 2, ..., n-1)
         determines the SHAPE of the orbital
         l=0 → s orbital (spherical)
         l=1 → p orbital (dumbbell)
         l=2 → d orbital (cloverleaf)
         l=3 → f orbital (complex)

3. m_l — magnetic quantum number    (m_l = -l, ..., 0, ..., +l)
          determines ORIENTATION of orbital in space

4. m_s — spin quantum number        (m_s = +½ or -½)
          electron "spin" — intrinsic angular momentum
          each orbital can hold at most 2 electrons (↑ and ↓ spin)
```

### Pauli Exclusion Principle

**No two electrons in an atom can have the same set of all four quantum numbers.**

This means each orbital holds at most 2 electrons (one spin up, one spin down). This principle, discovered by Wolfgang Pauli in 1925, is responsible for the structure of the periodic table and the stability of matter.

```
        **       **

Each s orbital can hold 2 electrons.
```

## 15. Electron Configuration and the Periodic Table

### Filling Order (Aufbau Principle)

Electrons fill orbitals starting from the lowest energy, following this sequence:

```
Filling order:
1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d → 4p → 5s → 4d → 5p → 6s → 4f → 5d → 6p → ...

Memory aid (diagonal rule):

    1s
    2s  2p
    3s  3p  3d
    4s  4p  4d  4f
    5s  5p  5d  5f
    
    Fill diagonally from top-right to bottom-left:
    1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, ...
```

### Examples

```
Element    Z    Configuration
--------   --   ------------------------------------------
Hydrogen   1    1s¹
Helium     2    1s²
Lithium    3    1s² 2s¹
Carbon     6    1s² 2s² 2p²
Neon      10    1s² 2s² 2p⁶         (full second shell — noble gas)
Sodium    11    1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹     (like Li, one s electron in new shell)
Argon     18    1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ (full third shell — noble gas)
Iron      26    1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s² 3d⁶
```

### Why Electron Configuration Explains Chemistry

The **outermost electrons** (valence electrons) determine chemical behavior:
- Alkali metals (Li, Na, K): one valence electron → very reactive, form +1 ions
- Halogens (F, Cl, Br): seven valence electrons → want one more → form -1 ions, react with metals
- Noble gases (He, Ne, Ar): full outer shell → unreactive
- Transition metals (Fe, Cu, etc.): partly filled d orbitals → variable oxidation states, colorful compounds

## 16. Ionization Energy

### Definition

The **ionization energy** is the minimum energy required to remove one electron completely from an isolated, ground-state atom.

```
For hydrogen (n=1):

  E_ionization = 0 - (-13.6) = 13.6 eV = 2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J
```

For multi-electron atoms, the first ionization energy is the energy to remove the outermost electron.

### Trends in the Periodic Table

```
Across a period (left → right):
  Ionization energy INCREASES
  Reason: more protons pull electrons more tightly
  (nuclear charge increases, atomic radius decreases)

Down a group (top → bottom):
  Ionization energy DECREASES
  Reason: outer electrons are in higher shells,
          farther from nucleus and shielded by inner electrons

First Ionization Energy (approximate, in eV):

  H   13.6  | Li  5.4  | Na  5.1  | K   4.3
  He  24.6  | Be  9.3  | Mg  7.6  | Ca  6.1
            | B   8.3  | Al  6.0
            | C   11.3 | Si  8.2
            | N   14.5 | P   10.5
            | O   13.6 | S   10.4
            | F   17.4 | Cl  13.0
            | Ne  21.6 | Ar  15.8
```

**Noble gases** have the highest ionization energies in their periods — their filled shells are very stable.

**Alkali metals** have the lowest — their single outer electron is easily removed.

The second ionization energy (removing the second electron) is always much higher than the first, because you are removing a core electron from an already-positive ion.

## 17. Applications of Spectroscopy

### 1. Identifying Elements in Stars (Stellar Spectroscopy)

When starlight is passed through a spectrometer, the Fraunhofer absorption lines reveal which elements are present in the star's outer atmosphere. This is how we know:

- The Sun is 70% hydrogen, 28% helium, with trace amounts of carbon, oxygen, iron, and others
- Distant galaxies are made of the same elements as local matter
- The universe has a uniform chemical composition at large scales

In 1868, helium was discovered in the Sun's spectrum **before it was found on Earth**. The dark line at 587.6 nm didn't match any known element — it was later found to be a new element, named for Helios (the Greek Sun god).

### 2. Atomic Clocks

**Cesium-133 atomic clocks** use the microwave transition between two hyperfine energy levels of cesium-133 atoms. The transition has a frequency of 9,192,631,770 Hz — defined as exactly 1 second since 1967.

```
Accuracy: ~1 second error per 300 million years

Used in:
- GPS satellites (your phone position depends on atomic clock precision)
- Internet time servers
- Financial transaction timestamps
- Scientific measurements
```

### 3. Neon Signs and Gas Discharge Lamps

```
Type of lamp  | Gas     | Color
--------------|---------|------------------
"Neon" sign   | Neon    | Red-orange
Blue sign     | Argon   | Blue-purple
Yellow lamp   | Sodium  | Yellow (589 nm)
Fluorescent   | Mercury + phosphor | White
Energy-saving | Mercury + various phosphors | Various
```

When electricity passes through a gas tube, electrons collide with gas atoms, exciting them. They emit their characteristic spectral colors when falling back to lower levels.

### 4. Lasers (Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation)

A **laser** exploits **stimulated emission** — when a photon encounters an already-excited atom with the right energy gap, it can trigger the atom to emit an identical photon (same frequency, phase, direction). With a large population of excited atoms (population inversion) and mirrors to bounce light back and forth, this produces an intense, coherent, monochromatic beam.

```
Laser Action:

  1. Pump energy in → excite atoms to upper level
  2. Photon triggers stimulated emission
  3. Two identical photons → each triggers two more
  4. Chain reaction: exponential light amplification
  5. Output through partially transparent mirror

  [Mirror]←─ hf ─→[excited atoms]←─ hf ─→[Partial mirror]→ LASER BEAM
```

Applications: surgery (cutting tissue), barcode scanners, optical communications (fiber optic cables), CD/DVD players, laser cutting in manufacturing, LIDAR in self-driving cars.

### 5. Fluorescence and Phosphorescence

When an atom or molecule absorbs high-energy photons (UV) and re-emits lower-energy photons (visible), it **fluoresces**. This is why:
- Fluorescent highlighters glow under UV light
- Fluorescent lights work (UV from mercury excites phosphor coating)
- Biological fluorescence microscopy allows scientists to tag specific proteins

### 6. Spectroscopy in Medicine

- **Pulse oximetry**: uses absorption of red and infrared light by hemoglobin to measure blood oxygen saturation non-invasively (the clip on your finger at a hospital)
- **MRI** (Magnetic Resonance Imaging): uses nuclear magnetic resonance of hydrogen nuclei in water — a quantum effect of spin
- **Raman spectroscopy**: identifies molecular structures from how scattered light changes frequency; used in pharmaceuticals, forensics, and art authentication

## 18. Summary

- **Dalton (1803)**: Atoms are solid, indivisible spheres. Each element has characteristic atoms. Atoms combine in whole-number ratios.

- **Thomson (1897)**: Discovered the electron via cathode ray tube experiments. Electrons are negatively charged, present in all matter. Proposed plum pudding model — electrons embedded in diffuse positive charge.

- **Rutherford (1911)**: Gold foil experiment showed most alpha particles pass straight through gold, but some bounce back sharply. Concluded the atom is mostly empty space with a tiny, dense, positive nucleus at the center. Electrons orbit far away.

- **Scale**: Nucleus ~10⁻¹⁵ m; atom ~10⁻¹⁰ m. The atom is 100,000 times larger than its nucleus. Matter is 99.9999% empty space.

- **Bohr (1913)**: Electrons occupy only specific allowed orbits without radiating. Jumping between levels involves absorbing or emitting a photon with energy exactly equal to the level difference: hf = E₂ - E₁.

- **Hydrogen energy levels**: E_n = -13.6 eV / n². Ground state (n=1) is most negative (most tightly bound). Ionization at n = infinity (E = 0).

- **Emission spectrum**: Electrons falling to lower levels emit photons of specific frequencies → bright line spectrum unique to each element.

- **Absorption spectrum**: Cool gas absorbs same frequencies it would emit → dark lines on continuous background. Used to identify elements in stars.

- **Spectral series in hydrogen**: Lyman (UV, to n=1), Balmer (visible, to n=2), Paschen (IR, to n=3).

- **Quantum mechanical model**: Electrons are probability clouds (wavefunctions), not classical particles in orbits. Four quantum numbers (n, l, m_l, m_s) describe each electron's state. Pauli exclusion principle limits two electrons per orbital.

- **Orbitals**: s (spherical), p (dumbbell), d (cloverleaf), f (complex). Each holds 2, 6, 10, 14 electrons respectively.

- **Electron configuration**: Filling order follows Aufbau principle. Determines chemical properties and periodic table trends.

- **Ionization energy**: Increases across a period, decreases down a group. Reflects how tightly outer electrons are held.

- **Applications**: Stellar spectroscopy (star composition), atomic clocks (GPS), neon signs, lasers (stimulated emission), pulse oximetry, fluorescence microscopy.

## 19. Key Equations

```
Hydrogen energy levels (Bohr model):
  E_n = -13.6 / n²   [eV]    or    E_n = -13.6 eV / n²

Photon energy from transition:
  E_photon = E_upper - E_lower    (always positive)
  E_photon = hf = hc / λ

Planck's constant:
  h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s
  hc = 1240 eV·nm   (useful shortcut)

Photon frequency and wavelength:
  c = f × λ
  f = E_photon / h
  λ = hc / E_photon = 1240 eV·nm / E_photon(eV)

Bohr radius (smallest orbit in hydrogen):
  a₀ = 5.29 × 10⁻¹¹ m = 0.0529 nm

Allowed radii in hydrogen:
  r_n = n² × a₀

de Broglie wavelength:
  λ = h / p = h / (mv)

Ionization energy of hydrogen from ground state:
  E_ion = 13.6 eV

Hydrogen ionization from level n:
  E_ion(n) = 13.6 / n²   [eV]

Key constants:
  Speed of light: c = 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s
  Electron charge: e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C
  Electron mass: m_e = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg
  1 eV = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
```
