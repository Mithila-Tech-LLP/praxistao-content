## Introduction: What Is Energy?

You have been using the word "energy" your whole life. You say you have no energy on a sleepy Monday morning. You read that a power plant generates energy. You hear that food has a certain number of calories of energy. But what does energy actually mean in physics?

In physics, **energy** is defined as the ability to do **work**. Work, as we saw in earlier chapters, means pushing or pulling something through a distance. If you have a lot of energy, you can do a lot of work. If you have little energy, you can do little work.

Energy is one of the most important ideas in all of science. Every single thing that happens in the universe — a star exploding, a flower growing, a car engine running, your heart beating — involves energy being transformed from one type to another.

### Types of Energy

There are many types of energy in the universe:

```
TYPES OF ENERGY
===============

MECHANICAL ENERGY          NON-MECHANICAL ENERGY
(motion & position)        (other forms)
        |                          |
   _____|_____              _______|_______
  |           |            |       |       |
Kinetic    Potential    Thermal  Chemical  Electrical
Energy      Energy      (heat)  (food/fuel)  (batteries)
(moving)  (stored)
             |
        _____|_____
       |           |
 Gravitational   Elastic
    (height)    (springs)
```

In this chapter we focus on **mechanical energy** — specifically:

1. **Kinetic energy** (KE): energy due to motion
2. **Gravitational potential energy** (GPE or just PE): energy stored due to height
3. **Elastic potential energy**: energy stored in stretched or compressed springs

These three forms are the foundation for understanding how rollercoasters work, how pendulums swing, how bungee cords catch you, and thousands of other everyday phenomena.

## Kinetic Energy: The Energy of Motion

Any object that is moving has **kinetic energy**. The word "kinetic" comes from the Greek word "kinesis" meaning motion.

Think about these everyday examples:
- A moving car has kinetic energy (and a crash proves it has a lot)
- A flying baseball has kinetic energy (it can break a window)
- A running child has kinetic energy (they can knock over a vase)
- A rolling boulder has kinetic energy (stay out of the way!)

The amount of kinetic energy depends on two things:
1. **Mass** — heavier objects have more kinetic energy at the same speed
2. **Speed** — faster objects have more kinetic energy at the same mass

A truck and a bicycle moving at the same speed: the truck has far more kinetic energy.
Two identical cars, one going twice as fast as the other: the faster car has much more kinetic energy (and is far more dangerous in a crash).

### The Formula for Kinetic Energy

The formula for kinetic energy is:

```
KE = ½ × m × v²

Where:
  KE = kinetic energy, measured in Joules (J)
  m  = mass of the object, measured in kilograms (kg)
  v  = speed (velocity) of the object, measured in metres per second (m/s)
  v² = v × v (speed squared)
```

The ½ in the formula comes from calculus and the work-energy theorem, but you do not need to worry about that. Just remember: one-half, times mass, times speed squared.

**Example:** A 2 kg ball rolling at 3 m/s has:
```
KE = ½ × 2 × 3²
KE = ½ × 2 × 9
KE = 9 Joules
```

### Why Doubling Speed Quadruples Kinetic Energy

This is one of the most important and surprising facts in physics. Look carefully at the formula:

```
KE = ½ × m × v²
```

The speed is **squared**. This means:

| Speed (m/s) | Speed² | KE (for m = 2 kg) |
|-------------|--------|-------------------|
| 1           | 1      | 1 J               |
| 2           | 4      | 4 J               |
| 3           | 9      | 9 J               |
| 4           | 16     | 16 J              |
| 5           | 25     | 25 J              |

When speed doubles from 1 to 2 m/s, KE goes from 1 J to 4 J — that is **4 times** as much.
When speed triples from 1 to 3 m/s, KE goes from 1 J to 9 J — that is **9 times** as much.

**The rule:** If you multiply speed by n, kinetic energy multiplies by n².

This is why speed limits matter so much for road safety. At 60 km/h, a car has a certain kinetic energy. At 120 km/h (twice the speed), it has **four times** the kinetic energy. The braking distance more than quadruples. A crash at twice the speed is not twice as bad — it is four times as bad.

```
KE vs SPEED (for a 1000 kg car)
================================

Speed      KE
10 m/s --> 50,000 J    [===]
20 m/s --> 200,000 J   [============]
30 m/s --> 450,000 J   [===========================]
40 m/s --> 800,000 J   [================================================]

Notice how each bar grows MUCH faster than the speed increases!
```

### Units of Energy: The Joule

The unit of energy (and work) is the **Joule**, abbreviated **J**, named after English physicist James Prescott Joule.

One Joule is actually quite a small amount of energy:
- Lifting a small apple (100 g) by 1 metre uses about 1 J
- A single heartbeat uses about 1 J
- A 60-watt light bulb uses 60 J every second
- Running up a flight of stairs uses about 2,000 J

For large amounts of energy, we use kilojoules (kJ):
```
1 kJ = 1,000 J
```

The calorie on food labels is actually a kilocalorie:
```
1 food Calorie (Cal) = 4,186 J ≈ 4.2 kJ
```

## Gravitational Potential Energy: Stored Height Energy

**Gravitational potential energy** (often shortened to just **potential energy** or **PE**) is the energy stored in an object because of its position — specifically, how high it is above some reference point.

Think of it like this: when you lift a heavy book above your head, you do work against gravity. That work does not disappear — it is stored in the book as potential energy. When you let go, gravity pulls the book down and that stored energy is released, converting to kinetic energy as the book falls.

Everyday examples:
- Water at the top of a dam has PE (hydroelectric power uses this)
- A boulder at the top of a hill has PE (dangerous if it rolls!)
- A diver on a high board has PE (converts to KE as they fall)
- A stretched spring has elastic PE (we will get to this shortly)

### The Formula for Gravitational Potential Energy

```
PE = m × g × h

Where:
  PE = gravitational potential energy, in Joules (J)
  m  = mass of the object, in kilograms (kg)
  g  = acceleration due to gravity = 9.8 m/s² (approximately 10 m/s² for estimates)
  h  = height above the reference level, in metres (m)
```

**Example:** A 5 kg textbook sitting on a shelf 1.2 m above the floor:
```
PE = 5 × 9.8 × 1.2
PE = 58.8 Joules
```

### Choosing a Reference Level

Notice that PE = mgh requires a height h. But height measured from where?

The answer is: you get to choose! The **reference level** is the height you decide to call h = 0. The most common choice is the floor or the ground, but you can choose anything convenient.

```
CHOOSING A REFERENCE LEVEL
===========================

     [book on shelf]  <-- h = 1.5 m if floor is reference
           |               h = 0.5 m if table is reference
           |               h = -0.5 m if ceiling is reference
     [table top]     <-- h = 1.0 m if floor is reference
           |               h = 0   if table is reference
           |               h = -1.0 m if ceiling is reference
     [floor]         <-- h = 0   if floor is reference (most common)
           |               h = -1.0 m if table is reference
           |               h = -2.0 m if ceiling is reference
     [ground floor]
```

A common rule of thumb: choose the reference level at the lowest point the object reaches. This keeps all heights positive and makes calculations cleaner.

### Why the Reference Level Does Not Matter

Here is a reassuring fact: physics does not depend on which reference level you choose, as long as you are consistent throughout a single problem.

This is because what physically matters is the **change** in height, not the absolute height. When a ball falls from height h₁ to height h₂, the change in PE is:

```
ΔPE = m × g × (h₂ - h₁) = m × g × Δh
```

The reference level cancels out of the calculation. Pick whatever makes the numbers simplest.

## Converting Between KE and PE: Pendulums and Roller Coasters

The most beautiful thing about kinetic and potential energy is how they convert into each other. When an object moves from a higher position to a lower one (in the absence of friction), its PE decreases and its KE increases by exactly the same amount. The total energy stays constant.

```
PE decreases → KE increases (falling / going downhill)
KE decreases → PE increases (rising / going uphill)
```

### The Pendulum

A pendulum is a perfect everyday demonstration of KE ↔ PE conversion.

```
PENDULUM ENERGY EXCHANGE
=========================

    Position:  A        B        C        B        A
               |        |        |        |        |
    Height:   HIGH     MID      LOW      MID     HIGH
               |        |        |        |        |
    PE:       MAX    MEDIUM     MIN    MEDIUM     MAX
    KE:        0     SOME      MAX     SOME        0
    Speed:    ZERO   SOME      FAST    SOME      ZERO

       A\        /A
         \      /
          \    /
    B     \  /     B
           \/
           C
         (lowest point)
```

At point A (either side, highest point):
- The pendulum is momentarily stopped
- All energy is PE
- KE = 0

At point C (bottom, lowest point):
- The pendulum moves fastest
- All energy is KE
- PE = 0 (taking C as reference level)

At point B (halfway):
- Half PE, half KE (roughly)
- Moving at intermediate speed

This exchange continues back and forth forever in an ideal (frictionless) pendulum. In reality, air resistance and friction at the pivot slowly drain away the energy.

### The Roller Coaster

A roller coaster is essentially an enormous energy conversion machine. The motor drags the car to the top of the first hill, doing work and giving the car gravitational PE. From that point on, the car converts PE to KE and back as it goes over hills and through valleys.

```
ROLLER COASTER ENERGY DIAGRAM
==============================

Height
  |
  |   *                              * Peak 3
  |   *  <- Peak 1 (highest)        *
  |    *       * Peak 2             *
  |     *     * *                  * *
  |      *   *   *                *   *
  |       * *     *              *
  |        *       *            *
  |                 *          *
  |                  *        *
  |                   ********
  |
  +-------------------------------------------------> Distance

At Peak 1:  PE is maximum, KE is minimum (but car must be moving)
At Valley:  PE is minimum, KE is maximum (car moves fastest)
At Peak 2:  PE rises again, KE decreases
```

The car always moves fastest at the bottom of a dip (where PE is lowest) and slowest at the top of a hill (where PE is highest).

**Important:** For this to work perfectly (exactly converting PE to KE and back), we must ignore friction. In a real roller coaster, some energy is lost to friction and air resistance as heat, so the car cannot reach heights quite as high as the first peak.

## Elastic Potential Energy: Energy Stored in Springs

Springs are not just in mattresses and pens — they are everywhere in engineering and physics. When you compress or stretch a spring, you store energy in it. This is called **elastic potential energy**.

Examples of elastic potential energy:
- A compressed spring in a pinball machine
- A stretched rubber band about to be released
- A bent bow (in archery) about to launch an arrow
- The spring in a wind-up toy
- Shock absorbers in a car
- A trampoline as you press down on it

### Hooke's Law

Before we can find the elastic PE, we need to understand how springs work. This is described by **Hooke's Law**, discovered by English scientist Robert Hooke in 1678.

**Hooke's Law** states: the force exerted by a spring is proportional to how much it has been stretched or compressed from its natural (resting) length.

```
F = -k × x

Where:
  F = force exerted BY the spring (in Newtons, N)
  k = spring constant (in N/m) — how stiff the spring is
  x = displacement from natural length (in metres, m)
      positive x means stretched
      negative x means compressed
  - = the negative sign means the spring pushes/pulls BACK (restoring force)
```

The negative sign means that the spring always tries to return to its natural length:
- If you stretch it (positive x), it pulls back (negative F, meaning back toward zero)
- If you compress it (negative x), it pushes out (positive F, meaning back toward zero)

This is why it is called a **restoring force** — the spring always tries to restore itself to its natural length.

```
HOOKE'S LAW DIAGRAM
====================

Natural length:
|---spring---|
[wall]        [no force on spring]

Stretched:
|---spring---------|   <-- x = stretch distance
[wall]              [object]
                    Arrow <-- (Spring pulls object back left)

Compressed:
|----spring-|       <-- x = compression distance
[wall]      [object]
            Arrow --> (Spring pushes object right)
```

### The Spring Constant k

The **spring constant** k (also called the stiffness constant) tells you how stiff or soft a spring is.

- Large k = stiff spring = needs a large force to stretch it a little
- Small k = soft spring = a small force stretches it a lot

Units of k: Newtons per metre (N/m)

```
SPRING CONSTANT EXAMPLES
=========================

Very soft spring (toy):        k ≈ 10 N/m
Watch spring:                  k ≈ 50-100 N/m
Pen click spring:              k ≈ 100-200 N/m
Car suspension spring:         k ≈ 10,000-30,000 N/m
Heavy-duty industrial spring:  k ≈ 100,000+ N/m
```

To find k experimentally: hang a known weight from the spring and measure how far it stretches.

**Example:** You hang a 500 g (0.5 kg) weight from a spring. It stretches 0.1 m. Find k.

The weight force pulling down = m × g = 0.5 × 9.8 = 4.9 N
At equilibrium, spring force = weight force, so:
```
F = k × x
4.9 = k × 0.1
k = 4.9 / 0.1
k = 49 N/m
```

### The Formula for Spring Potential Energy

When a spring is stretched or compressed by a distance x, the elastic potential energy stored in it is:

```
PE_spring = ½ × k × x²

Where:
  PE_spring = elastic potential energy stored in the spring (Joules)
  k         = spring constant (N/m)
  x         = stretch or compression distance (metres)
  x²        = x × x (distance squared)
```

Notice the ½ again! Just like kinetic energy has ½mv², spring PE has ½kx².

Also notice: x is squared, so it does not matter whether the spring is stretched or compressed — both give positive energy. A spring stores the same energy whether you stretch it 5 cm or compress it 5 cm.

### Compression vs Stretching

```
SPRING ENERGY STORAGE
======================

Compressed by x = 0.1 m, k = 200 N/m:
PE = ½ × 200 × (0.1)² = ½ × 200 × 0.01 = 1 J

Stretched by x = 0.1 m, k = 200 N/m:
PE = ½ × 200 × (0.1)² = ½ × 200 × 0.01 = 1 J

Same energy! The sign of x does not matter.

Compressed by x = 0.2 m (twice as much), k = 200 N/m:
PE = ½ × 200 × (0.2)² = ½ × 200 × 0.04 = 4 J

Four times the energy for twice the compression!
(because x is squared)
```

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Kinetic Energy of a Car

**Question:** A car of mass 1,200 kg is travelling at 20 m/s (about 72 km/h). Calculate its kinetic energy.

**Solution:**

Step 1: Write down what we know.
```
m = 1,200 kg
v = 20 m/s
KE = ?
```

Step 2: Write the formula.
```
KE = ½ × m × v²
```

Step 3: Substitute the values.
```
KE = ½ × 1,200 × 20²
KE = ½ × 1,200 × 400
KE = ½ × 480,000
KE = 240,000 J
KE = 240 kJ
```

**Answer:** The car has 240,000 Joules (240 kJ) of kinetic energy.

**Follow-up:** If the car doubles its speed to 40 m/s, what happens?
```
KE = ½ × 1,200 × 40²
KE = ½ × 1,200 × 1,600
KE = 960,000 J = 960 kJ
```

The kinetic energy went from 240 kJ to 960 kJ — exactly **4 times** as much. Speed doubled, KE quadrupled.

### Example 2: Gravitational PE of a Climber

**Question:** A rock climber of mass 65 kg climbs to a ledge 12 m above the ground. How much gravitational potential energy have they gained? (Use g = 9.8 m/s²)

**Solution:**

Step 1: Write down what we know.
```
m = 65 kg
g = 9.8 m/s²
h = 12 m (taking ground as reference level, h = 0)
PE = ?
```

Step 2: Write the formula.
```
PE = m × g × h
```

Step 3: Substitute.
```
PE = 65 × 9.8 × 12
PE = 65 × 117.6
PE = 7,644 J
PE ≈ 7,644 J ≈ 7.64 kJ
```

**Answer:** The climber has gained approximately 7,644 Joules of gravitational PE.

**Interpretation:** This is the same as the work done by the climber against gravity to reach that height. If the climber falls (in a very bad scenario), all this PE converts to KE just before hitting the ground. This is why falling from heights is so dangerous.

### Example 3: Roller Coaster at the Bottom

**Question:** A roller coaster car has a mass of 500 kg. It starts from rest at the top of a hill 20 m high. Ignoring friction, how fast is it moving at the bottom of the hill?

**Solution:**

This is an energy conversion problem. At the top: all PE. At the bottom: all KE.

```
Energy at top = Energy at bottom
PE_top        = KE_bottom
```

Step 1: Calculate PE at the top.
```
PE = m × g × h = 500 × 9.8 × 20 = 98,000 J
```

Step 2: Set PE equal to KE at the bottom.
```
KE = 98,000 J
½ × m × v² = 98,000
½ × 500 × v² = 98,000
250 × v² = 98,000
v² = 98,000 / 250
v² = 392
v = √392
v ≈ 19.8 m/s
```

**Answer:** The roller coaster car is moving at approximately 19.8 m/s (about 71 km/h) at the bottom of the hill.

```
ENERGY DIAGRAM FOR ROLLER COASTER
===================================

     [Top of Hill]                    [Bottom]
         *
         *   h = 20 m                    *
         *                               *
         *                               *
         |                               |
         | PE = 98,000 J                 | KE = 98,000 J
         | KE = 0 J                      | PE = 0 J
         |                               |
         |<-------- Total Energy = 98,000 J throughout -------->|
```

### Example 4: Spring Compression

**Question:** A spring has a spring constant of k = 400 N/m. How much elastic potential energy is stored in the spring when it is compressed by 15 cm?

**Solution:**

Step 1: Convert cm to metres.
```
x = 15 cm = 0.15 m
```

Step 2: Write down all values.
```
k = 400 N/m
x = 0.15 m
PE = ?
```

Step 3: Use the spring PE formula.
```
PE = ½ × k × x²
PE = ½ × 400 × (0.15)²
PE = ½ × 400 × 0.0225
PE = ½ × 9
PE = 4.5 J
```

**Answer:** The spring stores 4.5 Joules of elastic potential energy.

**Follow-up check:** What force is needed to hold it at this compression?
```
F = k × x = 400 × 0.15 = 60 N
```
About 60 N of force (equivalent to holding a 6 kg mass) is needed to keep the spring compressed.

## Energy Diagrams

Energy diagrams are a powerful way to visualise how KE and PE change throughout a motion. The total energy stays constant, but the split between KE and PE changes.

```
ENERGY BAR DIAGRAM: Ball Thrown Upward
========================================

[At launch]        [Halfway up]       [At peak]
                                       
PE  |               PE  |####|         PE  |#########|
    |                   |    |             |         |
KE  |#########|      KE |####|         KE |         |
    |         |          |   |             |         |
    +---------+          +---+             +---------+
    Total = E            Total = E         Total = E

[Halfway down]     [Just before landing]
                                       
PE  |####|          PE  |         |
    |    |              |         |
KE  |####|          KE  |#########|
    |    |              |         |
    +----+              +---------+
    Total = E           Total = E
```

Notice:
- When PE is maximum (at peak), KE is zero (momentarily stopped)
- When KE is maximum (at launch and just before landing), PE is zero (at ground level)
- Total energy E (represented by total bar height) never changes

```
ENERGY vs HEIGHT GRAPH: Object Falling
========================================

Energy
  ^
  |
  |  *  *  *  *  *  *  *  *  *  *    <-- Total Energy (constant)
  |  
  |  *                          *    <-- PE (decreases as height falls)
  |     *                    *
  |        *              *
  |           *         *
  |              *   *
  |                 *
  |                                  <-- KE (zero at start, max at bottom)
  |                 *
  |              *     *
  |           *           *
  |        *                 *
  |     *                       *
  |  *                             *
  +-----------------------------------------> Height decreases -->
```

The two lines (PE and KE) are mirror images of each other. They always add up to the same total.

## Common Misconceptions

**Misconception 1:** "A stationary object at height h has no energy."
**Correction:** It has no kinetic energy, but it has gravitational potential energy. Potential energy is real — drop the object and watch it convert to KE.

**Misconception 2:** "Heavier objects fall faster."
**Correction:** All objects fall at the same acceleration (g = 9.8 m/s²) in the absence of air resistance. However, heavier objects do have more KE at any given speed.

**Misconception 3:** "If speed doubles, KE doubles."
**Correction:** KE quadruples when speed doubles. Remember: KE = ½mv², so speed is squared. This is extremely important for road safety.

**Misconception 4:** "The reference level for PE must be the ground."
**Correction:** You can choose any reference level. What matters is the change in height (Δh), not the absolute height. Physicists choose whatever is most convenient.

**Misconception 5:** "Springs only store energy when stretched, not compressed."
**Correction:** Springs store energy both when stretched and compressed. The formula PE = ½kx² uses x², which is always positive regardless of direction.

## Summary

- **Energy** is the ability to do work, measured in **Joules (J)**

- **Kinetic energy (KE)** is the energy of motion:
  - KE = ½ × m × v²
  - Depends on mass and the SQUARE of speed
  - Doubling speed QUADRUPLES kinetic energy

- **Gravitational potential energy (PE)** is stored energy due to height:
  - PE = m × g × h
  - Depends on mass, gravitational acceleration (g = 9.8 m/s²), and height
  - The reference level (h = 0) can be chosen freely

- **Elastic potential energy** is energy stored in stretched/compressed springs:
  - PE_spring = ½ × k × x²
  - k is the spring constant (stiffness), measured in N/m
  - x is the displacement from natural length
  - x is squared, so both stretching and compressing store positive energy

- **Hooke's Law** gives the force a spring exerts:
  - F = -k × x
  - The negative sign means it is a restoring force (always pulls/pushes back toward natural length)

- **KE and PE convert into each other** in systems like pendulums and roller coasters:
  - Going down: PE converts to KE (speed increases)
  - Going up: KE converts to PE (speed decreases)
  - The total energy remains constant (in the absence of friction)

## Key Equations

```
KINETIC ENERGY
  KE = ½ × m × v²
  KE in Joules (J)
  m in kilograms (kg)
  v in metres per second (m/s)

GRAVITATIONAL POTENTIAL ENERGY
  PE = m × g × h
  PE in Joules (J)
  m in kilograms (kg)
  g = 9.8 m/s² (gravitational acceleration)
  h in metres (m), measured from chosen reference level

ELASTIC POTENTIAL ENERGY (SPRING)
  PE_spring = ½ × k × x²
  PE in Joules (J)
  k = spring constant in Newtons per metre (N/m)
  x = displacement from natural length in metres (m)

HOOKE'S LAW
  F = -k × x
  F in Newtons (N)
  k = spring constant in N/m
  x = displacement in metres (m)
  (negative sign = restoring force)

ENERGY CONVERSION (no friction)
  PE_lost = KE_gained
  m × g × Δh = ½ × m × v²  (falling objects)
  g × Δh = ½ × v²           (mass cancels!)
```
