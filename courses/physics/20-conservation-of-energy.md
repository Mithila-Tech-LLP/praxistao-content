## Introduction: The Most Powerful Law in Physics

Imagine you are watching a rollercoaster. The car climbs the first big hill slowly, then rushes down, screaming through a loop, climbing another hill, dipping into a valley. At every moment, something is happening to the car's energy.

Now ask: how do you predict how fast the car is moving at any point on the track? You could try to track every force, every acceleration, every instant of motion. That would be incredibly complicated. Or — you could use one of the most powerful tools in all of physics:

**The Law of Conservation of Energy.**

This law is so fundamental that Nobel Prize-winning physicist Richard Feynman called it one of the "most elegant results in all of physics." It allows you to solve problems that would be practically impossible using Newton's laws alone.

In this chapter, you will learn:
- What "conservation" means in physics (hint: it does not mean recycling)
- Why total energy never disappears
- When mechanical energy is conserved and when it is not
- How to use energy conservation to solve real problems

---

## The Law of Conservation of Energy

The **Law of Conservation of Energy** states:

> **Energy cannot be created or destroyed. It can only be transformed from one form to another. The total amount of energy in the universe remains constant.**

This is sometimes stated more simply as:

> **The total energy of an isolated system remains constant.**

An **isolated system** means one where no energy enters or leaves from outside. In practice, we often focus on a smaller system and track all the energy coming in and going out.

```
THE LAW OF CONSERVATION OF ENERGY
====================================

BEFORE any event:          AFTER any event:
Total Energy = E           Total Energy = E  (same!)

    [KE] + [PE] + [Heat] + [Chemical] + [etc.]
              =
    [KE] + [PE] + [Heat] + [Chemical] + [etc.]

Nothing is created. Nothing is destroyed.
Only the FORM of energy changes.
```

This law was established through hundreds of years of experiments. No one has ever found an exception. Not once. Not ever. It is one of the most thoroughly tested ideas in all of science.

## Total Mechanical Energy

In mechanics (the study of motion), we usually deal with just two forms of energy:
- **Kinetic energy (KE)**: energy of motion
- **Potential energy (PE)**: stored energy (gravitational or elastic)

**Total mechanical energy (E)** is the sum of these two:

```
E = KE + PE
E = (½ × m × v²) + (m × g × h)
```

### When Total Mechanical Energy Is Conserved

In some situations, the total mechanical energy stays constant throughout the motion. This happens when:

1. There is **no friction** (or friction is negligible)
2. There is **no air resistance** (or it is negligible)
3. No other forces do work on the system (like a motor pushing the object)

These conditions describe what physicists call an **ideal** or **frictionless** system.

### The Golden Rule

In a frictionless system:

```
E_total = KE + PE = constant (does not change)

This means:
KE₁ + PE₁ = KE₂ + PE₂

(energy at point 1) = (energy at point 2)

Which gives us:
½mv₁² + mgh₁ = ½mv₂² + mgh₂
```

This is the equation you will use over and over again to solve energy problems. It says: no matter where the object is in its journey, the sum of its KE and PE is always the same.

## Energy Transformations

Even when mechanical energy is not conserved (because friction is present), the total energy of the universe still is. Energy does not vanish — it just changes form.

### Common Everyday Transformations

```
ENERGY TRANSFORMATION MAP
==========================

Chemical energy (petrol) 
    --> Thermal energy (hot gases in engine)
    --> Mechanical energy (moving pistons / car motion)
    --> Kinetic energy (car moving down road)
    --> Thermal energy (brakes heat up when slowing down)

Gravitational PE (water at top of dam)
    --> Kinetic energy (water rushing down)
    --> Mechanical energy (turbine spinning)
    --> Electrical energy (generator)
    --> Light energy (your light bulb)
    --> Thermal energy (bulb gets warm)

Chemical energy (food you eat)
    --> Thermal energy (body heat)
    --> Mechanical energy (muscles moving)
    --> Kinetic energy (you running)
    --> Gravitational PE (you climbing stairs)
    --> Thermal energy again (friction with stairs)
```

Notice something: energy transformations often end up as **thermal energy (heat)**. Friction almost always converts mechanical energy into heat. This is unavoidable. We will come back to this soon.

## When Mechanical Energy IS Conserved

Let us look at three classic situations where we assume friction is absent (or negligible), so mechanical energy is conserved.

### The Ideal Pendulum

A pendulum swinging back and forth is the classic demonstration.

```
PENDULUM: ENERGY AT FIVE POSITIONS
=====================================

Position:  1(left)   2(left mid)   3(bottom)   4(right mid)   5(right)

                     1                                   5
                    /|\                                 /|\
Height:          h /   \                             h /   \
                  /     \                             /     \
              2  /       \  --> 4                    /
              h/2         \h/2
                           \
                            3 (reference: h=0)

At position 1 (or 5):
  KE = 0         (momentarily stopped)
  PE = mgh       (maximum)
  Total E = mgh

At position 3 (bottom):
  KE = ½mv²      (maximum, moving fastest)
  PE = 0         (at reference level)
  Total E = ½mv²

At position 2 (or 4):
  KE = some      (partway between 0 and max)
  PE = mgh/2     (halfway up)
  Total E = same

Conservation law connects positions 1 and 3:
  mgh = ½mv²
  Rearranging: v = √(2gh)
```

Using conservation of energy, we can find the speed at the bottom without knowing anything about the forces during the swing — just the height dropped. Beautiful!

### The Ideal Roller Coaster

Same principle, different shape.

```
ROLLER COASTER CONSERVATION OF ENERGY
=======================================

   Height h₁                  Height h₂         Height h₃
     (40 m)                    (15 m)              (25 m)
        *
        *
         *                       *
          *        h = 0 ref   *   *               *
           *       (valley)   *     *             * *
            *                *       *           *
             *              *         *         *
              **************           *********
              
              A                 B           C

Point A: height = 40 m, starting at rest (v = 0)
  KE_A = 0
  PE_A = m × g × 40 = 40mg
  Total E = 40mg

Point B: height = 0 m (valley)
  PE_B = 0
  KE_B = Total E = 40mg
  v_B = ?   -->  ½mv² = 40mg  -->  v² = 80g  -->  v = √(80×9.8) ≈ 28 m/s

Point C: height = 25 m
  PE_C = m × g × 25 = 25mg
  KE_C = Total E - PE_C = 40mg - 25mg = 15mg
  v_C = ?  -->  ½mv² = 15mg  -->  v² = 30g  -->  v = √(30×9.8) ≈ 17.1 m/s
```

Key insight: the mass cancels out! The speed at any point depends only on the height, not the mass of the object. A heavy roller coaster car and a light one both move at the same speed at the same height (if we ignore friction).

### A Ball Thrown Upward

```
BALL THROWN UPWARD
===================

   Peak height h
        *          KE = 0,   PE = mgh
        *
        *
        *          KE = ½,   PE = ½mgh  (halfway up)
        *
        *
    Launch point   KE = max, PE = 0
        *
   [Ground level] h = 0

The ball decelerates going up (KE → PE)
The ball accelerates going down (PE → KE)
At the peak: all energy is PE, momentarily stopped
At the bottom: all energy is KE

Using energy conservation:
KE_bottom = PE_top
½mv² = mgh
v = √(2gh)   for velocity at bottom given drop height h
h = v²/(2g)  for max height given launch speed v
```

## When Mechanical Energy Is NOT Conserved

In the real world, friction is almost always present. When friction acts, mechanical energy is **not** conserved — some of it is converted to thermal energy (heat).

### Friction Converts Mechanical Energy to Heat

When two surfaces slide against each other, friction converts kinetic energy into heat. The surfaces warm up slightly. This energy has not been destroyed — it has just changed form and is now much harder to recover.

```
WITH FRICTION: Energy bookkeeping
==================================

         KE + PE (mechanical)
              |
              | Friction acts
              |
              v
    KE + PE + Heat (total energy)
    
The total is still conserved!
But the MECHANICAL part (KE + PE) has decreased.
The difference has gone into HEAT.

Mathematically:
KE₁ + PE₁ = KE₂ + PE₂ + W_friction

Where W_friction = work done by friction (energy lost to heat)
```

**Example:** A block slides down a ramp. At the top: KE = 0, PE = 100 J. At the bottom: KE = 70 J, PE = 0. Where did the other 30 J go?
Answer: 30 J was converted to heat by friction between the block and the ramp. The total energy is still 100 J — you just cannot see the 30 J anymore because it is spread out as heat.

### Air Resistance

Air resistance acts similarly to friction. As an object moves through air, it pushes air molecules aside. This requires energy. That energy comes from the object's mechanical energy and ends up as heat in the air (and the object).

```
NO air resistance:
Ball dropped from 10 m --> hits ground at v = √(2×9.8×10) = 14 m/s

WITH air resistance:
Ball dropped from 10 m --> hits ground at v < 14 m/s
(some mechanical energy lost to air resistance / heat)
```

For slow, heavy objects over short distances, air resistance is small enough to ignore. For fast objects (bullets, skydivers, aeroplanes), it is very significant.

### Inelastic Collisions

When objects collide and stick together (or crumple), a lot of mechanical energy is converted to:
- Heat (from friction between crumpling surfaces)
- Sound (the bang of a crash)
- Permanent deformation (bending metal stores some energy)

```
CAR CRASH
==========

Before crash:    KE = large (both cars moving)
                 PE = 0

After crash:     KE = small (crumpled cars barely moving)
                 PE = 0
                 Heat = some (warm metals)
                 Sound = some (the crash noise)
                 Deformation = some (crumpled metal)

Total energy before = Total energy after
BUT mechanical energy is NOT conserved.
```

This is actually by design! Car engineers deliberately make cars crumple in a crash. Crumpling absorbs energy (converts KE to deformation energy), reducing the kinetic energy that would otherwise be transmitted to the passengers. Crumple zones save lives.

## The Ski Jump Example

Let us walk through a complete analysis of a ski jump, which involves going downhill (PE → KE), launching off a ramp, flying through the air, and landing.

```
SKI JUMP OVERVIEW
==================

                      * Take-off point
                     *  (height = 30 m)
                    *
                   *
                  *
                 *
                *
               *
              *
     --------*--------  (ground level, h = 0)
     
     At top of run:    height = 80 m, v ≈ 0
     At take-off:      height = 30 m, v = ?
     At landing:       height = 0 m,  v = ?
```

**Step 1:** Find speed at take-off (height drops from 80 m to 30 m, so Δh = 50 m)

Using energy conservation (ignoring friction):
```
KE at take-off = PE lost
½mv² = mgΔh
½v² = gΔh
v² = 2 × g × Δh
v² = 2 × 9.8 × 50
v² = 980
v = √980 ≈ 31.3 m/s   (about 113 km/h!)
```

**Step 2:** Find speed at landing (height drops from 80 m all the way to 0 m, so Δh = 80 m)

```
v² = 2 × g × Δh
v² = 2 × 9.8 × 80
v² = 1,568
v = √1568 ≈ 39.6 m/s   (about 143 km/h!)
```

This shows why landing correctly is critical — ski jumpers hit the ground at about 140 km/h!

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Pendulum Swing

**Question:** A pendulum bob has a mass of 0.2 kg. It is pulled to one side so that it is 0.3 m higher than its lowest point, then released. What is its speed at the lowest point? (g = 9.8 m/s², ignore air resistance)

**Solution:**

Step 1: Identify what we know.
```
m = 0.2 kg
h = 0.3 m (height above lowest point)
v_bottom = ?
Taking the lowest point as reference level (h = 0 there)
```

Step 2: Apply conservation of energy.
At the top: all energy is PE, no KE (starts from rest).
At the bottom: all energy is KE, no PE.

```
PE_top = KE_bottom
m × g × h = ½ × m × v²
```

Step 3: Notice the mass m appears on both sides — it cancels!
```
g × h = ½ × v²
9.8 × 0.3 = ½ × v²
2.94 = 0.5 × v²
v² = 2.94 / 0.5
v² = 5.88
v = √5.88
v ≈ 2.42 m/s
```

**Answer:** The pendulum reaches a speed of approximately 2.42 m/s at the lowest point.

**Note:** The mass did not matter! A heavier or lighter bob at the same height will reach the same speed at the bottom. This makes intuitive sense: heavier bobs have more PE, but also more inertia (KE formula also has m), so they balance out perfectly.

### Example 2: Ski Jump Speed

**Question:** A skier of mass 70 kg starts from rest at the top of a ski jump ramp, which is 25 m above the bottom of the ramp. Calculate:
(a) The skier's speed at the bottom of the ramp (ignore friction)
(b) How high the skier would need to be to reach 30 m/s at the bottom

**Solution (a):**

```
m = 70 kg
h = 25 m
v₀ = 0 (starts from rest)
v = ?

Using conservation of energy:
PE_top = KE_bottom
mgh = ½mv²
gh = ½v²      (mass cancels)
v² = 2gh
v² = 2 × 9.8 × 25
v² = 490
v = √490
v ≈ 22.1 m/s  (about 80 km/h)
```

**Solution (b):**

To reach v = 30 m/s, what height h is needed?

```
v² = 2gh
30² = 2 × 9.8 × h
900 = 19.6 × h
h = 900 / 19.6
h ≈ 45.9 m
```

The skier would need to start from a height of about 46 m — nearly twice as high!

(This shows again why the v² relationship matters: to go from 22 m/s to 30 m/s, you need nearly twice the height.)

### Example 3: Friction Steals Energy

**Question:** A 2 kg box slides down a 5-metre ramp inclined at 30°. The ramp is 2.5 m tall (vertical height). At the bottom, the box is measured to be moving at 4 m/s. How much energy was lost to friction?

**Solution:**

Step 1: Find the total mechanical energy at the start.
```
At the top: KE = 0 (starts at rest), PE = mgh = 2 × 9.8 × 2.5 = 49 J
Total energy at start = 49 J
```

Step 2: Find the total mechanical energy at the end.
```
At the bottom: PE = 0 (at reference level), KE = ½mv² = ½ × 2 × 4² = ½ × 2 × 16 = 16 J
Total mechanical energy at end = 16 J
```

Step 3: Find energy lost to friction.
```
Energy lost = Energy at start - Energy at end
Energy lost = 49 - 16
Energy lost = 33 J
```

**Answer:** 33 Joules were lost to friction (converted to heat between box and ramp surface).

**Check:** This makes sense. The box started with 49 J but ended with only 16 J. The 33 J "missing" became heat — you could detect it as a slight warming of the box and ramp surfaces.

```
ENERGY BAR DIAGRAM FOR THIS EXAMPLE
======================================

       [Start]         [End]
       h=2.5m          h=0m

  49J |####|          |####| <- KE = 16 J
      |####|          |####|
  30J |####|          |####| +
      |####|          |    | <- Heat = 33 J
  16J |####|          |~~~~|
      |####|          |~~~~|
      PE  KE          PE  KE  Heat
     (49J)(0)        (0)(16J)(33J)

Total bars: 49 J both times (if you include heat!)
Mechanical bars: 49 J → 16 J (decreased by friction)
```

### Example 4: The Bouncing Ball

**Question:** A rubber ball of mass 0.1 kg is dropped from a height of 2.0 m. It bounces back up to a height of 1.6 m. 
(a) How much energy was lost in the bounce?
(b) What percentage of energy was lost?
(c) Where did the lost energy go?

**Solution (a):**

Energy before bounce (just before hitting ground):
```
KE = PE_initial = mgh₁ = 0.1 × 9.8 × 2.0 = 1.96 J
```

Energy after bounce (at top of second rise):
```
PE₂ = mgh₂ = 0.1 × 9.8 × 1.6 = 1.568 J
```

Energy lost:
```
ΔE = 1.96 - 1.568 = 0.392 J ≈ 0.39 J
```

**Solution (b):**

```
Percentage lost = (energy lost / initial energy) × 100
Percentage lost = (0.392 / 1.96) × 100
Percentage lost = 20%
```

**Solution (c):**

The 20% lost energy went into:
- **Thermal energy (heat)**: the ball and floor warmed very slightly from the impact
- **Sound energy**: you heard a "thud" — that sound carried some energy away
- **Deformation energy**: the ball briefly squashed and un-squashed; not all this energy is recovered

```
BOUNCE ENERGY DIAGRAM
======================

Before drop:
  PE = 1.96 J  (all PE at height 2.0 m)
  KE = 0

Just before bounce:
  PE = 0
  KE = 1.96 J (all converted to KE)

Just after bounce:
  PE = 0
  KE = 1.568 J (some lost in impact)
  Heat + Sound = 0.392 J

At top of second bounce:
  PE = 1.568 J
  KE = 0

Each successive bounce is 80% of the height before it.
```

## Conservation of Total Energy vs Mechanical Energy

It is vital to understand the difference between these two:

```
TWO IMPORTANT STATEMENTS
==========================

Statement 1 (ALWAYS TRUE):
"The total energy of the universe is conserved."

  This means: KE + PE + Heat + Chemical + Electrical + ... = constant
  Friction does NOT violate this law.
  Nothing ever violates this law.

Statement 2 (SOMETIMES TRUE):
"The total MECHANICAL energy (KE + PE) is conserved."

  This is only true when:
    - No friction
    - No air resistance  
    - No non-conservative forces doing work

  Friction DOES violate this (because it converts mechanical → heat)
```

**A helpful analogy:** Imagine you have £100. You can exchange pounds for dollars, euros, yen, and so on. The total wealth is always £100 worth, just in different currencies. But if some of your money becomes "thermal energy dollars" — heat you cannot recover — then the useful mechanical energy decreases, even though total energy is still conserved.

## Common Misconceptions

**Misconception 1:** "Friction destroys energy."
**Correction:** Friction converts mechanical energy into thermal energy (heat). Total energy is always conserved. Friction does not create or destroy energy — it just changes its form.

**Misconception 2:** "An object at the bottom of a hill has no energy."
**Correction:** At the bottom of a hill, potential energy is zero (if we take the bottom as reference). But kinetic energy may be at its maximum. Total mechanical energy is not zero.

**Misconception 3:** "Heavier objects on a roller coaster go faster."
**Correction:** In the absence of friction, speed depends only on height lost, not mass (mass cancels in the equation). Friction does make mass somewhat relevant — heavier objects experience more friction force, but they also have more energy to start with. In most cases, it roughly balances.

**Misconception 4:** "Energy conservation means we should save energy."
**Correction:** In physics, "conservation" means the total amount stays constant — it is a law of nature, not a suggestion. The everyday phrase "conserve energy" means use less electrical/fuel energy, which is about efficiency, not the physics law.

**Misconception 5:** "A ball bouncing forever would violate conservation of energy."
**Correction:** Actually, a ball bouncing forever would REQUIRE energy to appear from nowhere (to replace what is lost each bounce to heat and sound). Real balls always lose energy each bounce and eventually stop. That is because of conservation of energy: each bounce converts some mechanical energy to heat/sound, and you cannot get it back.

## Summary

- The **Law of Conservation of Energy** states that energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transformed between forms. Total energy is always constant.

- **Total mechanical energy** (E = KE + PE) is conserved when there is no friction, air resistance, or other energy-draining forces.

- The key equation for conservation of mechanical energy is:
  - KE₁ + PE₁ = KE₂ + PE₂  (frictionless)
  - ½mv₁² + mgh₁ = ½mv₂² + mgh₂

- In frictionless problems, **mass often cancels out**, meaning speed depends only on height (for gravitational PE problems).

- **Friction converts mechanical energy into thermal energy (heat)**. This means mechanical energy is NOT conserved when friction is present, even though total energy still is.

- Other forms of energy that can absorb mechanical energy include: sound, deformation energy, and electrical energy.

- **Energy bar diagrams** are a useful tool: draw a bar for KE and PE at different positions. The total height should stay the same (if mechanical energy is conserved) or decrease as some energy becomes heat.

- In a **pendulum**: all PE at the ends, all KE at the bottom
- In a **roller coaster**: maximum speed at lowest points, minimum at highest points
- In a **bouncing ball**: energy is lost each bounce to heat and sound; the ball never quite reaches its original height

## Key Equations

```
TOTAL MECHANICAL ENERGY
  E = KE + PE
  E = ½mv² + mgh

CONSERVATION OF MECHANICAL ENERGY (no friction)
  KE₁ + PE₁ = KE₂ + PE₂
  ½mv₁² + mgh₁ = ½mv₂² + mgh₂

SIMPLIFIED (when starting from rest at height h, falling to h=0):
  mgh = ½mv²
  gh  = ½v²           (mass cancels)
  v   = √(2gh)        (speed at bottom)
  h   = v² / (2g)     (height for given launch speed)

WITH FRICTION
  KE₁ + PE₁ = KE₂ + PE₂ + W_friction
  W_friction = energy lost to heat/friction

ENERGY PERCENTAGE LOST
  % lost = (E_lost / E_initial) × 100
  % lost = [(E_initial - E_final) / E_initial] × 100

USEFUL NUMBERS
  g = 9.8 m/s²  (or 10 m/s² for estimates)
  1 Joule = 1 kg·m²/s²
```
